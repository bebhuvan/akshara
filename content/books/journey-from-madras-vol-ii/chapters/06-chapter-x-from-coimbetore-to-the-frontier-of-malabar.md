Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">ON the 1st of <em>November</em> I went ten <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Kanya-uru</em>, which is a small village without any shops, and is situated at some distance north from the <em>Noyel</em> river. The country near <em>Coimbatore</em> is fully cultivated, but very bare of trees. A few very fine hedges show how well they would thrive, if all the fields were inclosed. Towards <em>Kanya-uru</em> large proportions of the fields are unoccupied, but the country is better wooded. Much of the soil is poor, and all at any distance from the <em>Noyel</em> is dry-field.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 2.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Hedges of the <em>Euphorbium antiquorum.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">2d <em>November</em>.—I went ten <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Avanasi</em>, the residence of a <em>Tahsildar</em>. The country looks well; about one half of the arable lands being under cultivation, and many of the fields being surrounded by good hedges, especially those of the <em>Elanda Moula</em>, or <em>Euphorbium antiquorum</em>. This kind of hedge requires to be annually repaired, by inserting cuttings in the places where old plants have decayed; but large cuttings being taken, and supported by <em>Bamboos</em> and thorns, they become immediately a fence sufficient against cattle.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rude cultivation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The principal cultivation here is <em>Horse-gram</em> (<em>Dolichos biflorus</em>), with which very little trouble is taken. The ploughing is so rude, that hardly any of the bushes are overturned; and the field at a little distance appears as if it were waste. Many bushes resist even the repeated ploughings given to the fields of <em>Cambu</em>, but they are soon overtopped by this vigorous plant.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Face of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 2.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the vicinity of <em>Avanasi</em> are many <em>Palmira</em> groves, which in a country so naked give it a good appearance. Here there are two <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-292" data-page="292" data-folio="276" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n291_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 276">[p. 276]</button> reservoirs for watering rice-ground. The one receives all its supply of water from the rain which it collects. The ground irrigated by this tank amounted to eleven <em>Candacas</em>, equal to twenty-two <em>Cheis</em>, or twenty-nine acres; but, owing to its being out of repair, it now supplies only ten <em>Cheis</em>. The other reservoir receives a supply of water from a rivulet called the <em>Semudir</em>, which, after giving a supply to another large reservoir, falls into the <em>Noyel</em> at <em>Tripura</em>. The dam turning the water from the <em>Semudir</em> into the reservoir at <em>Avanasi</em>, is in such bad repair, that the supply is deficient, and thirty-two <em>Cheis</em> only are at present cultivated, of the eighty which formerly were irrigated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Avanasi.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Before the invasion of General Meadows, <em>Avanasi</em> contained two hundred houses, which are now reduced to about fifty, that are chiefly inhabited by the <em>Bráhmans</em>, musicians, and dancers belonging to a temple of <em>Siva</em>. These people pretend, that their temple is equal in sanctity to the celebrated <em>Baranasi</em> at <em>Kási</em>; but this pretension is laughed at by their neighbours. In this district there are many weavers, <em>Coicular</em>, <em>Jadar</em>, and <em>Parriar</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sheep and goats.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Many sheep are bred throughout <em>Coimbetore</em>, and especially in this district. Under the term <em>Bacri</em>, the Mussulmans here include both the long-legged goat and the sheep. The former, in the native language of this country, is called <em>Veladu</em>; of the sheep there are in this place two kinds, the one called <em>Curumbar</em>, and the other <em>Shaymbliar</em>. The goats here are greatly inferior to those above the Ghats; but the sheep, though small, are of a good quality, fattening readily, and making most delicious meat. Even grass-mutton may be had here tolerably fat; for the pasture, although it looks very bare, seems to be more nutritious than that on the banks of the Ganges, where no tolerable mutton can be reared without the assistance of grain. Even the Mussulman officers never thought of fattening their sheep with grain, and indeed made very little difference between fat and lean mutton. class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 2. <em>Curumbar</em> sheep.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Curumbar</em> (Plate XVII, Fig. 42, 43.) is a short bodied sheep, with a short small tail, like that of a hare, or goat: the rams have short horns turned back, and their ears are very short and pendulous. The ewes seldom have horns. The wool is thick and curly, and has little or no hair intermixed with it. Here they are in general white, with black heads; but above the <em>Ghats</em> they are frequently altogether black. It is of the wool of this kind only that blankets are made. They are shorn twice a year.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Shaymbliar</em> sheep.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Shaymbliar</em> (Plate XVIII, Fig. 44, 45.) is of a thinner make than the <em>Curumbar</em>. Their horns and tails are similar; but their ears are longer, and their wool is very scanty, their principal covering being hair. In this country they are generally of a redish brown colour; but in <em>Mysore</em> they also are most commonly black.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Management of sheep.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Both kinds lamb once a year from the 15th October to the 15th of November. Twice a day, during the two following months, about ¼ of a <em>Seer</em>, or about seventeen cubical inches of milk, are taken from each. The long legged goat gives double that quantity for three months after each kid, and breeds twice a year. The milk of all the three, together with that of cows and buffaloes, is mixed for making butter. My Bengal servants acknowledge, that both the <em>Ghee</em> (boiled butter) and curdled milk of this country are superior to those of their own, where a preference is given to unmixed cow's milk. Wethers of all the three kinds are made by bruising the testicles of the animals when they are two years old, and never while they are young: the natives prefer the meat of the goat to that of the sheep, and the meat of the <em>Shaymbliar</em> to that of the <em>Curumbar</em>; which is directly in opposition to the taste of most Europeans. Owing to this, however, the cultivators in general keep only the goats and <em>Shaymbliars</em>; while the <em>Curubas</em>, or weavers of blankets, keep the <em>Curumbars</em>, as these only can supply them with wool.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-298"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="298" data-folio="278" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n297_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 278">[p. 278]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 2.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Curubas</em>, or shepherds.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <em>Coimbetore</em> no kind of cattle are housed at any season. Previous to the ploughing season, they are always folded on the lands that are to be cultivated. In order to increase the quantity of manure, the farmers every where keep sheep and goats; but it is chiefly in this neighbourhood, that the <em>Curubas</em> pasture their flocks of <em>Curumbars</em>. The <em>Curubas</em>, who by the Mussulmans are called <em>Donigars</em>, are all of <em>Karnáta</em> extraction, and in <em>Coimbetore</em> never cultivate the ground. Their sole occupation is feeding their flocks, and weaving their wool into coarse blankets; none of which made here, exceed in value four <em>Vir’-Ráya Fanams</em>, or 1<em>s.</em> 11¾<em>d.</em> Each man possesses from fifty to one hundred sheep, which he pastures on the fields all day without paying any rent; and at night he folds them on the arable lands of the cultivators, who might each give a <em>Bulla</em> of grain to the proprietor of one hundred sheep for the manure. Every family of the <em>Curubas</em> pays a poll-tax, and there is a duty on their blankets.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 3. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">3d <em>November.</em>—I went five <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Tripura</em>, fording the <em>Noyel</em> at that town. The country is not so well occupied as that through which I came yesterday; and in every village there are many ruinous houses. The soil is rather poor, but the fields are well fenced. The <em>Noyel</em> is a river very inferior to the <em>Bhawání</em>, and was easily fordable, although much swollen by a very heavy rain that lasted all night. On crossing this river, I entered the district under Mr. Hurdis. <em>Tripura</em> is an open town, containing three hundred houses, with a large weekly market or fair. I observed, that the women here did not conceal themselves when their curiosity prompted them to view me as a stranger. This is also the case in all the country above the <em>Ghats</em>; but in the part of <em>Coimbetore</em> north from the <em>Noyel</em> river, the women in general ran out of my way, and satisfied their curiosity by peeping from behind walls and hedges, as is usual in the country of Bengal.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Tripura</em>, or <em>Palar</em> district.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 3.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Tahsildar</em> of this district resides at a place called <em>Palar</em>, where there is a fort, but only thirty houses, of which fifteen are <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-299" data-page="299" data-folio="279" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n298_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 279">[p. 279]</button> inhabited by <em>Bráhmans</em>. The district is fifteen <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey from north to south, and twelve from east to west. The <em>Tahsildar</em> met me with great readiness, to give me an account of his district. He says, that none of it is absolutely waste; as the fields that are not cultivated pay a trifle as a rent for grass. The country suffered little during the invasion of General Meadows, as it lay at some distance from the routes of the contending armies. Last year many of their cattle died of the epidemic distemper.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Land-measure.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The land-measure differs every where in the province under Mr. Hurdis; and all the revenue accompts are kept according to an old measurement made by <em>Chica Déva Ráya</em>. In this district no less than three different land-measures prevail. 1st, at <em>Palar</em>, thirty-two <em>Vaums</em> or fathoms make one <em>Russy</em>, <em>Caur</em>, or chain; which is, therefore, two hundred and sixteen feet. Three chains by two make a <em>Bulla-sowing</em>, which is 6 <sup>426</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> acres. 2dly, at <em>Madupura hobly</em>, the <em>Bulla</em> is a square of sixty-four <em>Vaums</em> each side, or contains 4,284 acres. 3dly, at <em>Tripura</em>, forty-eight <em>Vaums</em> square make a <em>Bulla</em>-<em>land</em>, equal to 2,41 acres.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Mau</em>, or <em>Candaca</em> of watered land, is equal to two <em>Cheis</em> of the new measurement, or contains 2,644 acres.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dry-measure.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The measures of grain also vary extremely. The <em>Puddy</em> varies from 64 to 72 <em>Rupees</em> weight of grain, or from 56 <sup>83</sup>/<sub>100</sub> to 63 <sup>96</sup>/<sub>100</sub> cubical inches: four <em>Puddies</em> make one <em>Bulla</em>, sixteen <em>Bullas</em> make one <em>Morau</em>, six <em>Moraus</em> make one <em>Podi</em>, which therefore varies from about 10 <sup>15</sup>/<sub>100</sub> to 11 <sup>42</sup>/<sub>100</sub> bushels.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Weights.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The weights near this are every where the same. 8 <em>Rupees</em>=1 <em>Pull</em>, 3 <em>Pulls</em>=1 <em>Seer</em>=0,6067 lb.; also 33 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>3</sub> <em>Seers</em>, or 100 <em>Pulls</em>, are =1 <em>Tolam</em>=20 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub> lb.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Money.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 3. Rice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Here is established a <em>Niruc</em>, or regulation, by which all coins have a certain value affixed to them; and at this rate they are received in the payment of the revenue; but in dealings between private persons attention is not paid to this rule. Accompts are commonly kept in <em>Chucris</em>, or <em>Canter'-Ráya Pagodas</em>, and <em>Fanams</em>; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-300" data-page="300" data-folio="280" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n299_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 280">[p. 280]</button> but the coins commonly current are <em>Pondichery</em> and <em>Sultany Rupees</em>, and <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanams</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On the rice land in this neighbourhood there is only one crop, which is sown after the sprouted manner, from between the 12th of July and the 18th of August. The land is watered partly from reservoirs, and partly from canals, which are brought from the <em>Noyel</em> by dams. It lets for from 9 <em>Chucris</em> to 4½ for the <em>Candaca</em>, or for from 1<em>l.</em> 1<em>s.</em> 3<em>d.</em> to 10<em>s.</em> 7½<em>d.</em> an acre. The dams on the <em>Noyel</em> are said to be 32 in number. Of these four were in this district; but two of them have been so long ruinous, that no accounts remain of the quantity of land to which they gave water. Owing to the want of repairs, rather more than a third of the land formerly watered by the two remaining dams, is now uncultivated. The water from some of the dams on the <em>Noyel</em> is applied directly to the fields from the canals; in others, it is previously collected in reservoirs, in order that no more ground may be cultivated than the supply of water is adequate to irrigate.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dry-field.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">For six years past there has been a great scarcity of rain, which has injured considerably the cultivation of the dry-field. About ⅜ of what was formerly cultivated is now neglected; and for pasture it has always been customary to leave some of the fields fallow. The whole, however, are now let; but the rent given for those which are in grass is very trifling. The greatest article of cultivation here is <em>Colu</em>, or the <em>Dolichos biflorus</em>, called <em>Horse-gram</em> by the English of Madras; next to that, about equal quantities of <em>Cambu</em> (<em>Holcus spicatus</em>), mixed with <em>Bullar</em> (<em>Dolichos Lablab</em>), and of <em>Sholum</em> (<em>Holcus sorghum</em>); next to those, <em>Upum</em> cotton. The other articles cultivated on dry-field are inconsiderable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The produce of a <em>Bulla</em> land, <em>Palar</em> measurement, is stated to be 200 <em>Tolas</em> of cotton, with the seed, or about 629 pounds an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Cambu</em> seed per <em>Bulla</em>, 52 <em>Bullas</em> produce 20 <em>Podis</em>. <em>Bullar</em>  -  -  -  -   16  -  -  -  -   2¾</p></blockquote></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-301"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="301" data-folio="281" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n300_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 281">[p. 281]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. This is at the rate of rather less than six and a half acres for a plough.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gardens watered by the <em>Capily.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The quantity of ground cultivated as garden, and watered by the <em>Capily</em>, is in this district very considerable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Palar</em> and <em>Chinghery</em> subdivisions 180 <em>Bullas</em>, or 1156 acres. In <em>Madupuru</em> - - - - - - - - 187 ditto, or 801 ditto. In <em>Tripura</em> - - - - - - - - - - 159 ditto, or 383 ditto. 2340 acres.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It produces <em>Sholum</em> (<em>Holcus sorghum</em>), <em>Cambu</em> (<em>Holcus spicatus</em>), <em>Kevir</em> (<em>Cynosurus corocanus</em>), <em>Meti</em>, or fenugreek (<em>Trigonella fænum græcum</em>), wheat of the <em>Hotay</em> kind (<em>Triticum spelta</em>), <em>Jiray</em> and <em>Danya</em>, two of the carminative seeds, tobacco, garlic, onions, <em>Tenay</em> (<em>Panicum italicum</em>), <em>Banguns</em> (<em>Solanum melongena</em>), and capsicum. Almost every farmer cultivates some of this ground.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pasture.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The whole land in this district is said to be arable ; but certain of the poorest fields are set aside for pasture, and pay a small rent. Some of them continue always in grass ; others are alternately cultivated for <em>Horse-gram</em> (<em>Dolichos biflorus</em>), and produce grass. Four <em>Bullas</em> of <em>Palar</em> measurement (25 <sup>3</sup>⁄<sub>4</sub> acres) are reckoned sufficient pasture for 20 oxen. In the dry season, they must be either sent <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-302" data-page="302" data-folio="282" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n301_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 282">[p. 282]</button> to the hilly country, or fed with the straw of <em>Sholum</em>, or <em>Cambu</em>, the two species of <em>Holcus</em> cultivated in this country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 3. Rent.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A man who has four ploughs, four or five servants, with occasional labourers, and sixteen oxen, is said to cultivate ½ + ⅛ <em>Bulla</em> (4,163/1000 acres) of garden, and 3¾ <em>Bullas</em> (24,1/10 acres) of dry-field, and has 1½ <em>Bulla</em> (9,64/100 acres) of pasture: in all, 37,9 acres: for this he pays 1220 <em>Sultany Fanams</em> a year, which would be at the rate of 1<em>l.</em> an acre for the average rent of the whole district. Another man is said to have ¾ <em>Bulla</em> (4,82/100 acres) of garden, 1 <em>Bulla</em> (6,426/1000 acres) of dry-field, and ½ + ⅛ <em>Bulla</em> (4,163/1000 acres) of pasture; for which he pays 850 <em>Fanams</em> a year, which is at the rate of 1<em>l.</em> 14<em>s.</em> 5<em>d.</em> an acre. Both these statements were given me by the <em>Tahsildar</em>, with much seeming accuracy, from the public accompts; but they appear to me perfectly absurd. He was entirely a man of paper, and came prepared to show long statistical accompts, on which, it seemed to me, no reliance could be placed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Want of curiosity in the natives.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I did not wonder at the <em>Tahsildar</em> being ignorant of the neighbouring country, as he was not a native of the place; but in the whole town he could not find a person that could inform me of the place where the iron sold in their weekly markets was made: all agreed, that it came from the neighbouring district, called <em>China Mali</em>; but every one differed concerning the village.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 4. Face of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 4.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">4th <em>November</em>.—I went ten <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Tallawai Pallyam</em>, as being the most likely place to find the iron forges; but in this I was disappointed, no iron having been ever made there. Some parts of the country through which I passed were well cultivated, while others were quite waste. Although the soil is in general poor; yet traces remain to show that the whole has once been cultivated; and there are many excellent fences even in places where the fields are waste. The quantity of rice ground is very small, and I saw none of it cultivated, although I passed under the bank of a large reservoir, containing much water. I passed another large reservoir, with a stream of water running through it; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-303" data-page="303" data-folio="283" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n302_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 283">[p. 283]</button> but its bank was broken. The canals from the <em>Noyel</em> and its branches are very small, and would be employed to most advantage in filling reservoirs. As I approached <em>Tallawai Pallyam</em>, I saw some small conical hills scattered through the country, which derives its name from that circumstance, <em>China Mali</em> signifying <em>little hills</em>. <em>Tallawai Pallyam</em> is a poor village without a shop, and contains only twenty houses. The cultivators say, that for five years past there has not once been enough of rain to fill their tank, and this has been the case with a great part of the province. The produce of dry grains during the same space of time has not been more than one half of the usual quantity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Languages.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The names of plants in <em>Coimbetore</em> are quite different from those given to the same at <em>Tritchenopoly</em>, although in both countries the language of the <em>Tamuls</em> is spoken. The <em>Tamul</em> of <em>Coimbetore</em> is perfectly intelligible to my Madras servants, although natives of a different <em>Désa</em>. Among the Mussulmans it is called the <em>Arabi</em> language, and their own northern dialect is here called the <em>Asmani</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 5. Iron forges.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 5.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">5th <em>November</em>.—I went a short stage to <em>China Mali</em>, and by the way examined a forge for smelting iron, at a village named <em>Cottumbally</em>. It is wrought by the low people called <em>Siclars</em>; and the plan is nearly the same with that of the forges above the <em>Ghats</em>; but it is in every respect more miserable. The furnaces are built in the open air; so that in the rainy season they cannot be used; and the bellows, being made of a goat’s skin, give very little wind. The man who works it sits on a stone, and, holding the bag between his legs, presses down the end with his right arm, and raises it with the same. The bag at each time is not half emptied, and in fact a pair of common kitchen bellows would give as much wind. The furnace has a lateral slit, close to the ground, for letting out the vitrified matter. The iron is taken out in front. The furnace is first filled with charcoal, then a small cupful of black sand is put on the top. As it burns down a scoopful of charcoal and another cupful of sand are added; and this is continued from <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-304" data-page="304" data-folio="284" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n303_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 284">[p. 284]</button> early in the morning until three or four in the afternoon, when a mass of iron is formed and removed ; and this is the whole day's work. The cup contains about half a pint, and the scoop about three quarts ; so that the expenditure of fewel is immense. The mass of iron is very imperfectly fused. The sand is found in the channels of little torrents, which wash it down from the hills in the rainy season. Much of it, I am told, comes from a village called <em>Vir' Sholavarum</em>, in <em>Canghium</em> district, which is on the south side of the <em>Noyel</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Some people of the <em>Shanar</em> tribe, who make iron near <em>China Mali</em>, tell me, that when they take the mass of iron from the furnace, they immediately cut it in two with a strong <em>Kudali</em>, or hatchet. In this state it is sold to the blacksmiths, who by repeated heatings and beatings reduce each portion to a small bar. Four <em>Shanar</em> work at each furnace, every one performing a part at each stage of the business. In the rainy season they collect the sand. Then they make the charcoal ; and finally, in an interval of about three months between the crop seasons of the <em>Palmira</em> and coco-nut palms (<em>Borassus flabelliformis</em> and <em>Cocos nucifera</em>), they smelt the iron. They pay a thirtieth part of the iron smelted to the government, besides a duty for permission to cut timber for fewel.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At almost every village in the <em>Perinduru</em> district, iron is also smelted from black sand.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Strata</em> near the <em>Noyelar</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Throughout the country watered by the <em>Noyelar</em>, the <em>strata</em> are vertical, and composed in general of aggregate stones in a slaty form. The <em>strata</em> run nearly east and west ; and in many places, especially near rivers or torrents, have been over-flowed by the <em>Tufa calcaria</em>, already frequently mentioned. The sporadic concretions usually found above the <em>Ghats</em>, and the great diffused masses found in <em>Coimbetore</em>, seem to consist exactly of the same materials. The whole calcarious matter, however, in <em>Coimbetore</em> is by no means in large beds ; many sporadic concretions are every where to be found.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-305"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="305" data-folio="285" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n304_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 285">[p. 285]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 5. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The country through which I passed to-day, except where occupied by the small conical hills, is nearly in the same state with that described yesterday. Although the people complain of a want of rain, I passed a large reservoir full of water, which is not applied to irrigate the fields.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Hedges of <em>Mulu-kilivay.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Many of the hedges here, and in other parts of <em>Coimbetore</em>, are made of a thorn called <em>Mulu-kilivay</em>. It seems, from its habit, to be a <em>Rhus</em>; but, not having found the fructification, I am very uncertain concerning its place in the botanical system. It makes a very good fence: cuttings, three or four cubits long, are put in the ground between the 12th of March and the 10th of April. The ends are buried in the earth about a span, and very soon shoot out roots. From the moment it is planted, it forms a fence against cattle; but seems to require a better soil than either the <em>Euphorbium Tirucalli</em>, or the <em>Euphorbium antiquorum</em>, which are the most common hedges here, and will grow any where.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Low state of the arts.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The people of <em>China Mali</em> are either unwilling to give me any information, or are in a beastly state of ignorance. In the whole town I could not procure means to weigh a piece of iron half the produce of one smelting. The inhabitants of this province, indeed, appear to be as far behind those of <em>Mysore</em> in intelligence, and in most of the arts, as these again are behind the natives of Calcutta or Madras. As is the case in every part of Bengal where arts have not been introduced by foreigners, the only one that has been carried to tolerable perfection is that of weaving.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Population.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the reign of <em>Hyder</em>, <em>China Mali</em> contained above 200 houses. These are now reduced to 125, of which 17 belong to <em>Bráhmans</em>, who keep 18 houses of dancing-girls and musicians, leaving 90 houses for those who are supported by honest industry. Of these, 41 are inhabited by weavers, 5 by shop-keepers, and 7 by cultivators.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Small-pox.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 5.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The small-pox has been lately raging in the town, and is said to have proved fatal to 100 persons; a very terrible mortality in so <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-306" data-page="306" data-folio="286" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n305_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 286">[p. 286]</button> small a place! Inoculation is unknown to the natives; and the mention of it excites their astonishment and abhorrence. They trust for cure to the application of the leaves of the <em>Melia Azadirichta</em>, a tree that is sacred to the goddess <em>Marima</em>, who inflicts this dreadful distemper. The priest (<em>Pujári</em>) at her temple is a <em>Handy</em>, a person of very low cast; yet in these times of affliction he gets presents even from the <em>Bráhmans</em>. The disease having now stopped, a grand sacrifice is to be performed at night, in order to thank the angry deity for having restrained her wrath. In this, however, the <em>Bráhmans</em> do not join. The number of singers, drums, horns, and other powerful sources of noisy discord, which have been assembled for the occasion, leave me no room to hope for sleep.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 6. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">6th <em>November</em>.—I went five <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Perinduru</em>. The soil of the country through which I passed is in general poor, and not much of it cultivated. There are few fences, but a good many gardens of the <em>Palmira</em> tree, or <em>Borassus</em>. The <em>Tahsildar</em> says, that the whole rice-ground in the district is of very little extent. Two canals from the <em>Noyel</em> come through it. The one fills a reservoir, the water from the other is applied directly to the fields; but the extent watered by both means is inconsiderable. In the district of <em>China Mali</em> there is no rice-ground. In this district there is also much land watered by the <em>Capily</em>, and cultivated for what is called here <em>Tarkári</em>. The rent of such land is higher than that of dry-field. The <em>Tahsildar</em> says, that three quarters of the district are now waste, owing to a want of people. To me it appears, that he over-rates the population greatly; but he says, that many of the waste fields are of a very poor soil; and, although they have been once or twice cultivated, they were found not to repay the labour bestowed on them, and have ever since been neglected. I doubt much the accuracy of this statement; for I see fields now cultivated, that are apparently of as bad a soil as those which are waste. By the way, I passed one village totally in ruins. The people say, that since the death of <em>Hyder</em> they have not had one year with a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-307" data-page="307" data-folio="287" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n306_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 287">[p. 287]</button> proper fall of rain. This year there has been abundance, but it came too late by two months.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov 6. <em>Perinduru.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lands granted for the support of worship.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this district there about 800 looms. <em>Perinduru</em>, the chief town, contains at present 118 houses, of which 24 are inhabited by <em>Bráhmans</em>, most of whom are attached to a temple. It has a mud fort, which is not inhabited; and there are many ruins in the town. The temple had formerly lands producing 10,000 <em>Gópály Fanams</em> (139<em>l</em>. 13<em>s</em>. 3<em>d</em>.) a year. It is now allowed 1018 <em>Rupees</em> (103<em>l</em>. 1<em>s</em>. 4½<em>d</em>.) a year to support its establishment. The village gods have small <em>Enams</em>, or lands for which they pay half-rent. There are besides lands, belonging both to Mussulmans and <em>Bráhmans</em>, dedicated to the service of God; and these lands are either free, or pay a very trifling rent. The Mussulmans, on account of their lands, are bound to perform certain ceremonies; but the <em>Bráhmans</em> may do as they please. These free lands (<em>Enams</em>) may be mortgaged by what is called <em>Bhógyam</em>: the money is advanced for a certain term of years, the lender taking the produce of the land for interest; and the property is entirely forfeited, if at the stipulated time the money be not repaid. By this means, as is usual all over India, the lands originally intended for the support of religion are now perverted to quite different purposes.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 7.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">7th <em>November.</em>—I went eight <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Erodu</em>, or, as it is called in our maps, <em>Eroad</em>. The country through which I passed is in a state similar to that between <em>China Mali</em> and <em>Perinduru</em>, and contains no rice lands.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Erodu.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 7.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Erodu</em> has a large mud fort, occupied by a battalion of <em>Sepoys</em>, which, in this part of the country, now procures a ready supply of recruits. <em>Tippoo’s</em> soldiers now begin to enter readily into the Company’s service, the late augmentation of the <em>Sepoys’</em> allowances having had a most excellent effect. In the government of <em>Hyder</em> the suburb contained about 3000 houses. <em>Tippoo’s</em> government had reduced them one third part, and the whole was entirely destroyed during the invasion of General Meadows. It is now rising up again, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-308" data-page="308" data-folio="288" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n307_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 288">[p. 288]</button> and contains about 400 houses. The situation is fine, and healthy; and the place will probably soon attain its former importance, its centrical position rendering it very fit for a military station. The weavers in this district amount to 2050 persons, <em>Coicular</em>, <em>Jadar</em>, and <em>Parriar</em>. These last are said to make the best cloth; but the whole is very coarse.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Irrigation by a fine canal.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The canal, coming by <em>Erodu</em> from the <em>Bhawání</em>, is an excellent work, and waters a narrow space of ground fifteen <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey long, and of various breadths. At this place the canal is carried over a small rivulet by means of an aqueduct. It is said that formerly it extended all the way to <em>Caruru</em>, and was carried over the <em>Noyel</em> river by means of an aqueduct, that must have been a great work. The whole is said to have been made by a <em>Vaylalar</em> farmer, named <em>Caling Ráya</em>, who being a rich man, and of great influence, raised from among the people of his cast a sum sufficient for the purpose. This was more than 400 years ago. His family is extinct, and never seems to have received any reward in lands on account of the grand work that he completed. The lands watered by it at present amount to 1045 <em>Mau</em>, or <em>Candacas</em>, which have been found to measure from 2 to 3 <em>Cheis</em> each; and, taking the medium, the whole will be 3459 acres, of which about 83 only are waste. In this district the waste dry-field amounts to 400 <em>Bullas</em>, or about 1713 acres.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">8th <em>November.</em>—I remained at <em>Erodu</em>, and procured the following statements from the <em>Tahsildar</em>, a very intelligent <em>Bráhman</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A <em>Mau</em> or <em>Candaca</em> of watered land is here so much as will sow 100 <em>Seers</em> of rice in the sprouted-seed cultivation. The <em>Seer</em> is equal to 80 <em>Rupees</em> weight, and therefore the quantity of seed for an acre will be very little less than one bushel. The best land lets at 250 <em>Sultany Fanams</em>, and the worst at 60 for the <em>Mau</em>; which is at the rate of from 2<em>l.</em> 7<em>s.</em> 1½<em>d.</em> to 11<em>s.</em> 4<em>d.</em> an acre. Both sprouted seed and transplanted cultivations are in use, and the former is most prevalent. One kind of rice called <em>Mulaghi</em> requires eight <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-309" data-page="309" data-folio="289" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n308_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 289">[p. 289]</button> months to ripen, and is sown between the 13th of July and the 19th of August. No other crop can follow it in the same year. In a good crop it produces 30 <em>Mau</em> from a <em>Candaca</em> land, or about 30 bushels an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The other kinds admit of two crops in the year; producing in both, when they are good, from 45 to 49 bushels an acre. The first crop is of a kind of rice called <em>Anadanum</em>, which is sown between the 12th of May and the 12th of July, and ripens in five months. It produces about 25 bushels an acre. Three kinds of rice, <em>Sambau</em>, <em>Déva Ráya Sambau</em>, and <em>Shindalay</em>, are sown as a second crop, between the 14th of November and the 10th of January, and ripen in six months. The first in a good crop produces 24 bushels, the two latter about 20 bushels, an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Difference in the produce of sown and transplanted rice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although the supply of water here is equally good and regular with that at <em>Nala Ráyana Pallyam</em>, and the produce here is very much less than at that place, yet we need not thence conclude that the statements given at the two places are erroneous; for the greater fertility of the rice ground at <em>Nala Ráyana Pallyam</em> may arise from the transplanted cultivation having been there adopted; while here the sprouted-seed is still retained, the inhabitants not having been forced by a high rent to exert themselves.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dry-field.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The dry-field here lets for from 40 to 10 <em>Sultany Fanams</em> the <em>Vullam</em>, which is of the same extent as that of <em>Coimbetore</em>. In a good soil it grows up in thirty years, in a bad one it requires fifty.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 9. Irrigation by canals.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Industrious <em>Bráhmans.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">9th <em>November.</em>—I went a very long stage, called ten <em>Malabar</em> hours' journey, to <em>Pashar</em>. The canal from the <em>Bhawání</em> continued near my route on the left, and goes on three <em>Malabar</em> hours' journey farther, to a place called <em>Colanelly</em>. The high ground on my right was in general very poor. Of what is tolerably good a large proportion is cultivated. <em>Pashar</em> is an open village, containing 130 houses, of which 40 are inhabited by <em>Bráhmans</em>. There is, however, only one small temple that has a <em>Bráhman Pújári</em>, or priest. The others have betaken themselves to honest industry, and rent the lands which they formerly held in <em>Enam</em>; that is to say, almost the whole rice-ground belonging to the place. They are said actually to have put their hands to the plough. Great complaints are made here, of a want of rain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rocks of schistose quartz.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I observed near <em>Pashar</em> very large rocks of white quartz, in which it is evidently disposed in plates, like schistus, from one quarter of an inch to one inch in thickness, standing vertically, and running east and west in the direction of the common strata of the country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 10. Face of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 10.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">10th <em>November.</em>—I went eight <em>Malabar</em> hours' journey to <em>Codo-mudi</em>, a town on the bank of the <em>Cavery</em>. The road is interrupted by several torrents, swelled much by the heavy rains. A great part <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-312" data-page="312" data-folio="292" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n311_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 292">[p. 292]</button> of the route led me through a country fully cultivated and inclosed; and, although not so well wooded as England, yet I think, on the whole, the most beautiful that I have seen in India. The <em>Cavery</em>, which at present is a noble river, and many hills scattered through the country, add much to the beauty of the scenery. The soil is however in general poor, and near <em>Codo-mudi</em> many of the fields are waste. <em>Codo-mudi</em> has a temple, said as usual to be of great antiquity, and provided with an establishment of 11 <em>Bráhmans</em>, and 21 musicians and dancing-women. It is a poor building; but, this being a holiday, it was crowded with multitudes of all ages and both sexes, many of whom were prostrated before the images. The houses in <em>Codo-mudi</em> are 118, of which 28 are occupied by <em>Bráhmans</em>. It is a new town, and money has been advanced to assist the people to build houses. <em>Colanelly</em>, which we passed on the way, has been deserted. At this place a canal is taken off from the <em>Cavery</em>, without the assistance of a dam. A canal of this kind is called a <em>Corum</em>. In the dry season this is carried across the channel of the <em>Noyel</em>, and waters the fields near <em>Pogolur</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Enamdars</em>, or persons holding free lands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Bráhmans</em>, who now live here, were formerly all <em>Vaidíkas</em>, or men dedicated to religious meditation; and in <em>Hyder's</em> government lived on the opposite bank of the <em>Cavery</em>, where they had <em>Enams</em>, or free lands. Having lost this property, they have been obliged to rent some lands, which they cultivate by means of their servants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 11. Irrigation. Canals.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">11th <em>November</em>.—I went seven and a half <em>Malabar</em> hours' journey to <em>Pogolur</em>, in the district under the management of Mr. Hurdis. By the way, I visited the place where the <em>Corum</em>, or canal taken from the <em>Cavery</em> at <em>Codo-mudi</em>, is conducted over the river <em>Noyel</em>. In the rainy season, the water taken from the <em>Cavery</em> at A, in the annexed plan, is allowed to fall again into that river by the passage B; for the quantity of water in the <em>Noyelar</em> is then sufficient to supply the canal DE. But in the dry season, when the <em>Noyel</em> is absorbed by the sands of its extensive channel, the water of the <em>Cavery</em> is conducted to D by the canal A. C. D, and is conveyed across the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-313" data-page="313" data-folio="293" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n312_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 293">[p. 293]</button> channel of the <em>Noyel</em> by a temporary dam of earth (D), erected immediately below the course of the canal.</p></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-013"><img src="/figures/journey-from-madras-vol-ii/fig-013_p0313.webp" alt="Diagram of the Kaveri or Cavery River, Noyelar, Codomudi, Pogolur, and canal with points A, B, C, D, E." loading="lazy"></figure><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <em>Pogolur</em> village, this canal supplied with water 200 <em>Canays</em>, or 265 acres of rice-land, besides much in some other places. The whole of the rice-lands are cultivated; and, according to the village accompts, three-fifths of the dry-field in <em>Pogolur</em> are also cultivated. <em>Pogolur</em> is a small village without shops, and contains only about one half of the houses that it did in <em>Hyder's</em> government. Few of the fences near it are good; but there is much good soil, especially near the <em>Noyelar</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Enamdars.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The whole of the rice-lands are occupied by the <em>Bráhmans</em>, to whom they formerly belonged in <em>Enam</em>, or free gift. <em>Tippoo</em> made them pay a moderate rent of four-tenths of the produce. Last year this was converted to money, at the rate of 22 <em>Rupees</em> for the ten <em>Canays</em>, which is about 3<em>s.</em> 5 <sup>3</sup>/<sub>4</sub><em>d.</em> an acre. Their <em>Enams</em> may therefore be considered as still valuable property. The rent for this year has not yet been fixed. One half of these lands produce annually two crops of rice. Four <em>Bráhmans</em> hold the whole, and are called <em>Potails</em>. These let them out to other <em>Bráhmans</em>, who cultivate them by means of servants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 12. Information procured from Major Macleod.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">12th <em>November.</em>—I went to visit Major Macleod, the collector of the northern division of the <em>Coimbetore</em> province; and having passed the day with him at <em>Pramatí</em>, on the east side of the <em>Cavery</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-314" data-page="314" data-folio="294" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n313_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 294">[p. 294]</button> I returned at night to <em>Pogolur</em>. The river here is about six or eight hundred yards wide, with a strong but smooth current. It is shallow; and, even at this season, not above forty yards of it exceed the depth in which a man could walk.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Hindu</em> casts.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Right and left hand sides.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Major Macleod is a gentleman extremely beloved by the natives under his authority, and very conversant in the manners of the <em>Hindus</em>, to whose prejudices he shows every reasonable attention. He thinks, however, that Europeans in general give too much credit to the assertions of the natives concerning the rules of their cast; which are commonly alleged as an excuse for declining any duty that is disagreeable. He does not permit the hereditary chiefs of casts to settle the disputes of their followers by fine or excommunication; and has had no difficulty in making persons be again received into society, who had been made outcasts owing to the pique or caprice of leading men. In cases of complaint against any one for his having infringed the rules of cast, he orders an assembly of the most respectable people of the tribe to meet in the public office before the <em>Tahsildar</em>, who inquires into the business; and, after having consulted the assembly concerning their real customs, decides on the nature of the guilt, and its appropriate punishment. Any person who is troublesome, and refuses to submit to the decision of the <em>Tahsildar</em> and assembly, is immediately banished from the district. He has had no great difficulty in allaying the disputes between the right and left hand sides. He has caused arbitrators from both sides, men of prudence and temper, to meet in the public office, and there to come to an agreement concerning what the custom should be. A copy of this agreement is given to each of the parties, and another to the <em>Tahsildar</em>, who is ordered to enforce it both by fine and corporal punishment. When it has been necessary to divide any town into separate quarters for the two sides, the party insisting on any adversary's removing to his own quarter must build for him a new house. Any man may retire from his adversary's quarter, whenever he pleases.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-315"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="315" data-folio="295" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n314_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 295">[p. 295]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 12. Tenures.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Major Macleod says, that the custom of the country has always been understood to be, that no tenant could be turned out of his possession so long as he paid his rent. Under the former government, however, the officers of revenue removed the tenants as they pleased, and gave the best land to their favourites. This will always be the case, wherever the principal officer of a province is not very alert in redressing injustice, and very accessible to the lower classes of inhabitants; which is rarely the case among the natives of rank. Every village had a register, containing a valuation of its arable lands, which is always said to have been made by some prince, or governor, and called by his name; there having, however, been no other copy than that in the possession of the village accomptant, there was no check upon him and the head-man. These officers therefore were constantly varying, for corrupt purposes, the rates of the different fields; and, if they took care to keep the total amount the same, they might make the assessment on the fields held by themselves and friends quite light, and lay what they ought to pay on their neighbours, or on lands that were not occupied. Major Macleod thinks, therefore, that in justice no attention ought to be paid to these valuations; and accordingly, in the <em>Saliem</em> part of his district, has made a new valuation of the whole. He is also of opinion, that this valuation should only be continued for a specific number of years; at the end of which the government may have an option of increasing the rent, in proportion to the improvement of the country, and to the progressive diminution of the value of the precious metals. This he would do by laying a per-centage upon the whole, which seems to me liable to many objections. He admits, that in the course of a few years the present valuation must become an unequal tax; but he thinks that a new valuation at the end of every lease would be attended with great difficulty, and open a door for numerous abuses. Under the administration of a weak or corrupt collector, it no doubt would do so; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-316" data-page="316" data-folio="296" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n315_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 296">[p. 296]</button> but with such men as the collectors brought up under Colonel Read, I have no doubt of its being attended with the greatest benefit, both to the government and to the tenant.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Division of crops.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Major Macleod thinks it impracticable for the government to avoid the most excessive embezzlement, in receiving rent by a division of the crops. It might be done by a petty <em>Polygar</em>, but not in any large government. When the Company obtained possession of the <em>Saliem</em> country, the rice grounds that are watered by the fine canals from the <em>Cavery</em> were rented by a division of the crops. At that time a great part of these grounds was waste, and the rents were low, and collected with difficulty. The changing them into a fixed revenue, to be paid in money, occasioned murmurs at first; but the whole lands are now cultivated; tenants are eager to procure them, and the revenue is greatly increased. In fact, the stimulus of rent raised with moderation, according to circumstances, is the best source of industry in every country, and hence contributes equally to improve the revenue and the condition of the tenantry.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Zemindars</em>, and renters of districts.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At present, the whole public lands are held immediately of the government, and none are farmed out to collectors, or hereditary <em>Zemindars</em>. The former are always oppressors; and, although the latter give a security and ease in collecting the revenue, there can be little doubt, that hereditary proprietors of large landed estates are a political evil in a country governed by foreigners. The regulations introduced by Colonel Read for collecting the revenue, seem to me sufficient to secure the regular payment of more than can ever be procured from <em>Zemindars</em>; and I am persuaded, that any deficiencies must arise either from a neglect of duty, or from dishonesty in the collectors. I here allude to hereditary <em>Zemindars</em>, merely as affecting the revenue, and political state of the country: they must be considered as useful toward the improvement of agriculture.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-317"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="317" data-folio="297" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n316_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 297">[p. 297]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 12. <em>Enams</em>, or lands rent free.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">There are some small <em>Enams</em>, or private properties in land, but none of great extent. Major Macleod proposes, that the lands formerly belonging to the <em>Bráhmans</em> should be restored to them, at a rent somewhat lower than could be procured by letting them to the best bidder; but their extent, and the rent to be paid for them, should be defined in the usual manner. The <em>Enams</em>, as well as the pensions granted by <em>Hyder</em> and <em>Tippoo</em> to Mussulman establishments, have been continued. The <em>Enams</em> belonging to the <em>Gráma Dévatas</em>, or village gods, have been all measured, and valued on actual inspection by Major Macleod, who has reduced their size where they seemed more than was necessary to support the expense of the usual ceremonies. The lands belonging to the temples of the great gods have been entirely reassumed; and in their stead monthly pay is given to the necessary attendants. On the whole, the quantity of <em>Enam</em>, or land not belonging to the public, is very small; but it is looked upon by Major Macleod as highly injurious. He allows, that it is better cultivated than the land belonging to the public; but this arises from the <em>Enamdars</em> letting the whole of their lands at a very low rent, and thus seducing away the tenants of the government. In the present state of the country, the <em>Enamdars</em> are content to get any rent, rather than allow their lands to be waste; and when the population recovers, they will raise their lands as high as the government does.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Village officers.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Major Macleod alleges, that the chiefs and accomptants of villages have no just right to the hereditary possession of their offices; and says, that it was always by means of bribery and corruption, that the son of a person who had been turned out for mismanagement, was permitted to enjoy his father's office. I admit the utility of Major Macleod's system; but am persuaded, that it is contrary to the customary law of the natives.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Condition of the people.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cultivators and peasantry continue exactly in the same dress, and same houses, that they used in <em>Tippoo's</em> government, and have a prejudice against changes. Major Macleod thinks, that their women <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-318" data-page="318" data-folio="298" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n317_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 298">[p. 298]</button> are beginning to wear more gold and silver in their ornaments than they formerly did. The merchants and manufacturers are evidently improving in their manner of living, are forsaking their pyramidical or conical huts, and are erecting tiled houses. To enable them to do this, government, without charging interest, advances money, which is repaid by instalments.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Stamp duty on cloths.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The manufacturers are now satisfied, that the stamp-tax will be on the whole easier to them, than the different duties on looms, houses, and transit, which it supplants; and, from the ease of collection, it will be more productive to government. The custom-houses which are at present farmed, do not in Major Macleod’s opinion impede trade, and the revenue which they produce is considerable. Fixed rates are pasted up at every custom-house; and a copy is given to the <em>Tahsildar</em>, who is bound to protect every trader from delay or imposition on the part of the farmer.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Determination of civil causes.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">All disputes are settled in open court, by arbitrators mutually chosen; and these are not permitted to retire until they decide the cause, in order to leave no room for corruption and intrigue; against which, among the natives, it is necessary to guard with the utmost vigilance. This seems an admirable plan, and much superior to the commissioners in Bengal. In fact, the <em>Tahsildar</em>, with this assistance, seems fully adequate to manage the collection of the revenue, the police, and the judicial department; but without the active inspection of an intelligent superior, there is great room for abuse.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Coin.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The present state of the coin is a serious grievance, and bears heavy on the poor. Major Macleod thinks, that a uniform coinage, with pieces forming aliquot parts of each other, would be so willingly received by the inhabitants, that, without a murmur, they would, for new money, pay into the collector’s treasury all their old coin, at such a discount as would defray the expence of the mint. The only difficulty in the whole measure would be, to procure a sufficient quantity of new coin.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-319"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="319" data-folio="299" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n318_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 299">[p. 299]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 12. <em>Capily</em> gardens, or <em>Bagait</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Bagait</em>, or gardens watered by the machines called <em>Capily</em> and <em>Yatam</em>, are of great importance. This manner of cultivation enables a small extent of ground to support many people, and to pay a high rent; and it is less liable to fail, from a want of rain, than the common cultivation of the dry-fields. Major Macleod therefore advances money to every farmer who engages to dig a well. This advance is repaid in between eighteen months and two years. For the first year a garden pays only the rent which it did while cultivated as dry-field; in the second year, one half of the additional rent is laid on; and in the third year it pays the full rent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Machinery for irrigation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Where the water is near the surface, Major Macleod prefers the <em>Yatam</em>, as the cheapest manner of irrigating a garden; but where the water is far from the surface, he prefers the <em>Capily</em>. He has not however ascertained, by actual experiment, the relative advantages of these two machines.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 13. Appearance of the country, and strata.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">13th <em>November</em>.—I went ten <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Caruru</em>, or <em>Caroor</em>. A considerable proportion of the country is not cultivated, and there are very few fences. The soil is in general poor, with many projecting rocks, especially of pure white quartz, among which are found irregular masses perfectly pellucid. There is a quarry near <em>Caroor</em>, of a stone called <em>Carum-gull</em>, or the black stone. It differs from the hornblende of <em>Mysore</em>, being mixed with felspar; but is used for the same purposes, and is called by the same name.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Caroor</em>, or <em>Caruru</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Caruru</em> is a considerable town, situated on the northern bank of the <em>Amara-wati</em> river, and having at a little distance from it a neat fort, containing a large temple, and a garrison of <em>Sepoys</em>. The town contains 1000 houses. Its merchants seem, however, to be chiefly petty dealers, nor are the weavers in the place numerous.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Different denomination of land.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 13.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Lands now waste, but formerly cultivated, in this part of the country, are in the language of the <em>Tamuls</em> called <em>Tirsi</em>; by the Mussulmans they are called <em>Banjur</em>. The lands in cultivation are called <em>Sagwulli</em>. Lands not watered are called <em>Kiet</em>; and those which <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-320" data-page="320" data-folio="300" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n319_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 300">[p. 300]</button> are watered are called <em>Danwudi</em>. In this district almost the whole of the latter are cultivated, and belong entirely to the <em>Bráhmans</em>. Last year one half of the dry-field was waste; the quantity that will be occupied this year is not yet ascertained. The proportion occupied by rivers, roads, rocks, woods, &amp;c. in the opinion of the <em>Tahsildar</em>, does not exceed one tenth part of the whole.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Irrigated land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this district there are below <em>Pogolur</em> two canals (<em>Corums</em>) from the <em>Cavery</em>, that water much rice-land, and are full throughout the year. Several canals for watering the ground are also brought from the <em>Amara-wati</em>, both by means of dams (<em>Anacuts</em>), and by simple canals, or <em>Corums</em>. The supply of water in this river does not always last the whole year; so that, in some seasons, there is only one crop of rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this district a great deal of sugar-cane is raised. It is cultivated nearly in the same manner as at <em>Bala-pura</em>, and ripens in ten months. A crop of <em>Ratoons</em> is sometimes taken, but it is very poor. Between every two crops of sugar-cane it is customary to take two or three crops of rice. Two thousand holes are formed in every <em>Canay</em> of ground, which is equal to 100 <em>Culies</em> of 32 <em>Adies</em> square. Three cuttings are put in each hole. In a good crop, a <em>Canay</em> of land produces of <em>Jagory</em> 120 <em>Tolams</em> of 27½ <em>Seers</em> of 28 <em>Rupees</em>. This is at the rate of only 8¾ cwt. from an acre. When cheap, the <em>Jagory</em> sells at half a <em>Rupee</em> a <em>Tolam</em>, or 6<em>s</em>. 4½<em>d</em>. a hundred-weight. The whole value of the produce of an acre, at this rate, is 2<em>l</em>. 16<em>s</em>.; but the <em>Jagory</em> often sells at double the price here stated. A Mr. Campbell has lately undertaken to make the <em>Jagory</em> into sugar, and has received from the Company considerable encouragement. He advances 20 <em>Rupees</em> for every <em>Canay</em> of land which the farmers plant, and is to receive one half of the <em>Jagory</em>. Out of this half he is to pay the rent to the government. The twenty <em>Rupees</em> are to be repaid him out of the farmer’s half. The farmer’s share is therefore one half of the produce, and he receives money in advance to enable him to cultivate the land.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-321"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="321" data-folio="301" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n320_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 301">[p. 301]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 14. <em>Amara-wati.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">14th <em>November.</em>—I went seven and a half <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Cutamboor</em>, a small village without a shop. The river <em>Amara-wati</em> is at least 400 yards wide; but its stream is very gentle, and almost always fordable. To-day it was about two feet deep. The channel is entirely of sand, and the banks are very low; so that, for watering the rice-grounds, canals (<em>Corums</em>) are easily taken from it.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Near the river the rice-grounds are extensive, and fully cultivated. Farther on, the soil becomes poor, and has many large projecting rocks; but they do not rise high above the surface. There are few inclosures, and much of the dry-field is waste. The country south from the river <em>Noyel</em> is remarkably bare of trees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 15.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">15th <em>November.</em>—I went seven and a half <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Arava-courchy</em>. The road passes through a pretty country; but the soil is poor, and there are very few inclosures. I saw very little cultivation; but the <em>Tahsildar</em> insists that two-thirds of the whole of his district are cultivated, and the remainder pays a small rent for grass. To judge from what I have seen of the country, I should conclude that not more than a quarter of the dry-field is cultivated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dry-field.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The articles of any importance that are cultivated here on this kind of ground are about equal quantities of <em>Sholum</em> (<em>Holcus sorghum</em>), and <em>Cambu</em> (<em>Holcus spicatus</em>), with some accompanying legumes; a smaller quantity of <em>Colu</em>, or Horse-gram (<em>Dolichos biflorus</em>), and a small quantity of <em>Shamay</em> (<em>Panicum miliare</em> E. It is clear, however, that <em>Bharata-khanda</em> contains all the habitable world, as far as was known to the authors of the books esteemed sacred among the <em>Hindus</em>, and is by no means applied to signify the country which we call <em>Hindustan</em>. Indeed, I have never been able to discover any name that the <em>Bráhmans</em> have for the country over which their doctrine has extended. They always describe it by a circumlocution, and say all the country between <em>Himavat-giri</em> and <em>Raméswara</em>. The <em>Bráhmans</em> speak of nine <em>Khandas</em> in this <em>Jambu Dwipa</em>, or world inhabited by men; but all that is said concerning them, <em>Bharata-khanda</em> excepted, seems to be the silly extravagance of a disordered imagination.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Bhágírathi, or Ganges.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 15.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Bharata-khanda</em> is surrounded by a sea of salt water, and its most celebrated river is the <em>Bhágírathi</em>, called by way of eminence the <em>Gangá</em>, or river. It is only that part of the river which lies in a line from <em>Gangóttara</em> to <em>Ságara</em> that is holy; and that is named the <em>Gangá</em>, or <em>Bhágírathi</em>. The <em>Hoogley</em> river of European geographers, therefore, is considered as the true <em>Ganges</em>; and the great branch that runs east to join the <em>Mégna</em>, or <em>Bráhma-putra</em>, is by the <em>Hindus</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-327" data-page="327" data-folio="307" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n326_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 307">[p. 307]</button> called <em>Padma</em> (vulgo <em>Pada</em>) or <em>Padmawatí</em>, and is not by them esteemed equally sacred. Although the water of the whole river from <em>Gangóttara</em> to <em>Ságara</em> is holy, yet there are five <em>Tírthas</em>, or places more eminently sacred than the rest; and to these, of course, all pilgrims from a distance resort to perform their ablutions, and to take up the water that is used in their ceremonies. These <em>Tírthas</em> are, <em>Gangóttara</em>; <em>Haridwára</em>, or <em>Maya</em>; <em>Prayága</em> (called by the Mussulmans <em>Elahabad</em>), <em>Uttara Janagiri</em>, a little below <em>Monghir</em>; and <em>Ságar</em>, at the mouth of what we call the <em>Hoogley</em> river. <em>Náráyana Shastri</em>, who has been at all these places, says, that at <em>Gângóttara</em> three small streams fall down from impassable snowy precipices, and unite into a small bason below, which is considered by the <em>Hindus</em> as the source of the <em>Ganges</em>, over which at that place a man can step. It is situated about twenty days journey north and west from <em>Haridwára</em> (<em>Hurdwar</em>); and the <em>Bráhman's</em> road lay on the west side of the river, until he came near <em>Gangóttara</em>. He observed no considerable stream joining the <em>Bhágírathi</em> from the east, until he came to the <em>Alikanandra</em>. <em>Prayága</em>, however, is the most celebrated <em>Tírtha</em>, or holy place by water; as <em>Kási</em> is the most sacred <em>Kshétra</em>, or place of worship by land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mussulman cultivators.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the district of <em>Arava-courchy</em> are some families of Mussulman farmers. They were formerly <em>Candashara</em>, or persons holding lands free of rent on condition of serving as private soldiers. After the invasion by Colonel Laing, <em>Tippoo</em> abolished this kind of militia; and the persons who composed it continue to occupy the lands, but pay rent like other farmers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 16. Face of the country,</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 16.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">16th <em>November</em>.—I went ten <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Mulinuru</em>. The country is better enclosed, and less rocky, than that through which I came yesterday; but it is equally uncultivated. By the way I passed an iron forge, of the same structure with that seen in Major Macleod’s district, and, like it, calculated to smelt black sand. At <em>Arava-courchy</em> I had been informed, that at <em>Mulinuru</em> I should find a market; but on coming up I found, that the whole <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-328" data-page="328" data-folio="308" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n327_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 308">[p. 308]</button> place had been destroyed by an invading army, probably that under Colonel Fullarton, and that it has never since been rebuilt. All that remains is a small temple, which has got an establishment of <em>Bráhmans</em>, dancing women, and musicians. The neighbouring country is adorned with many plantations of the <em>Borassus</em>. The calcareous <em>Tufa</em> abounds at least as much on the south side of the <em>Noyel</em> as it does toward the north, and in some places covers the whole surface of the ground in continued masses. West from <em>Mulinuru</em> is a field of this kind, where the calcareous masses assume a botryoidal form.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Weather.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">For some days the weather has become comparatively pleasant. It is very clear, and, although hot in the day and evening, is then by no means oppressive; while the mornings are delightful.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 17. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">17th <em>November</em>.—I went a long stage to <em>Daraporam</em>. Near this are two fine canals, that water much rice-land in a good state of cultivation. The soil of the dry-field is poor, and but little of it is cultivated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Daraporam.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Daraporam</em>, or more properly <em>Dharma-puram</em>, is a large mud fort, the commandant of which, according to the report of the natives, agreed to surrender the place to Colonel Fullarton. As he wished, however, to make an appearance of resistance, some pioneers were sent into the ditch to undermine the wall; which they did very coolly, while over their heads the garrison kept up a tremendous fire. When the passage was open, the firing ceased, and our troops walked in quietly, without any injury having been done on either side. Previous to this the town was very large; but it is now only beginning to recover from a state of ruin. Mr. Hurdis having made it the head office (<em>Cutchery</em>) of his district, it will soon increase. He has laid out the plan of a new town, in which all the streets will be straight and wide; and in this a good many new houses have been built. The inland situation of the place is, however, a great disadvantage; and in favourable seasons the cultivators cannot find a market for their grain.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-329"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="329" data-folio="309" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n328_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 309">[p. 309]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 18–20. Management of Mr. Hurdis.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">18th to the 20th <em>November.</em>—I remained with Mr. Hurdis, a most intelligent and active young gentleman. He manages the disputes about cast, and those arising between the right and left hand sides, in the same manner as is done by Major Macleod. The nature, indeed, of the whole management of both their districts is nearly the same; and in place of a jealousy between them, as belonging to two different services, they live in the greatest cordiality, and the only struggle between them is an honourable emulation in the performance of their duty.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Speedy justice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Both gentlemen make it a rule, that their <em>Umlahs</em>, or native officers, should not leave the court, until every cause that comes before it is decided.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rents.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Hurdis thinks that the present rents are greatly too high; and, no doubt, the peasantry here, as well as in almost every part of India, are miserably poor. I am inclined to think, however, that other causes contribute more to this than the greatness of the rents. Mr. Hurdis says, that all the land which is not cultivated is by no means unlet (<em>Tirsi</em>); but owing to the want of rain, and of stock, the farmers are not able to cultivate the whole of what they rent. This, in my opinion, shows, that the fields are by no means over-assessed; and that the farmers, if they would not grasp at more than they have stock to manage, might be in a much more comfortable situation. One great cause indeed of the poverty of the farmers, and consequent poverty of crops, in many parts of India, is the custom of forcing land upon people who have no means of cultivating it. Thus all the lands are apparently occupied; but it is in a manner that is worse than if one half of them were entirely waste. I believe every intelligent farmer in England will say, that one acre fully improved will give more profit than two that are half cultivated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Polygars.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. class="shoulder-sidenote">Garden cultivation.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Machines for irrigation.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov.18—20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Garden-ground rents here at 80, 60, 50, and 40 <em>Canter'-Ráya Fanams</em> a <em>Vullam</em>, or at 11<em>s</em>. 7<em>d</em>., 8<em>s</em>. 8¼<em>d</em>., 7<em>s</em>. 3<em>d</em>., and 5<em>s</em>. 9½<em>d</em>. an acre, according to its quality. When the water is far below the surface, it is raised by the <em>Capily</em>, one of which can supply a <em>Vullam</em> of land, or 4¼ acres. If the depth of the water be less, it is raised by the <em>Yatam</em>, on which four men walk along the balance. A <em>Vullam</em> of land requires from one to two <em>Yatams</em>, according to the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-335" data-page="335" data-folio="315" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n334_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 315">[p. 315]</button> distance the water has to be raised ; but two <em>Yatams</em>, wrought by ten men, are here reckoned cheaper than one <em>Capily</em>, wrought by one man and two oxen : the men, however, do other work in the garden.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tobacco.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The principal article cultivated is tobacco ; and a crop of grain is always procured in the course of the year from the same ground. The produce of a <em>Vullam</em> land of a good quality is 700 bundles of tobacco, weighing on an average 8 <em>Polams</em>, and worth 25 <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanams</em> a hundred. The crop of <em>Sholum</em> is estimated at 6 <em>Podis</em>, or at 15½ bushels an acre. The crop of <em>Cambu</em> from tobacco land is estimated at the same amount with that of <em>Sholum</em> ; that of <em>Ragy</em> is estimated at 7 <em>Podis</em>, or 18 bushels an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sale of grain.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The farmers who are in easy circumstances keep their grain until they can retail it in the weekly markets. Poor men, in order to discharge their rents, are under the necessity of selling it to dealers, and in general lose 20 per cent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Servants.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 18—20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The servants employed here in agriculture are hired in the beginning of the year for twelve months. They may change their service when this term expires, if they be not in their master’s debt ; but, as he generally advances money for their marriages, and other ceremonies, they are seldom at liberty to go away. They get twenty <em>Bullas</em> of rough rice (<em>Paddy</em>) a month, with four <em>Fanams</em> and one <em>Siliga</em> of rough rice yearly ; and their master pays their house rent. The whole is about 31 bushels of rough rice, of which one half is husk, with two shillings in money, besides the house rent, which will not exceed one or two shillings a year. These servants generally have one wife, who at seed-time and harvest works for the master for daily wages. A woman’s daily wages are four <em>Puddies</em> of grain, worth about nine-tenths of a penny. A man gets 6 <em>Puddies</em> of grain. A servant with these wages can once or twice a month procure a little animal food. Milk is too expensive. His common diet consists of some boiled grain, with a little salt and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-336" data-page="336" data-folio="316" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n335_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 316">[p. 316]</button> capsicum, and perhaps some pickles. His drink is the water in which the grain was boiled. He has very little clothing, and that little is extremely dirty; his house is a hovel, and he is commonly over-run with vermin and cutaneous disorders. The women, although not clean, are fully-clothed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Saline earths.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Throughout the <em>Coimbatore</em> province there are earths impregnated with muriatic salts, and others with nitrates; both of which have occasionally been made into culinary salt, and nitre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Saltpetre.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <em>Tippoo's</em> reign the makers of saltpetre received advances from government, and prepared the saltpetre from the earth. It was twice boiled, and was delivered to the government at 1 <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanam</em> for the <em>Bulla</em> containing 4 <em>Puddies</em> of 72 <em>Rupees</em> weight each, or at about 7<em>s</em>. 6¾<em>d</em>. a hundred-weight. This earth seems to contain the nitre ready formed, as no potash was added to it by the makers. It is only to be found in the hot season; so that I had no opportunity of examining its contents. I saw the two places in this neighbourhood where it is collected. The soil in both is very sandy and rocky, and the ways passing over them are much frequented by men and cattle. From the 10th of January until the 10th of February the saline earth is scraped from the surface, and is lixiviated, boiled, and crystallized twice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 21.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Calcareous <em>Tufa.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">21st <em>November</em>.—I went about eleven miles to <em>Puna-puram</em>. By the way I saw very little cultivation, but the whole country has formerly been ploughed. From a want of trees and hedges it is very bare, and the soil is rather poor. Immense fields of lime-stone are every where to be seen; and the <em>strata</em> of it at <em>Puna-puram</em> are much thicker than I have observed any where else. Many wells having been dug through these <em>strata</em>, to the depth of twelve and fifteen feet, give the traveller a good view of them. The calcarious matter seems to have been gradually deposited in horizontal <em>strata</em>, or layers. It involves small angular masses of quartz, and other stones, which, I suppose, must have arisen from its having <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-337" data-page="337" data-folio="317" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n336_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 317">[p. 317]</button> flowed over the surface of the original <em>strata</em> while it was in a soft state, and collected fragments of these as it rolled along. On the surface of the layers, or in cavities, some of it assumes a botryoidal form, while other parts of these cavities have a smooth undulating or conchoidal surface. The original <em>strata</em> are all <em>aggregate</em> rocks. <em>Puna-puram</em> is a small fort, of which the hereditary chief is a young boy. He was brought to me by his grandmother, and male relations, who are the chief farmers in the place. This season they have had scarcely any rain, to which some of the waste appearance of the country must be attributed ; but they say, that they have suffered much from the neighbouring <em>Polygars</em>, especially during a commotion that took place about three years ago.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 22. <em>Polygars.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">22d <em>November.</em>—I went seven and a half <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Mangalam</em>, an open village belonging to a <em>Polygar</em>. The country is not so stony as that through which I passed yesterday ; but it is equally uncultivated. <em>Mangalam</em> is now reduced to forty houses. It formerly contained one hundred. This diminution is attributed to the oppression of <em>Tippoo</em>, and to want of rain ; for many of the cultivators have removed to places blessed with a more favourable climate. The <em>Polygar</em> is one of the most stupid looking men that I have ever seen, and goes about with very little attendance, or state.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Saline soil.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Wherever wells have been dug into the lime-stone, water has been found at no great distance from the surface ; yet here there is little or no garden cultivation. Much of the well water has a saline taste ; and in almost every part of the neighbourhood culinary salt may be procured in the dry season by scraping the surface of the earth, and by lixiviation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 23. Robbers.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">23d <em>November.</em>—I went seven <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Pujar</em>-<em>petta</em>, an open village with a few shops. Like almost all those in this neighbourhood, it is surrounded and intersected by many hedges, which serve as a defence against the thieves and robbers <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-338" data-page="338" data-folio="318" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n337_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 318">[p. 318]</button> who come to drive away the cattle; and these miscreants, owing to the vicinity of the <em>Polygars</em>, have always been numerous. The village belongs immediately to the government, but is surrounded by the lands of <em>Polygars</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This day's road led through a country which is in nearly a similar state with all that I have seen west from <em>Darapuram</em>; but the soil in some places is much better, and really very good. The hills of <em>Coimbetore</em>, and those that bound the <em>Ani-malaya</em> pass on the south, are both visible from <em>Pujar-petta</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 24. <em>Palachy.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">24th <em>November</em>.—I went six <em>Malabar</em> hours' journey to <em>Palachy</em>. As I approached it, the country became gradually more cultivated, and better inclosed; and its environs look well, being adorned with groves of coco-nut palms; but there are no other trees near it. The town contains 300 poor houses and a small temple, and derives its name from the second wife of a <em>Vaylalar</em>, who came to the place when the country was entirely covered with woods, and began to clear it by the <em>Cotu-Cadu</em> cultivation. The town is rising fast into importance, having been made the residence of a <em>Tahsildar</em>, and being placed in the line of the new road that has been opened to <em>Pali-ghat</em>. Near it is a small fort.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Roman coins.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this vicinity was lately dug up a pot, containing a great many Roman silver coins, of which Mr. Hurdis was so kind as to give me six. They were of two kinds, but all of the same value, each weighing 56 grains. One of the kinds is of <em>Augustus</em>. The legend round the head is CAESAR AVCVSTVS DIVI F PATER PATRIAE; that is, <em>Cæsar Augustus Divi Filius Pater Patriæ</em>. Above the reverse, representing two persons standing with two bucklers and spears placed between them, the legend is AVCVSTI F COS DESIC PRINC IVVENT; that is, <em>Augusti Filio Consule designato, principe juventutis</em>. Under the figures is written CAESARIA, or <em>Cæsaria</em>, at some city of which name it has been struck. The other coin is of the same weight, and belongs to <em>Tiberius</em>. The legend round the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-339" data-page="339" data-folio="319" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n338_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 319">[p. 319]</button> head is TI CAESAR DIVI ΛVC F ΛVCVSTVS; <em>Tiberius Cæsar Divi Augusti Filius Augustus.</em> On the reverse, representing a person seated, and holding a spear in one hand and a branch in the other, is the following legend: PONTIF MAXIM, or <em>Pontifex Maximus.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Statistical accounts of the revenue officers.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Tahsildar</em> showed me a very regular account of the whole lands in his district, according to the mensuration and valuation made by <em>Chica Déva Ráya</em> of <em>Mysore</em>. The proportion of land not possibly arable is stated to be very small; and almost the whole face of the country, except in the immediate vicinity of <em>Palachy</em>, appears to the traveller to be waste; yet the <em>Tahsildar's</em> accompts state the whole arable lands to be occupied.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures of the farmers.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The manner of letting the lands here is very singular. The worst ground, being left for pasture as a common, pays no rent, and must be much more extensive than the <em>Tahsildar</em> states; as is clearly proveable by the immense extent of uncultivated land that is every where to be seen. The remainder of the ground belonging to each village, and which is reckoned all that is arable, has an average valuation fixed upon it. In some villages this is 20 <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Bulla</em> for the whole arable land, good or bad; in others, it is so high as 50 <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Bulla</em>. If the fields rated as <em>Bullas</em> contained no more than the proper measure, the first rent would be <em>2s.</em> 10¾<em>d.</em> an acre, the latter <em>7s. 3d.</em>; the average value of the whole lands of a village having been fixed, the fields are divided into three qualities, according to the goodness of their soil; and they are then divided among the cultivators by an assembly of these people; in which, in order to prevent partialities, the officers of revenue have no right to interfere. The farmers complain, that the land is forced on them, and that they are compelled to rent more than they have stock to enable them to cultivate. A man who rents 17 <em>Bullas</em> of land is able only to plough 9 of them; whereas, if he had full stock, he would plough between 11 and 12, leaving one third part in fallow. The rents, however, have been <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-340" data-page="340" data-folio="320" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n339_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 320">[p. 320]</button> lowered; in some villages one-fifth, in others one-third, in order to compensate the loss which the farmer suffers by this manner of renting lands, where there is not a sufficient stock to cultivate the whole. This sort of tenure seems to be a great evil, and, in order to keep down the rent, will occasion constant clamours of poverty among the farmers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Size of farms and plough-lands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">One plough is reckoned here adequate to cultivate 2 <em>Bullas</em> of land, or 8,<sup>569</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> acres. A few farmers possess 10 ploughs, but by far the greater number have only one.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Servants, and price of labour. <em>Pudials.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">There are here two kinds of servants employed by the farmers to cultivate the lands: they are called <em>Pudial</em>, and <em>Pungal</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Pudials</em> receive yearly 3 <em>Podis</em> of grain (29 bushels), worth 48 <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanams</em>, with 10 <em>Fanams</em> in money, and a house. The 58 <em>Fanams</em> are equal to 1 <em>l.</em> 8 <em>s.</em> 9 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub><em>d.</em> The wife and children of the <em>Pudial</em> are paid for whatever work they perform. He is hired by the year; but, if he contracts a debt with his master, he cannot quit the service till that be discharged.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Pungals.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Pungals</em> go to a rich farmer, and for a share of the crop undertake to cultivate his lands. He advances the cattle, implements, seed, and money or grain, that is necessary for the subsistence of the <em>Pungals</em>. He also gives each family a house. He takes no share in the labour, which is all performed by the <em>Pungals</em> and their wives and children; but he pays the rent out of his share on the division of the crop, which takes place when that is ripe. If a farmer employs six <em>Pungals</em> to cultivate his land, the produce is divided into 15 portions, which are distributed as follow:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>6 to the farmer, or <em>Punnadi</em>, for rent, seed, &amp;c.</li><li>1 to ditto for profit.</li><li>2 to ditto for interest of money advanced.</li><li>6 to the <em>Pungals</em>, or labourers.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">15 portions.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-341"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="341" data-folio="321" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n340_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 321">[p. 321]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Out of their portions the <em>Pungals</em> must repay the farmer the money which he has advanced for their subsistence. The farmers prefer employing <em>Pudials</em>, when they can be procured; but among the labourers the condition of the <em>Pungals</em> is considered as preferable to that of the <em>Pudials</em>. Nov. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The manner of cultivating these crops is as follows: the field, while in grass, is manured by folding on it as many cattle as can be procured. Then between the 26th of May and the 27th of July it is ploughed five times. During this season there are slight showers of rain; but in a few days afterwards the heavy rains generally commence. When this happens, sow the <em>Cambu</em> broad-cast, and cover it with the plough. On the second or third day furrows are drawn through the field, at the distance from each other of six cubits. Into these a man, who follows the plough, drops the seeds of <em>Tovary</em>, <em>Muchu-cotay</em>, <em>Mutu-cotay</em>, and of <em>Tata-Pyra</em> (see the annexed Table), while another plough comes behind, and covers them with a second furrow. These accompanying seeds are never intermixed; one being sown in one part of the field, and another in another part: but in every field a proportion of each is sown. The <em>Tata-Pyra</em> is sometimes mixed with the <em>Cambu</em> seed, and sown broad-cast. At the end of one month, the young <em>Cambu</em> is about 4 or 5 inches high, and the field is then ploughed. In five months it ripens, and two months afterwards the accompanying grains come to maturity. The ears of the <em>Cambu</em>, when ripe, are cut off, and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-346" data-page="346" data-folio="326" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n345_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 326">[p. 326]</button> immediately trodden out. The grain, after being separated from the spikes, is dried in the sun two or three days, and put up in store-houses, so as to be secured from moisture and the circulation of air. After having been kept one year, its value is much diminished, and at the end of two years it becomes totally useless.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Cambu</em> straw is only used for thatch, and is allowed to stand on the field until between the 12th of March and the 10th of April, when it is pulled up by the roots. These being large, the ground is loosened by the operation, and, without having been ploughed, is immediately afterwards sown with <em>Sholum</em>, or <em>Wulindu</em>, or <em>Pachapyra</em>, or <em>Ellu</em> (see the Table). After these seeds have been sown broad-cast, the field is once ploughed. If <em>Shamay</em> is to be sown, the field is ploughed once, the seed is sown between the 12th of May and the 11th of June, and then covered by the plough. One month after having been sown, the <em>Sholum</em> field must be again ploughed ; the others ripen without any trouble. <em>Sholum</em> straw is here reckoned the best fodder. These crops ripen between the 14th of September and the 14th of October ; and immediately after they are reaped the field is ploughed, and sown with <em>Colu</em>, or <em>Horse</em>-<em>gram</em>, the seed of which is covered by a second ploughing. At the end of a month weeds ought to be removed by the hand. In five months more it is ripe.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cotton.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When cotton is cultivated with <em>Sholum</em>, the seed of the latter is first sown, and then that of the cotton is scattered over the field. Both are then covered by the plough, and at the end of the first month the field is again ploughed. At the end of the second month the weeds are removed by a small hoe. After the <em>Sholum</em> has been reaped, the field is ploughed three times between the cotton plants, which grow quite irregularly three or four cubits from each other. Between the 10th of February and the 10th of April the cotton produces a full crop. Next year, according to the native reckoning, between the 15th of October and the 12th of December, the field is ploughed again three times, and at the usual season gives a crop of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-347" data-page="347" data-folio="327" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n346_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 327">[p. 327]</button> three fourths of what it produced in the first year. The plants are immediately pulled up, and the field is allowed a year’s fallow.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 24. Soil.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The soil here is partly a red, and partly a dark coloured sandy loam; but in some neighbouring villages there is a rich black soil, which every year produces a crop of <em>Upum</em> cotton, mixed with the <em>Cicer arietinum</em>, or with two umbelliferous plants, called <em>Danya</em> and and <em>Cuderi Womum</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Cuderi Womum.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Cuderi Womum</em>, or Horse-<em>womum</em>, is used as a carminative for horses; and, such being considered by the natives of this country as necessary for these animals, a mixture of it with pepper, onions, and the like, is once a week given to every horse.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Pasture.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">I have already mentioned, that besides the bad stony land, which is common, the farmers here keep in fallow for pasture one third of their whole land. They pay full rent for the latter, but nothing for the use of the commons. For pasture, they never are necessitated to send their cattle to the hills. The sickness that prevailed last year among the cattle over a great part of the country was not severely felt at <em>Palachy</em>; but the year before it had raged. The cattle of the cow kind in this neighbourhood are of the same breed with those above the <em>Ghats</em>, but are rather inferior in size.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Polygars and Hindu militia.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 24.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Ani-malaya Polygars</em> are twelve in number. My information is taken from one of them, called the <em>Gopina Gauda</em>. He says, that six generations ago they were sent into the country by <em>Trimula Náyaka</em>, the <em>Rájá</em> of <em>Madura</em>. Several of them are of <em>Telinga</em> descent, but not any are of the <em>Madura</em> family. Each of them paid an annual tribute, and, according to the extent of his district, was bound to keep up a certain number of <em>Candasharas</em>, or foot soldiers. Whenever called upon, the <em>Polygars</em> were bound to serve in the field with all these infantry; but then they got <em>Batta</em>, or subsistence money, from the <em>Rájá</em>. Each <em>Candashara</em> had a small farm, which he or his family cultivated for his support in peace, and for his clothing. The head <em>Candashara</em> of every village had a large farm, and acted under the <em>Polygar</em> as captain; but out of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-348" data-page="348" data-folio="328" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n347_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 328">[p. 328]</button> profit of his farm he was bound to provide arms for his company. Some of the villages in each district were thus divided among the <em>Candasharas</em> ; while others were let for a rent, out of which the <em>Polygar</em> maintained his family, and paid his tribute. Within his own district he possessed the power of life and death, with every kind of jurisdiction, civil and military. Of the twelve <em>Polygars</em> of <em>Ani-malaya</em>, five are of the <em>Vaycliar</em> cast, a <em>Telinga</em> tribe ; four are <em>Vaylalar</em>, a <em>Tamul</em> cast ; one is a <em>Golar Totier</em>, also of <em>Telinga</em> extraction ; one is a <em>Poloa</em>, which is a cast of <em>Malayalam</em> ; and the twelfth is of the <em>Vir'-pachry</em> family, the head of which is now in a kind of rebellion. The <em>Gopina Gauda's</em> district contained 60 villages, maintained 1000 <em>Candasharas</em>, and paid a tribute of 40,000 <em>Vir'-Ráyá Fanams</em>, or 951<em>l.</em> 7<em>s.</em> 2 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub><em>d.</em> Things continued in this state until the government of <em>Hyder</em>, who entirely did away the military tenure, but left each <em>Polygar</em> some lands in <em>Enám</em>, or free of rent, in place of what it might be supposed they before enjoyed for the support of their families. The <em>Enám</em> left to the <em>Gopina Gauda</em> was six villages, or one-tenth of his district. In this <em>Enám</em> he retained the full jurisdiction that he formerly possessed over his district ; for, in eastern governments, the life and property of the subject are frequently intrusted to the discretion of the most petty officers, or land-holders. On <em>Tippoo's</em> accession, the <em>Asoph</em> or lieutenant of <em>Coimbetore</em>, <em>Khadir Ali Khan</em>, forced the <em>Polygars</em> to pay tribute for the lands which <em>Hyder</em> had allowed them to retain, and they were entirely disarmed ; but they were allowed to retain over their vassals both civil and criminal jurisdiction. Ten years ago <em>Tippoo</em> endeavoured to seize them, in order, by circumcision, to make them Mussulmans ; but they made their escape into the country of the <em>Cochin Rájá</em>, and continued there until the fall of <em>Seringapatam</em>. The lands left to them by <em>Hyder</em> as <em>Enáms</em> have now been restored for a tribute, amounting to three-fourths of what was exacted by <em>Tippoo</em> in the beginning of his reign ; and their jurisdiction is similar to that of the <em>Tahsildars</em>, except that the government does not <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-349" data-page="349" data-folio="329" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n348_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 329">[p. 329]</button> interfere with the manner in which they let their lands. In fact, they are now almost on the same footing with the <em>Zemindars</em> of Bengal, only they possess a small authority in matters of police, and a limited civil jurisdiction, and their rents are more moderate. <em>Gopina Gauda</em> alleges, that he pays three-fourths of his collections; Mr. Hurdis estimates his profits at 40 per cent. Formerly, during the confusion which subsisted in the open country, the districts of these chiefs, being inaccessible without great trouble, were an asylum for those in distress; but since the Company’s government has given security to all well-disposed persons, most of the people who had retired thither have returned to their former places of residence; on which account the estates of the <em>Polygars</em> are now thinly inhabited. The <em>Polygars</em> collect their rents without the assistance of armed men. <em>Candasharas</em> are allowed to the <em>Tahsildars</em>; but they serve them rather in their capacity of officers of police, than in collecting the revenue.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Vaylalar</em>, a tribe of <em>Tamuls</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 24.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Throughout the <em>Coimbetore</em> province the <em>Vaylalar</em> are a numerous tribe of the <em>Tamul</em> race, and are esteemed to be of pure <em>Súdra</em> cast. They are of several different kinds; such as <em>Caracata</em>, <em>Palay</em>, <em>Chóla</em>, <em>Codical</em>, <em>Cotay</em>, <em>Pandáva</em>, and <em>Shayndalay Vaylalars</em>: of this last kind are those who give me information. All <em>Vaylalars</em> can eat together; but these different kinds do not intermarry, nor can a man marry a woman of the same family with himself in the male line. The <em>Vaylalar</em> are farmers, day-labourers, and servants who cultivate the earth; many of them can keep accompts, and read books written in their native language. At <em>Canghium</em> resides <em>Canghium Manadear</em>, hereditary chief of all the <em>Shayndalay Vaylalars</em>. Formerly this person settled all disputes in the cast; but Mr. Hurdis, having found that the hereditary chiefs excommunicated unjustly the people of their clans, ordered that all cast business should be settled in public court by the <em>Tahsildar</em>, with the advice of a council of persons skilled in the rules and customs of the cast in question. The people seem to be satisfied with this change. The <em>Vaylalars</em> are not permitted to drink intoxicating liquors; but such of them as have not <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-350" data-page="350" data-folio="330" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n349_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 330">[p. 330]</button> received <em>Upadésa</em> may eat animal food. If their first wife has children, they cannot marry another; nor do the men ever keep concubines in their houses. The women continue to be marriageable after the age of puberty; but widows are not allowed to take a second husband, nor to live with men as concubines. For adultery, if the fault has been committed with a person of the cast, a woman is seldom divorced, unless her shame has become very public. The widow ought to burn herself with her husband's corpse, and this is still sometimes, though very rarely, practised. The tombs of such women as have committed this action are considered as places of worship, and their memory is venerated as that of saints. They are all worshippers of <em>Siva</em>; but the proper <em>penates</em>, or family gods, are various <em>Saktis</em>, or female destructive spirits; such as <em>Kálí</em>, <em>Bhadra</em>-<em>Kálí</em>, and the like. The <em>Vaylalar</em> offer sacrifices at the temples of these idols, and, if they have not received <em>Upadésa</em>, eat the flesh; but in <em>Chéra</em> the <em>Pújáris</em> or priests in these temples are all <em>Pundarums</em>, who are the <em>Súdras</em> dedicated to the service of <em>Siva's</em> temples, in the same manner as the <em>Satánanas</em> are dedicated to those of <em>Vishnu</em>. In sickness, they make vows to ornament the temple of the <em>Sakti</em> who is supposed to occasion the disease; and if they recover, they employ the potter, who makes an image of a child or a horse, which is placed in the court of the temple. This kind of offering is extremely common in every part of <em>Coimbetore</em>, but I have not seen it in any other part of India. If the proper funeral ceremonies are performed, the <em>Vaylalar</em> believe that after their decease they will reside at the feet of <em>Iswara</em>. They do not know what becomes of those who after death are not burned with the due rites. They do not require a <em>Puróhita</em> to read <em>Mantrams</em> at any of the family ceremonies; but, if the <em>Panchánga</em> chooses to come and read, he receives something for his trouble. Their <em>Gurus</em> are the <em>Siva Bráhmanas</em>, or <em>Bráhmans</em> who act as <em>Pújáris</em> in the temples of <em>Siva</em>, and the great gods of his family. These are considered as greatly inferior to the <em>Smartal</em>, either <em>Vaidíka</em>, or <em>Lokika</em>. The <em>Guru</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-351" data-page="351" data-folio="331" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n350_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 331">[p. 331]</button> comes annually to each village, distributes consecrated leaves and holy water, and receives a <em>Fanam</em> from each person, with as much grain as they choose to give. Some of them purchase an <em>Upadésa</em> from the <em>Guru</em>; giving for it, according to their circumstances, from one to ten <em>Fanams</em>. Those who have procured this may make a <em>Lingam</em> of mud, and perform <em>Puja</em> or worship to this rude emblem of the deity, by pouring flowers and water over it while they repeat the <em>Upadésa</em>. Such persons must abstain entirely from animal food. Those who have no <em>Upadésa</em> must pray without any set form, but are allowed to eat the flesh of sacrifices.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Handy Curubas.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Handy Curubaru</em> are settled in this country in small numbers, and are generally employed as armed messengers for the police. They are all of <em>Karnátaca</em> extraction, and came originally from <em>Kana-giri</em> and <em>Anagundi</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Totear</em> are a <em>Telinga</em> tribe settled here in considerable numbers as cultivators. They are very poor, and remarkably ignorant, which prevented me from obtaining any rational account of their customs.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">27th <em>November</em>.—I went seven <em>Malabar</em> hours’ journey to <em>Ani</em>-<em>malaya</em>. Until I came to the river <em>Alima</em>, the road passed through a country well cultivated and inclosed. I forded the <em>Alima</em> at a town called <em>Umbrayen-pallyam</em>, which has formerly been a large place, but is now mostly in ruins, having been destroyed by the <em>Nairs</em> in their wars with <em>Tippoo</em>. I then proceeded up the side of the <em>Alima</em>, having a fine canal with rice-fields to my left, and woods on my right. These occupy the grounds of a village, in which there was formerly much cultivation of dry grains. This also was destroyed by the <em>Nairs</em>, who are considered by the people here as fierce and cruel barbarians.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Ani-malaya.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Ani-malaya</em>, or <em>Elephant-hill</em>, is so called from the great number of elephants and hills in its neighbourhood. It is a town which contains about 400 houses, and is situated on the west side of the <em>Alima</em>. It is the common thoroughfare between <em>Malabar</em> and the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-352" data-page="352" data-folio="332" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n351_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 332">[p. 332]</button> southern part of the <em>Arcot</em> dominions, being placed opposite to the wide passage that is between the southern end of the <em>Ghats</em> of <em>Karnáta</em>, and the hills that run north from <em>Cape Comorin</em>. The <em>Madura Rájás</em>, the former lords of the country, built a fort close to the river; which having fallen to ruins, the materials were removed by the <em>Mysore Rájás</em>, and a new fort was built at some distance to the westward. Twelve years ago <em>Tippoo</em> gave it some repairs, and, to procure materials for the purpose, pulled down five large temples. It is still a very poor work, and is in the district of <em>Palachy</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Devastation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The greater part of the dry-field in the neighbourhood is now overgrown with woods; for eight entire villages to the westward have been completely destroyed by the <em>Nairs</em>, and have never been repeopled. There are three dams on the <em>Alima</em>, that water much rice-ground, the greater part of which is cultivated. There was formerly a fine tank, supplied with water from a branch of the <em>Alima</em> called the <em>Shinar</em>; but it fell into decay, and now the workmen are only beginning to put it in order. The whole watered-land in the village of <em>Ani-malaya</em> amounts, according to the measurement of <em>Chica Déva Rájá</em>, to 750 <em>Candacas</em>, which should be about 3100 acres. The dry-field is rated in the books at 400 <em>Bullas</em>; but of this three-fourths have become totally waste, and 70 <em>Bullas</em> only are actually cultivated. Ten villages in the immediate vicinity are without a single inhabitant. This shows how very inaccurate the accompts are that were shown to me at <em>Palachy</em> by the <em>Tahsildar</em>. Indeed, very little dependence is to be placed on the statements of native officers of revenue.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Land-measure.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 27. Rent.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When the measurement of this district was made by the order of <em>Chica Déva Rája</em> of <em>Mysore</em>, a pole was taken, which was 25 <em>Adies</em>, or native feet, in length. Marks have been made on a long stone, which is preserved as a standard. These show the pole to have been 24 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> English feet in length. 20 poles in length, by 15 in breadth, are called a <em>Candaca</em> of watered-land, which is therefore 4,<sup>134</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> acres. The <em>Candaca</em> of grain is rather more than 3 bushels. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-353" data-page="353" data-folio="333" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n352_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 333">[p. 333]</button> The whole rice-lands pay 72½ <em>Canter'-Ráya Fanams</em> a <em>Candaca</em> (10<em>s.</em> 10½<em>d.</em> an acre), whether the soil be good or bad. Every ten years the different farmers draw lots for the fields, each of which, being a long narrow stripe of land, contains all the varieties of soil.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Watered-lands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The farmers of <em>Ani-malaya</em> are mostly <em>Sudras</em>; and, owing to the want of hands and stock, can only take one crop in the year from their lands; but there being plenty of water for two crops, one half of the farm is cultivated at one season, and the other at another. Rice and a little <em>Betel-leaf</em> (<em>Piper Betle</em>) are the only articles raised upon watered ground. The crop sown between the 13th of July and the 13th of August is cultivated after the dry-seed manner. The sprouted-seed may be sown at any time between the 10th of May and the 10th of December, and is attended with the least trouble. This year a little transplanted rice has been tried, but in the present want of labourers it is considered as requiring too much trouble.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Produce.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the accompanying <span class="sc">Table</span> will be seen the particulars of the cultivation of rice in this district. The estimate is formed on the average of good soils, according to the report of the cultivators, who say, that the smallest produce is about three quarters of that stated in the Table. I however think it rather probable, that what I have given may be considered as the average produce of the whole lands, good and bad. The <em>Cutari</em> rice is that most commonly cultivated, as it is less liable than the others to be injured by the herds of wild elephants; for these animals, although they eat rice, do not kill that kind when they tread on it. The <em>Cartic Sambau</em> is the best. At <em>Ani-malaya</em> no manure, either of leaves or dung, is used.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-354"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="354" data-folio="334" Nov. 27. Dry-field rent.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although this is in the <em>Palachy</em> district, the manner of letting the dry-field at the two places is quite different. The rent here is paid according to the kind of crop. A <em>Bulla</em> land, sown with <em>Cambu</em> or <em>Sholum</em>, pays 25 <em>Canter’-Ráya Fanams</em>, or 3<em>s.</em> 7½<em>d.</em> an acre; if cultivated for <em>Shamay</em>, <em>Colu</em>, &amp;c. it pays 15 <em>Fanams</em>, or 2<em>s.</em> 2<em>d.</em> an acre; if left fallow for pasture, it pays 5 <em>Fanams</em>, or about 8½<em>d.</em> an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Hilly country between <em>Travancore</em> and <em>Madura.</em> Drug-renters.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Here is a person called <em>Malaya-pudy</em>, or <em>hill-village-man</em>. He rents the exclusive privilege of collecting drugs in the hills south from <em>Ani-malaya</em>. These are collected for him by a hill people called <em>Cadar</em>, of whom, among the hills two days journey hence, there is a village of 13 houses. The renter has there a small house, to which he occasionally goes to receive the drugs that the <em>Cadar</em> have collected, and brings them home on oxen. The men only work for him, and each daily receives in advance four <em>Puddies</em> of rice, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-355" data-page="355" data-folio="335" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n354_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 335">[p. 335]</button> worth half a <em>Vir’-Ráya Fanam</em>, or about 3<em>d</em>. At the end of the year the accompts are settled, every article having a fixed value; and the whole that each person has delivered having been estimated at this rate, he receives the balance, if any be due. In <em>Tippoo’s</em> government, the renter paid annually 30 <em>Canter’-Ráyá Pagodas</em>, or 6<em>l</em>. 4<em>s</em>. 1 <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub><em>d</em>. His rent has this year been raised to 150 <em>Pagodas</em>, or 31<em>l</em>. 0<em>s</em>. 8 <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub><em>d</em>.; but then he is allowed to take all the ivory that is found where elephants have died, and which formerly belonged to the government. The articles collected on account of the renter are as follow:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 27.</aside></div><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Nonaputta</em>; the bark of a <em>Morinda</em>, which is used as a red dye.</li><li>2. <em>Magali Calangu</em>; the root of a non-descript <em>Cynanchum</em>, which is a favourite pickle with the natives, and smells exactly like bugs.</li><li>3. <em>Inji</em>; wild ginger.</li><li>4. <em>Munjal</em>; wild turmeric.</li><li>5. <em>Mutti palu</em>; the juice of a tree, which by long keeping concretes into a kind of gum; both juice and gum are used by the natives to fumigate their clothes.</li><li>6. <em>Cunghi-lium</em>; the resin of a non-descript tree, which I have called <em>Chloroxylon Dupada</em>, and which is a kind of frankincense.</li><li>7. <em>Shica-gai</em>; the fruit of the <em>Mimosa saponaria</em>, used by the natives to wash the oil out of their hair.</li><li>8. Honey and wax. There are here four kinds of honey-bee; 1st. <em>Malan ten</em>, a large bee which builds in cavities of rocks, and forms a large nest. One will produce four <em>Puddies</em>, or about 3 quarts of honey; and four <em>Polams</em>, or 12 <sup>8</sup>⁄<sub>10</sub> ounces of wax. In procuring this there is much trouble, as the bee stings violently, and builds in places very difficult of access. A <em>Bamboo</em>-ladder is let down by means of a rope, from the summit of the rock, to where the honey is. The <em>Cadar</em>, taking a fire-brand in his hand, descends by the rope to the ladder, and, having chased away the bees by means of the fire, he <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-356" data-page="356" data-folio="336" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n355_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 336">[p. 336]</button> collects the honey, and is then drawn up. Two men this year have been so violently stung by the bees, that they let go their hold, and were killed by the fall. 2d. <em>Todugy ten</em>, a middling sized bee, that builds in the hollow trunks of old trees. Its nest is but about a fourth part of the size of that of the <em>Malan ten</em>. The only trouble in collecting this is the enlarging the hole by which the bees enter, so as to get at the combs. Their sting is of no consequence. 3d. <em>Coshu ten</em>, a very small bee with a proportionably small quantity of honey, and that of a bad quality. It also builds in hollow trees. 4th. <em>Cambu ten</em>, a large bee which builds its nest round the branches of trees. The quantity of honey is small, but it is of the best quality. This bee is easily driven away by the twig of a tree switched round. The common price of wax is 30 <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanams</em> for the <em>Tolam</em> of 800 <em>Rupees</em> weight, or 4<em>l.</em> 2<em>s.</em> 6<em>d.</em> a hundred-weight.</li><li>9. <em>Casturi Munjal</em>; a kind of wild turmeric, which has a smell somewhat resembling musk. It is mixed with the powder of sandal-wood, with which the <em>Hindu</em> women of rank rub their skins.</li><li>10. <em>Levanga putty</em>; the bark of the <em>Laurus Cassia</em>. It is the <em>Cassia lignea</em> of India, which is very inferior to that of China.</li><li>11. Ivory.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tribes occupying the southern hills.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cardamoms.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The renter trades with villages belonging to <em>Travancore</em>, and inhabited by rude tribes called <em>Visuar</em> or <em>Coravan</em>, <em>Vucamar</em>, and <em>Munnan</em>. These tribes occupy a hilly tract ten days journey in length, and are scattered through this extent in villages of ten or twelve huts. They use the <em>Cotu-cadu</em> cultivation, and collect the same articles with those above mentioned, and have besides cardamoms, which is the only thing that they sell to the renter who lives at <em>Ani-malaya</em>. In January they are brought to him fit for the market, and he knows nothing of the manner in which they are <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-357" data-page="357" data-folio="337" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n356_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 337">[p. 337]</button> prepared, only that they grow on the hills without cultivation. The <em>Cadar</em> inform me, that their neighbours in the hills of <em>Travancore</em> know the places fit for cardamoms, by observing in the woods places where some of the plants grow. There the hill-people cut all the trees, and give the sun access to the plants, which afterwards shoot up apace. It is three years, however, before they come to perfection. In the third and fourth years they produce abundantly, and then die; when the wood is allowed to grow up, and another part is cleared for a future crop. Between the 10th of January and the 9th of February the fruit is fit for cutting. If the seed be to be preserved in the capsules or husks, the <em>scapi</em>, or fruit-stems, before the fruit is quite ripe, are cut off by the root, and kept in a heap for some days; after which the capsules are separated from them by the hand. If the seed only be to be collected, the fruit-stems are allowed to ripen, until they become redish, and until the birds begin to eat the seed. They are then cut, dried under the pressure of a stone for three or four days, and rubbed with the hand to separate the seed. This sells in the market here for 6 <em>Canter'-Ráya Pagodas</em> a <em>Tolam</em>, or 10<em>l.</em> 6<em>s.</em> 5½<em>d.</em> a hundred-weight. The capsules are rarely brought hither for sale, and are higher priced.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pepper, wild.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Wild black-pepper is also found in these hills; but it is of a bad quality.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Myrobalans.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In some of the hills which belong to <em>Erupa Náyaka</em>, one of the Company's <em>Polygars</em>, a renter has the exclusive privilege of collecting the <em>Myrobalans</em> called <em>Cadugai</em>, which are the fruit of the <em>Myrobalanus Arula</em> Buch: MSS.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tamarinds.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Ani-malaya</em> are three persons called tamarind-renters, who pay a trifling rent for the exclusive privilege of collecting the tamarinds, honey, wax, and <em>Nonaputta</em>, that are found in the woods, which lie near the town. The people employed by them are called <em>Malasir</em>, and are also the wood-cutters of the country.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-358"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="358" data-folio="338" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n357_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 338">[p. 338]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 27. <em>Nerium tinctorium</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">There is here plenty of the <em>Pala-tree</em>, or <em>Nerium tinctorium</em> Roxb: MSS.; but at present nobody makes it into <em>Palac</em>, or indigo. Fourteen years ago a man from <em>Darapuram</em> came for this purpose, but he was carried away by tigers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sandal-wood.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the gardens round the town a few sandal trees have been planted. It does not come to any perfection; but its leaves serve as an offering to the idols. It does not grow on the hills.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Unhealthy forests.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I could have wished to have passed some days among these hills in botanical investigations; but at this season my attendants would have been exposed to great danger from the unhealthy air, and one half of them would probably have been seized with fevers; as I experienced in the hills of the <em>Káveri-pura</em> pass, which are not reckoned so bad as those of <em>Ani-malaya</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wild elephants.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The elephants are increasing here in number, owing to no hunt having been made for some years past. They are very destructive and formidable, and kill many poor people who are travelling in a solitary manner.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Cadar</em>, a rude tribe.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nov. 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Cadar</em> are a rude tribe inhabiting the hills in this neighbourhood, and speaking a dialect that differs in accent only from the <em>Tamul</em>. The men live by collecting drugs for the renter, as I have already mentioned. The women collect wild roots that are edible. They have no means of killing game, but eat any that they find dead. They rear no domestic animals, nor cultivate any thing whatever; but their clothing is as good as that of the neighbouring peasantry. They pay no taxes, and the renter settles all disputes among them. They live in villages called <em>Malaya-pudy</em>. They always marry in their own tribe, but cannot take a girl who is of the same family with themselves in the male line. They are allowed a plurality of wives. The lover presents the mother of his mistress with some cloth, and iron tools, and the ceremony consists in a feast given to the relations. The girls continue to be marriageable after the age of puberty, and a widow can without disgrace marry <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-359" data-page="359" data-folio="339" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n358_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 339">[p. 339]</button> again. If a woman commit adultery, the tribe assembled deliver her over to her paramour, who pays a fine to the husband, and takes the woman to be his wife. They do not drink spirituous liquors; and they bury the dead. After death, the spirits of good men reside with a god named <em>Mudivirum</em>, while those of wicked men go to a bad place. Their temples are small huts, in which rude stones represent <em>Mudivirum</em>, and two female deities called <em>Pay-cotu-Ummum</em>, and <em>Kali Ummum</em>. These deities protect their votaries from tigers, elephants, and disease, but have no priests. Once a year the whole people assemble at the temple, and offer rice and flowers to the images, and sometimes sacrifice a goat. When in the low country, they say that they are of <em>Vishnu's</em> side; but they pray to every image that they see. They say, that the men of another tribe living in the hills, and called <em>Visabun</em>, or <em>Corabun</em>, are their <em>Gurus</em>, and are able to read and write. They make presents to their <em>Guru</em>, and he gives them consecrated ashes. They have nothing to do with the <em>Bráhmans</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Guard on the frontier of <em>Malayala</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">28th <em>November</em>.—I went seven <em>Malabar</em> hours' journey to <em>Mingara</em>, a place in the middle of the <em>Ani-malaya</em> forest, and on the frontier of the country which formerly belonged to the <em>Tamuri Rája</em>, where a guard of 15 armed men is placed by the <em>Tahsildar</em> of <em>Palachy</em>. The men are hutted on the banks of a mountain torrent; and, although relieved once a fortnight, suffer exceedingly from this unhealthful climate. They are stationed here to prevent the passage of thieves and armed vagabonds, to prevent smuggling, and to intercept unlawful correspondence. The three small huts which they occupy are the only habitations near the place.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wild elephants.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER X. Nov. 28.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On strong high trees the guard has constructed two stages, to which the men fly when they are attacked by solitary discontented male elephants, who are not to be driven away by firing at them, unless the ball takes place in some sensible part. Herds of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-360" data-page="360" data-folio="340" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n359_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 340">[p. 340]</button> elephants come very frequently to drink at the torrent; but are easily alarmed, and run away at the first shot. The guard meets with no annoyance from tigers. For the sake of water, merchants stop to breakfast at this place, and very often pass the night under protection of the guard. The road is a great thoroughfare, and between this and <em>Ani-malaya</em> is very good for loaded cattle. Carts might pass all the way, but in some places with difficulty. A very little expense would make the whole good.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Forests.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The woods are stately, and clear of bushes or climbers; nor does the grass reach higher than the knee. The season for examining them would be March and April; at present they are extremely unhealthful. The greater part of the soil, in the woods between this and <em>Ani-malaya</em> is tolerably good, and consists of gently swelling lands, with a moderate descent towards <em>Malabar</em>; so that the whole might be cultivated. The forests are too remote from water carriage to be valuable on account of producing timber for exportation; and the hills afford a sufficient quantity of timber for the use of the country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The following are the trees which I observed in passing through this forest; the names are <em>Tamul</em>; and the account of their qualities is given on the authority of some wood-cutters that I purposely hired to accompany me.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Buriga.</em></li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A lactescent tree, with leaves three-lobed, petioled, alternate, and without stipules.
