Ferries, and boats of <em>Malabar.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">DECEMBER 14th.—I went a short stage to <em>Panyáni</em>. Soon after leaving <em>Valiencodu</em>, I crossed the mouth of a small river, which, by the influx of salt water as it approaches the sea, is extended to a great width. I was ferried over it by means of two canoes lashed together, which forms a very safe conveyance for baggage, or foot passengers, but is not adapted for cattle, the latter being forced to swim. Orders have been issued by the commissioners to construct proper stages on canoes at every ferry; so that cattle, and even artillery, may be transported with safety. The canoes in this part of <em>Malabar</em> are among the best and handsomest that I have ever seen.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On the north side of the river is some level marshy ground, into which the tide is received, and salt is formed by the evaporation of the water by the heat of the sun. Between this and <em>Panyáni</em> the country is very beautiful, and thickly covered with groves of coco-nut trees, which are separated by rice-fields that are now covered with the second crop. This, however, by no means looks thriving. On the mere sand of the sea-shore may be here seen flourishing the coco-nut palm. It is said, that in such situations it produces fruit for ten years only; but that is of little consequence; as it seems to be reared at a very trifling expense, and is afterwards left entirely to nature.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Panyáni</em>, or <em>Punany W<span class="unclear" title="Uncertain reading in source scan">a-</span> cul.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 14.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Panyáni</em> is also called by the natives <em>Punany Wacul</em>, and contains 500 houses belonging to traders, with above forty mosques, and at <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-444" data-page="444" data-folio="420" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n443_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 420">[p. 420]</button> least 1000 huts inhabited by the lower orders of people. It is very irregularly built; but many of the houses are two stories high, and seem to be very comfortable dwellings. They are built of stone, and thatched with coco-nut leaves. The huts are inhabited by boatmen and fishermen, who were formerly <em>Mucuas</em>, a low cast of <em>Hindus</em>; but now they have all embraced the faith of <em>Mahomet</em>. All the mosques are thatched, and their principal entrance is at the east end, where the roof terminates abruptly in fanciful mouldings, and carved work, that by the natives are considered as ornamental. The town is scattered over a sandy plain, on the south side of a river, which descends from <em>Ani-malaya</em>, and enters the sea by a very wide channel. The mouth, however, is shut by a bar, which admits boats only to enter.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Patemar boats.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The trading boats are called <em>Patemars</em>, and on an average carry 50,000 coco-nuts, or 1000 <em>Mudies</em> of rice, equal to 500 <em>Bengal</em> bags. There are many <em>Patemars</em> larger, but these seldom frequent this port.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 14.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">About fifty years ago the <em>Moplays</em> of this place were very rich, and possessed vessels that sailed to <em>Surat</em>, <em>Mocha</em>, Madras, and Bengal; but the oppression of <em>Tippoo</em> has reduced them to great poverty, and most of them are now under the necessity of acting as agents to <em>Mousa</em>, a Mussulman merchant of <em>Tellichery</em>. They have, however, a few small boats, that go to <em>Tellichery</em> and <em>Calicut</em> for supplies of European and Bengal goods. The port is also frequented by vessels (<em>Patemars</em>) from different places on the coast. Those from Bombay bring wheat, <em>Meti</em>, or fenugreek, the pulses called <em>Wulindu</em>, <em>Pyru</em>, and <em>Avaray</em>, sugar-cane, <em>Jagory</em>, and salt; they take back <em>Teak-wood</em> and coco-nuts. From <em>Rája-puram</em>, a town in the <em>Marattah</em> part of <em>Kankána</em>, vessels (<em>Patemars</em>) bring the same kinds of grain that are brought from Bombay, and also sugar-cane, <em>Jagory</em>, and <em>Cut</em>, or <em>Terra Japonica</em>: they take away the same returns. From <em>Gheria</em>, in the same country, are brought much <em>Jagory</em> and <em>Cut</em>, and coco-nuts are taken in return. Goa sends the same kind <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-445" data-page="445" data-folio="421" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n444_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 421">[p. 421]</button> of goods that are brought from Bombay. Much rice is exported from hence to the northern parts of the province of <em>Malabar</em>. There is no trade between <em>Panyáni</em> and the <em>Maldives</em>. From <em>Cochin</em> are brought canoes, spices, sugar, sugar-cane, <em>Jagory</em>, wheat, and mustard-seed; and the returns are iron smelted in the interior parts of the country, and rice both rough and freed from the husk. From <em>Anjengo</em> are brought cotton cloths wrought there, and coco-nuts. No account is kept here of the arrivals or departures of vessels (<em>Patemars</em>); but in the custom-house books every article exported or imported ought to be entered. The returns of these, which I expected from Mr. Drummond the collector, have not reached me.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Moplays.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 14.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Panyáni</em> is the residence of the <em>Tangul</em>, or chief priest of the <em>Moplays</em>, who says that he is descended from <em>Ali</em> and <em>Fatima</em>, the daughter of <em>Mahomet</em>. Both the <em>Tangul</em>, and his sister’s son, who according to the custom of <em>Malayala</em> is considered as the heir to this hereditary dignity, are very stout, handsome, fair men, but from their countenances would not be suspected to belong to the priesthood. The nephew is a middle-aged man, and at the jollity of a marriage, a few days ago, exerted himself so much, that he burst a blood vessel in his lungs, and could not venture to speak. The <em>Tangul</em> was remarkably civil, and, when I returned his visit in the evening, received me with great hospitality, and requested me to eat with him; a thing very uncommon with the natives of India. He promised to send me an account of the arrival of his sect in this country, and has kept his promise. It is written in <em>Arabic</em>, and is said to be the original from which <em>Ferishta</em> translated the account of this colony that is given in his works. The <em>Tangul</em> says, that his people are called <em>Moplaymar</em> in <em>Malayala</em>; and <em>Lubbaymar</em> at Madras; but among themselves they acknowledge no other name than that of Mussulmans. Being of Arabic extraction, they look upon themselves as of a more honourable birth than the Tartar Mussulmans from the north of India, who of course are of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-446" data-page="446" data-folio="422" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n445_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 422">[p. 422]</button> contrary opinion. The Arabs settled in India soon after the promulgation of the faith of Mahomet, and have made very numerous converts; but in many families of distinction the Arab blood seems as yet uncontaminated. They use a written character peculiar to themselves, and totally different from the present Arabic. The language of their original country is known to few of them, except their priests; and they have never acquired the language of the country in which they live so as to speak it in decent purity, but use a jargon as corrupted as what Europeans in general speak for <em>Hindustany</em>. The <em>Moplays</em> of <em>Malabar</em> are both traders and farmers; the <em>Lubbaymars</em> of Madras confine themselves entirely to the former profession. As traders, they are remarkably quiet, industrious people; but those who in the interior parts of <em>Malabar</em> have become farmers, having been encouraged by <em>Tippoo</em> in a most licentious attack on the lives, persons, and property of the <em>Hindus</em>, are fierce, blood-thirsty, bigoted ruffians. In religious matters, the <em>Tangul</em> is the head of this sect, and his office is hereditary. Mosques are very numerous. In each presides an <em>Imám</em>, or <em>Mulla</em>, appointed by the <em>Tangul</em>. He usually bestows the office on the sister’s son, or heir of the person who last enjoyed the office, unless he should happen to be disqualified by ignorance, or immorality. The <em>Tangul</em> has some lands, for which he pays no tax; but the inferior clergy are supported entirely by the contributions of their followers. The late <em>Sultán</em>, who wished to make innovations in every thing, did not respect this descendant of his prophet; but appointed another head for the priests of his faith in <em>Malabar</em>. This person, called <em>Arabi Tangul</em>, resides at <em>Panyáni</em>; but his followers are now reduced to five or six families, and he has lost one half of the property that <em>Tippoo</em> bestowed on his new favourite.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 15. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">15th <em>December</em>.—I went a long stage to <em>Adanad</em>. The country between <em>Panyáni</em> and <em>Ternavay</em>, although higher than the sea-shore, is level; and consists entirely of rice-grounds, which annually produce only one crop, and of which a great part seems to be waste.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-447"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="447" data-folio="423" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n446_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 423">[p. 423]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 15. <em>Panyáni</em> river.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On leaving the sea-coast, the number of trees, especially of coco-nut palms, decreases fast. I crossed the <em>Panyáni</em> river at <em>Ternavay</em>, where there is a small temple, but no town. The channel of the river is very wide; but at this season most of it is occupied by dry sands. The water is clear, and the stream gentle; the fords are, however, bad, owing to the depth of water, which in most parts is four feet, and no where less than three. Cattle, in crossing it, must therefore be unloaded, and the baggage carried to the other side by the drivers. This river in the rainy season is navigable for canoes almost up to <em>Pali-ghat</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After crossing this river, I came to a country like that near the <em>Nazareny</em> town in the <em>Cochi Rájá's</em> dominions, and consisting of narrow vallies surrounded by low bare hills. The vallies produce annually two crops of rice; each having a perennial stream, that is applied to the irrigation of the soil. The roots of the hills are occupied by the houses and plantations of the natives; and their sides in many places have been formed into terraces; but these are very badly cultivated, considering the abundance of rain in this country, which will ensure plenty of water for any crop that does not require more than four months to come to maturity. The soil, in many places of these hills, is very intractable, and consists of a kind of indurated clay, which, on exposure to the air, becomes as hard as a brick, and serves indeed all the purposes of stone.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Namburi Bráhmans.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 15.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Adanad</em> is no town, but is celebrated as the throne of the <em>Alvangheri Tamburacul</em>, or chief of the <em>Namburis</em>, who are the <em>Bráhmans</em> of <em>Malayala</em>. Soon after my arrival I sent a message, by a <em>Bráhmán</em>, to know, whether it would be most agreeable to this person to receive a visit from me, or for him to come to my tent. The answer was, that he would be very happy to see me whenever I was ready. My politeness was lost on the <em>Bráhman</em>, who kept me waiting in an outer apartment until my patience was exhausted, and I returned to my tents without the honour of an interview. I then sent to him an order from the government of Madras, commanding all <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-448" data-page="448" data-folio="424" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n447_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 424">[p. 424]</button> persons to give me such information as I wanted, and desired him to come to my tent. This was complied with, and he came attended by several <em>Namburis</em>. The <em>Alvangheri Tamburacul</em> having been seated on a chair, which he took care should be higher than mine, I soon discovered that he was an idiot, who grinned with a foolish laugh when the most serious questions were proposed to him. His attendants, however, were men of good sense, and apparently well informed; and from them the following account is taken.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 15.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 15.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The present <em>Tamburacul</em> is descended in the male line from the <em>Bráhman</em> who was appointed to that high dignity by <em>Parasu-ráma</em>, when he created <em>Malayala</em> and gave it to the <em>Namburis</em>. When a <em>Tamburacul</em> is likely to die without male children, he adopts a male of the same family, and appoints him successor; but, if he have sons, the eldest succeeds of course. <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, about 1000 years ago, came to <em>Malabar</em>, and made some reforms in the discipline of the <em>Bráhmans</em>; but the then <em>Tamburacul</em> was far from acknowledging the superiority of that personage, and the present one considers himself as much higher in dignity than the <em>Sringa-giri Swami</em>, who is the successor of <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, and chief of the <em>Smartal Bráhmans</em>. The <em>Tamuri Rájá</em>, as I have already mentioned, affected to consider himself as inferior only to the invisible gods; but this pretension is treated with the utmost contempt by the <em>Namburis</em>, the lowest of whom is of a much higher birth than any prince on earth. This high opinion of themselves is attributed to the power that they have of influencing the gods by their invocations (<em>Mantrams</em>), especially to the power which they have, by means of certain forms of prayer, of rendering an image the residence of a god. The <em>Namburis</em> pretend, that while this country was governed by princes appointed by the <em>Sholun Rájás</em>, these viceroys were entirely subject to the <em>Alvangheri Tamburaculs</em>, and did nothing more than, by means of the civil arm, carry their orders into execution. When the office of <em>Rájá</em> came to be hereditary, by the appointment of <em>Cheruman Permal</em>, the <em>Tamburacul</em> still pretended to have a right to <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-449" data-page="449" data-folio="425" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n448_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 425">[p. 425]</button> dispose of the government; but his power was confined to the performance of a ceremony called <em>Putapayshacum</em>, which is somewhat analogous to the anointing that our kings use. On this occasion, the <em>Tamburacul</em> and his <em>Namburis</em> received much <em>Dána</em>, and other charities; but they had no authority to reject the next heir. All the <em>Rájás</em>, except the <em>Velat</em> family, had, for many generations before the conquest, given up the ceremony of <em>Putapayshacum</em>. The <em>Rájás</em> possessed no authority to punish any <em>Namburi</em>, farther than, in case of some very atrocious crime, to banish him from their dominions. The <em>Namburis</em> were subject to the jurisdiction of the <em>Alvangheri</em>, who in his judgments was always assisted by a council of learned men, and guided by the <em>Hindu</em> law. The book that they consult on this subject is the <em>Asocha Prayaschittum</em>, composed by <em>Véda Vyása</em>, one of the gods, who assumed the form of a <em>Rishi</em>, and was also the author of the eighteen <em>Puránas</em>. The laws of <em>Menu</em> seem to be totally unknown to the <em>Namburis</em>, who all pretend to be <em>Vaidikas</em>, nor do any of them follow lay professions. Few of them, however, are men of learning. The only book on astronomical subjects that those here could mention was the <em>Jotis Sástram</em>, which, from their account, is a work on astrology. They will neither eat nor drink with the <em>Bráhmans</em> of other countries, whom they call <em>Puttar</em>, and whom they consider as very inferior to themselves in dignity. The others are equally proud; and these allege, that <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, in consequence of their disobedience, cursed the <em>Namburis</em>, and degraded them below the faithful <em>Bráhmans</em>, who adhered to his council. The <em>Namburis</em>, like other <em>Bráhmans</em>, marry, and live with their wives, of whom they take as many as they are able to support. A <em>Namburi</em>’s children are also considered as his heirs. They do not lose cast on account of fornication with a <em>Súdra</em> woman; and indeed, in order to prevent themselves from losing dignity by becoming too numerous, the younger sons of a <em>Namburi</em> family seldom marry. They live with the elder brother, and assist the ladies of the <em>Rájás</em>, and of the <em>Nairs</em> of distinction, to keep up their families; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-450" data-page="450" data-folio="426" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n449_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 426">[p. 426]</button> and in general they are the most favoured lovers, the young women of rank and beauty seldom admitting any person to their bed, but a <em>Bráhman</em>, and more especially a <em>Namburi</em>. A <em>Namburi</em> woman loses cast for infidelity, even if the crime has been committed with a <em>Namburi</em>. Many <em>Namburis</em> have lost cast by having committed murder, or by having eaten forbidden things. In such cases, their children have in general become Mussulmans. The <em>Namburis</em> eat no kind of animal food, and drink no spirituous liquors. They burn the dead, but a widow is not expected to perish on the funeral pile with the body of her husband. The <em>Namburis</em>, like the <em>Smartal</em>, allege, that <em>Siva</em>, <em>Brahmá</em>, and <em>Vishnu</em> are the same god; and most of them, like the <em>Smartal</em>, wear the mark of <em>Siva</em>; but the <em>Alvangheri Tamburacul</em> uses the mark of <em>Vishnu</em>. They are not too proud to be <em>Pújáris</em>, or priests, in even the temples of the <em>Saktis</em>; a circumstance that the <em>Bráhmans</em> of the East do not fail to mention, in order to render their rivals contemptible.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Persecution of the <em>Namburis</em> by <em>Tippoo</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On the accession of <em>Tippoo</em>, the <em>Namburis</em> met with much trouble; and many of them were caught and circumcised. Those, who could escape fled to <em>Travancore</em>. It was three years after the Company obtained possession of <em>Malabar</em>, before the <em>Alvangheri Tamburacul</em> would return to this his proper residence. The <em>Matam</em> is now rebuilt, and a throne is erecting for his seat. The Company allow 25,000 <em>Rupees</em> a year for the <em>Namburis</em> who officiate in the temples.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Nambuddies.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 15.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Every <em>Namburi</em> who stains his hands with blood ought to become an outcast; but an exception was made in favour of <em>Putter</em>, and his companions, who undertook to assassinate <em>Sholun Permal</em>, as I have already mentioned. Before he departed on this enterprize, the <em>Namburis</em> promised, that, in consideration of the laudable intention with which the deed was undertaken, the law should not be enforced against men who were acting for the good of a cast so favoured by the gods. After <em>Putter</em> and his companions, however, had murdered the unsuspecting prince, and had made their escape to the tank where the <em>Bráhmans</em> were performing their devotions, they <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-451" data-page="451" data-folio="427" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n450_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 427">[p. 427]</button> became struck with horror, and, sitting down on the steps, exclaimed, “How can we with our bloody hands approach such pure beings!” The <em>Bráhmans</em> replied, that, in consequence of the promises which had been made, if they had come down they must have been received; but, as they had chosen to sit at a distance, conscious of their impurity, they must ever afterwards be considered as inferior to the <em>Namburis</em>. The descendants of these persons are to this day called <em>Nambuddy</em>, or <em>sitting on steps</em>, and are considered by the <em>Namburis</em> as not much higher in rank than <em>Rájás</em>, or other princes.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 16. <em>Tritalay.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">16th December.—I went to <em>Tritalay</em>, a small market (<em>Bazar</em>) of 40 or 50 houses, situated on the south bank of the river. It is inhabited by <em>Hindus</em>, brought by <em>Tippoo</em> from the country to the eastward, with a view of accommodating travellers by keeping shops. This is a business to which the original inhabitants of <em>Malayala</em> have a great dislike. The place is situated in the great route between <em>Pali-ghat</em> on one hand, and <em>Calicut</em> and <em>Panyáni</em> on the other. It is, of course, a very great thoroughfare; but the roads are exceedingly bad, or, rather, there is no road whatever. The country through which I passed consists of innumerable low hills, divided from each other by narrow vallies, which indeed is the case almost every where in <em>Malayala</em>, or the <em>hilly country</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 17. Fears of the natives.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">17th <em>December.</em>—I remained at <em>Tritalay</em>, endeavouring to obtain an account of the agriculture and produce of the neighbourhood; but found a great difficulty from the fears of the natives, who consider every inquiry as being made with a view of increasing their burthens, and therefore wish to make their condition appear as poor as they can.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Weather in <em>Malabar.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The most intelligent farmers here give me the following account of the weather.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Canni</em> (14th September—14th October) they have strong winds from the westward, with a considerable quantity of rain, and much thunder.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-452"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="452" data-folio="428" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n451_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 428">[p. 428]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. <em>Dec.</em> 17.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <em>Tulam</em> (15th October—13th November) the westerly winds generally continue; but the rains abate, and come once only in four or five days. They are accompanied by much thunder.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Vrichica</em> (14th November—12th December), or sometimes in <em>Tulam</em>, the winds change to the eastward, and blow strong through the <em>Ani-malaya</em> passage. Three or four times in the course of this month there comes heavy rain from the eastward. By the natives, the air is reckoned very cold. To my feelings, the days were very hot, but the nights cool and pleasant. The cool air of the night, however, is apt to produce, on those who sleep exposed to its influence, a disease named <em>Vatum</em>. In this, the legs are drawn up to the buttocks, and become stiff and emaciated; and, if the patient escape with life, he never recovers the full use of his limbs. The disease, from the accounts of the natives, seems to be a violent rheumatism followed by palsy; I have, however, had no opportunity of tracing its progress.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Danu</em> (13th December—11th January) there are pretty strong winds from the south, and the air is still colder. These winds also produce the <em>Vatum</em>. All this month there are strong fogs and dews, but seldom rain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Macara</em> (12th January—9th February) there is no rain, and less fog than before; but the dews continue heavy. The winds are easterly and strong, and the weather is cool. The <em>Jack</em> fruit, called <em>Chaca</em> by the natives (<em>Artocarpus integrifolia</em>), is ripe, which is about six weeks earlier than at <em>Calcutta</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Cumbha</em> (10th February—11th March) there are very strong easterly winds, but no rain, and very slight dews. The weather begins to get hot. <em>Mangoes</em> are in season.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Mina</em> (12th March—10th April) there is very seldom any rain, and most of the rivulets become dry. The weather is hot, with slight breezes from the eastward. <em>Mangoes</em> continue in season.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 17.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <em>Mayda</em> (11th April—11th May) the winds change to the westward, and there are four or five heavy showers, which are <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-453" data-page="453" data-folio="429" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n452_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 429">[p. 429]</button> accompanied by thunder, and generally fall at night. The heat is great. This is the commencement of the ploughing season.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Ayduma</em>, or as it is also called <em>Vrishuppa</em> (12th May—11th June), the winds are westerly, and not strong. Moderate rains for the first half of the month, and these are sometimes accompanied by hail. The heat abates considerably. Toward the end of the month the rains become very heavy, and are accompanied by much thunder.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Maytuna</em> (12th June—13th July) the rains increase, with strong westerly winds, and much thunder: the heat is moderate.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Carcataca</em> (14th July—13th August) there is less thunder; but the westerly winds, and the rains, increase in violence. There is seldom a fair day, or even any considerable intermission from rain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Singhium</em> (14th August—13th September) the rains and wind somewhat abate, and the thunder is moderate.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Parumba</em> lands formed into terraces.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The low hills occupy a very large proportion of the country, and are clear from woods. Their sides are formed into terraces for the cultivation of hill-rice, <em>Ellu</em> (<em>Sesamum</em>), and <em>Shamay</em> (<em>Panicum miliare</em> E. M.). The violence of the rain is such, that it would sweep away any thing which was sown on a sloping surface; and it is merely to prevent this, that the terraces have been formed. They are seldom so level, however, as to enable the cultivator to confine the rain, and inundate their surface. The whole that can be cultivated has been divided into terraces; but that in a very slovenly manner, very different indeed from the hills in China. From the same field a good crop can be had once only in five years. This kind of land is here called <em>Malaya</em>, or hill; and is partly the property of the government, and partly that of the landlords (<em>Jenmcars</em>). That belonging to government is cultivated by the neighbouring farmers, rent free; that belonging to the private landlords pays them one fourth of the produce.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Produce and value of <em>Paddum</em> land.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 17.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Dhanmurry</em>, or <em>Paddum</em>, or low land, besides the tax to government, pays to the proprietor from one to four <em>Porays</em> of rough rice <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-454" data-page="454" data-folio="430" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n453_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 430">[p. 430]</button> for every <em>Poray candum</em>. If a <em>Poray candum</em> pay four <em>Porays</em> to the proprietor, it is called a four <em>Patom</em> land; if it pay three <em>Porays</em>, it is called three <em>Patom</em> land; and so on. The two highest kinds of land produce two crops in the year, the others produce only one. The land-tax is in the proportion of 1¼ <em>Vir’-Ráya Fanam</em> for every <em>Patom</em> rent. Thus four <em>Patom-land</em> pays five <em>Fanams</em> land-tax, which is at the rate of 20<em>s.</em> 5<em>d.</em> an acre. The remainder left to the proprietor is at the rate of 16<em>s.</em> 3<em>d.</em> The worst land pays at the rate of one-fourth of the best. The people at first would not acknowledge that the best land produced more than ten <em>Porays</em> upon one <em>Poray candum</em>; but, by putting a number of questions to them, of which they could not perceive the tendency, they were soon induced to confess, that they had concealed the truth. The common interest of money is 12 per cent. per annum; but as money lent on mortgage (<em>Canum</em>) is perfectly secure, four <em>Porays</em> of rough rice are reckoned an adequate interest for 100 <em>Vir’-Ráya Fanams</em> advanced on mortgage. If the farmer (<em>Cudian</em>), therefore, as usual here, advance 100 <em>Fanams</em> on a <em>Poray candum</em> of the best land, the interest of the money is equal to the rent (<em>Patom</em>), and the landlord (<em>Jenmcar</em>) has no right to any thing, but a bunch of plantains, or some such trifle, as an acknowledgment of tenure: but it is customary, on account of the high rank of the landlord, for the farmer to give him, as a mark of respect, a small quantity of grain. On this account, on a <em>Poray candum</em> of the best quality, eight-tenths of a <em>Poray</em> of rough rice are usually given. If Mr. Drummond be right in his estimate of the extent of a <em>Poray-candum</em>, this will make the produce of an acre in the first crop 25 bushels, and in the second about 17 bushels ; and on each crop will leave a profit to the mortgagee of about 1 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub> bushel. Reasoning on the same <em>data</em>, which cannot well be erroneous, the produce of the one crop on the worst land must be 5 <sup>6</sup>/<sub>10</sub> <em>Porays</em> from a <em>Poray-candum</em>, which will give about 13 bushels an acre. During <em>Tippoo's</em> government almost the whole of the landlords (<em>Jenmcars</em>) fled out of the province, and emigrated to avoid persecution. They have now returned, and are in nominal possession of their estates ; but as most of these have been alienated on full mortgage (<em>Canum</em>), they receive but a very small share of the produce.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote is-locus">Plantations.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this part of the country there are few coco-nut palms, the produce being too bulky for being carried to the sea side for exportation. The palms that are planted round the houses of the natives are chiefly <em>Betel-nut</em> (<em>Areca catechu</em>) ; and these are intermixed with <em>Jack</em>, <em>Mango</em>, orange, lime, and plantain trees. The ground that is applied to the raising of these plantations is the best of what is called <em>Parumba</em> ; and, when a tenant (<em>Cudian</em>) pays the land-tax, and advances 25 <em>Fanams</em> on mortgage, for a <em>Poray-candum</em>, he is not expected to give any rent to the landlord (<em>Jenmcar</em>). A <em>Poray-candum</em> therefore of this land is worth to the landholder 1 <em>Poray</em> of rough rice a year, or about 2 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> bushels an acre.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-456"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="456" data-folio="432" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n455_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 432">[p. 432]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 18. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">18th <em>December</em>.—After crossing the river about a mile above <em>Tritalay</em>, I went a long stage to <em>Cherupalchery</em>, which was the residence of the superintendant of the southern division of <em>Malabar</em>, while that office existed. Several good houses, or rather cottages, remain at the place as a monument, but there is no town nor shop. On this day's route the quantity of hill-ground is very great, and but a very small proportion of it is cultivated. Some of it has so gentle a slope, that it admits of being cultivated without being formed into terraces. To judge from the thickness of the grass, one would think that this ground was much more fertile than that of <em>Coimbetore</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Tamari Rájás.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Invasion by Hyder.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 18. No names of rivers in <em>Malabar</em>. Another account of the weather.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Cherupalchery</em> is situated in a district called <em>Nedunga nadu</em>, which formed a part of the <em>Tamuri Rájá's</em> dominions. The <em>Tamuri</em>, although of a cast inferior to the <em>Cochi Rájá</em>, and although possessed of less extensive dominions, was commonly reckoned of equal rank; which is said to have been owing to the superior prowess of his people. This produced a confidence in themselves, which, when <em>Hyder</em> invaded the country, proved ruinous. The <em>Cochi Rájá</em> quietly submitted to pay a tribute, and still enjoys the government of his country; while the pride of the <em>Tamuri</em> refused any kind of submission to <em>Hyder</em>, and now he is reduced to a cypher, supported by the bounty of the Company. <em>Hyder</em> in person invaded the country, but was soon afterwards called away by a war in the dominions of <em>Arcot</em>. The <em>Rájás</em> embraced this opportunity, and, having repossessed themselves, held their lands for seven years. A <em>Bráhman</em> named <em>Chinavas Row</em> was then sent against them, and drove them into the dominions of <em>Travancore</em>. After nine years of his administration, an English army came, and took <em>Pali-ghat</em>; but, on the approach of <em>Tippoo</em>, was obliged to retreat by <em>Panyáni</em>. The <em>Rájás</em> continued in exile until 1790; when, a little before the battle of <em>Tiruvana Angady</em>, they joined Colonel Hartly with 5000 <em>Nairs</em>. The second personage of the <em>Tamuri's</em> family now resides at <em>Carimporay</em>, a <em>Colgum</em>, or palace, that is situated west from <em>Cherupalchery</em>, on the banks of the river. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-457" data-page="457" data-folio="433" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n456_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 433">[p. 433]</button> It must be observed, that in <em>Malabar</em> no river has any peculiar appellation; but each portion is called by the name of the most remarkable place near which it flows.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A <em>Vaidika Bráhman</em> gives me the following account of the weather here, which may be compared with that of the farmers that I have before detailed. This account is taken from a <em>Sanskrit</em> work composed by the serpent <em>Subhramani</em>, and illustrated by a commentary of <em>Sankara Achárya</em>. The year is, as usual, divided into six <em>Ritus</em>, or seasons.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The first, containing <em>Macara</em> and <em>Cumbha</em> (12th January—11th March), is called <em>Sayshu Ritu</em>. In this the prevailing winds are easterly and northerly, and are not strong. There is no rain. The old leaves fall from the trees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The second, containing <em>Mina</em> and <em>Mayda</em> (12th March—11th May), is called <em>Vasanta Ritu</em>. The weather is hot, with light winds from the westward, and a few showers of rain. The new leaves come out on the trees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The third, containing <em>Ayduma</em> and <em>Maytuna</em> (12th May—13th July), is called <em>Gríshma Ritu</em>. There are now thunder, wind, and rain; which, being all united together, though not very severe, make a great tumult in the air.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The fourth, containing <em>Carcataca</em> and <em>Singhium</em> (14th July—13th September), is called <em>Varshá Ritu</em>. In this the thunder, wind, and rain are very severe.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The fifth, containing <em>Canni</em> and <em>Tulam</em> (14th September—13th November), is called <em>Sarat Ritu</em>. In this, rain comes both from the east and from the west. The winds are easterly.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The sixth, containing <em>Vrichica</em> and <em>Danu</em> (14th November—11th January), is called <em>Hémanta Ritu</em>. In this there are heavy dews, but no rain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 18.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The first three <em>Ritus</em> form <em>Utráyana</em>; in which the day-winds are easterly, and the night-winds westerly; the latter of which are the strongest. The last three <em>Ritus</em> form <em>Dakshanáyana</em>, in which the day-winds are westerly, and the night-winds easterly and the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-458" data-page="458" data-folio="434" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n457_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 434">[p. 434]</button> strongest. From this it would appear, that on shore the sea and land winds in some degree overcome even the violence of the monsoon; but at sea, near the coast, this is not observable during the strength of the south-west monsoon; at other seasons it is well known to seamen.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 19. Roads.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">19th <em>December.</em>—I went about nine miles to <em>Angada-puram</em>, having crossed a fine little river, a branch of that which falls into the sea at <em>Panyáni</em>. The low rice-fields seem to occupy but a small proportion of the country. The roads are very bad; but Mr. Wye, the collector, has lately obtained leave to lay out on their repair a small revenue, the produce of some ferries. Although the sum is small, yet it will have a considerable effect in a country where the soil is in general favourable, and where there are no carriages. In <em>Malabar</em> even cattle are little used for the transportation of goods, which are generally carried by porters. <em>Angada-puram</em>, by Europeans commonly written <em>Angrypar</em>, is at present a military station, the troops being in cantonments at some distance from the old fort. The situation is very pleasant, and many camp followers, and traders from <em>Coimbetore</em>, having settled shops (<em>Bazars</em>), have been the means of introducing many conveniences that are not commonly to be found in the inner parts of <em>Malabar</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">20th and 21st <em>December.</em>—I remained with Mr. Wye, from whom, in making my inquiries, I received every possible attention and assistance. I have also received from him very satisfactory answers to the queries which I proposed in writing to the Commissioners, and of which I shall here avail myself.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Barren lands in Mr. Wye's circle.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Wye has the collection of four districts, namely <em>Bettutanada</em>, and <em>Parupa-nada</em>, on the sea coast; and <em>Vellater</em>, and <em>Shirnada</em>, toward the <em>Ghats</em>. Of the last two districts, Mr. Wye thinks that one half is too steep, rocky, or barren for cultivation. He estimates a third of <em>Bettutanada</em>, and a fourth of <em>Parupa-nada</em> to be of the same nature.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Hills between <em>Malabar</em> and <em>Coimbetore</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Besides these districts, there is a tract of land occupying part of the mountains which separate <em>Malabar</em> from <em>Coimbetore</em>. The <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-459" data-page="459" data-folio="435" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n458_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 435">[p. 435]</button> <em>Namburis</em> or <em>Nairs</em> had no authority over its inhabitants, who speak the language of <em>Karnáta</em>. It is divided into two districts, <em>Attapadi</em>, and <em>Ayrata Cadawa</em>, each subject to a <em>Gauda</em>, or hereditary chief. The pass leading up to <em>Attapadi</em> goes by <em>Manar-ghat</em>, which was subject to the <em>Tamuri</em>, as chief of a district called <em>Nerunganada</em>; and the pass leading up to <em>Ayrata Cadawa</em> was named <em>Cherumbil</em>, and subject to the <em>Rájá</em> of <em>Velater</em>. Each <em>Rájá</em> took advantage of the hill chief, who could only have access to the commerce of the low country through his dominions, and forced him to pay a tribute for permission to trade. This tribute, for both chiefs, amounts to 1000 <em>Rupees</em>. The manner in which these chiefs manage their country, or raise the revenue, is here totally unknown; as the natives seldom venture up to the hills, on account of the unhealthiness of their air. The <em>Cherumbil</em> pass was reckoned the best; but, owing to the disturbances prevailing in the country, it has of late been neglected, and is now overgrown with trees. It might be cleared at the expense of three or four hundred <em>Rupees</em>. From these hilly districts there are roads, that lead to <em>Dan'-Náyakana Cotay</em>, and <em>Coimbetore</em>; and it would be of great importance to commerce to have these roads cleared, as also the passes which lead up from the <em>Irnada</em> district, in <em>Malabar</em>, to the southern parts of <em>Mysore</em>. For their respective productions, the two countries have a mutual demand, which at present is chiefly accommodated by the circuitous route of <em>Coimbetore</em>, and <em>Pali-ghat</em>; but, if direct roads were opened through the passes in the mountains, we might expect, says Mr. Wye, “that towns would spring up at the foot of every pass; that the customs would increase; and that small <em>Bazars</em> (towns containing shops), so much wanted, would be established on the different routes between the passes and the towns on the sea coast. The <em>Moplays</em> of the inland country, hitherto a most troublesome race of men, would, like their brethren on the sea coast, turn their attention to commerce, and procure a field of exertion for their restless spirit, which now so often interrupts the tranquillity of the country.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Forests.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The forests in every part of <em>Malabar</em> would appear to be private <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-460" data-page="460" data-folio="436" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n459_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 436">[p. 436]</button> property. A person who wants to cut timber must first apply to the landlord (<em>Jenmcar</em>) for permission; which is granted in a writing called <em>Cuticanum</em>, in which is specified the price that is to be paid for each tree. This varies, according to the distance of the trees from water carriage, from two to eight <em>Fanams</em> for a <em>Teak</em> tree, from one to two <em>Fanams</em> for a <em>Viti</em>, or black-wood tree (<em>Pterocarpus</em>), and from one to four <em>Fanams</em> for an <em>Aiony</em> tree (<em>Artocarpus hirsuta En: Meth:</em>): these are the only trees for which the landlords demand a price; but there are two others reckoned valuable; the <em>Vayntayca</em>, which resists the white ant; and the <em>Trimbucum</em>, an iron-wood, which belongs to the genus that Dr. Roxburgh in his MSS. calls <em>Hopea</em>. After the bargain has been made, a small advance is given, and the wood-cutter goes and fells whatever trees he wants. When he is ready to take them away, he informs the landlord, who numbers those that have been cut, and, before he allows one to be moved, receives the full value. The quantity of <em>Teak</em> trees annually produced in this circle does not, in Mr. Wye's opinion, exceed a hundred. This valuable tree grows chiefly about <em>Manar-ghat</em>, and is therefore too remote from a navigable river to be carried for a market to the sea coast.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No lac nor sandal-wood is produced in the hills of <em>Malabar</em>; at least, the few trees of sandal that may be found are devoid of smell.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Iron mines.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <em>Velater</em> there are 34 forges for smelting iron. This mixture forms small angular nodules closely compacted together, and very friable. It is dug out with a pick-ax, and broken into powder with the same instrument. It is then washed in a wooden trough, about four feet in length, open at both ends, and placed in the current of a rivulet; so that a gentle stream of water runs constantly through it. The powdered ore is placed in the upper end of this trough; and as the water passes through the heap, a man continually stirs it about with his hand. The metallic sand remains in the upper end of the trough, the quartz is carried to the lower end, and the clay is suspended in the water, and washed entirely away. The <em>Moplay</em> in general collects the ore by means of his own slaves. At other times, he buys it ready washed for the furnace; and then what he puts in one furnace costs him 10 <em>Fanams</em>. Each smelting requires 2160 lb.; the price, therefore, is not quite 3½<em>d.</em> the hundred-weight. In this ore the quantity of metallic sand is small, in comparison with that of the earthy matter.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Furnace.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Figures 52, 53, 54. The furnaces are excavated out of the front of a mound of clay, which is 4 feet high behind, and 5 feet four inches before; and about 7 feet wide, from front to back. The excavation made for each furnace is 2 feet 11 inches wide, and 2 feet deep; and is dug down from the top of the mound to the ground. From behind, opposite to each furnace, an arched cavity is dug into the mound; so as to leave a thin partition between the two excavations. For allowing the vitrified matter to run off, there is in this partition a hole one foot in diameter. Above the furnace is erected a chimney of clay, built with four plain sides, which in two different places is strengthened by four <em>Bamboos</em>, lashed together at the angles. The front of the chimney consists of baked clay, two inches in thickness. Behind, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-464" data-page="464" data-folio="438" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n463_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 438">[p. 438]</button> the clay is gradually thickened toward the summit; so that the upper mouth of the chimney is contracted to 8 inches in depth by 2 feet 11 inches in width. The front of the furnace is quite open.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Smelting.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Early in the morning, when going to smelt, the workmen put wet sand mixed with powdered charcoal into the bottom of the furnace; so as to fill it up as far as the hole in its back part, through which the vitrified matter is to run out. The sand and charcoal are well beaten, and formed so as to slope from the outer and upper edge, both toward the hole and toward the ground in front of the furnace. The hole is then well stopped with clay; and clay pipes are inserted at each corner of the furnace, for the reception of the muzzles of the bellows. A row of clay pipes, eight or ten in number, is then laid on the surface of the sand, at right angles to the back of the furnace. Their outer ends project a little beyond the front, and their inner ends reach about half way to the back. The front of the furnace is then shut up with moist clay; and stoppers of the same are put in the outer mouths of the pipes. By removing these stoppers, and looking through the pipes, the workmen judge how the operation is going forward. Ten baskets of charcoal, each weighing 63 lb., are then poured in by the chimney; and this having been kindled, the bellows are set to work. Then 16 <em>Porays</em> of prepared ore, weighing 2160 lb., and 20 baskets more of charcoal, as the fire makes room for them, are gradually added. The operation lasts 24 hours, two sets of men relieving each other at the bellows, and keeping up a constant blast. The principal workman who attends the fire adds the fewel and ore, and stops up breaches; and, when the mass of iron has formed, breaks the clay that shut up the hole in the back part of the furnace, and lets out much vitrified matter, that strongly resembles brown <em>hæmatites</em>, and no doubt contains much iron, which this imperfect operation is unable to reduce. The bellows are then removed, and the front of the furnace is broken down. A great part of the charcoal which has not been consumed is then pulled out with sticks or forks, and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-465" data-page="465" data-folio="439" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n464_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 439">[p. 439]</button> extinguished by water. The mass of iron is allowed to remain on the sand 24 hours, and to cool gradually. According to the success of the operation, it weighs from 8 to 12 <em>Tolams</em>, or from 256 to 384 lb. The mass, when cool, is broken in pieces with a large hammer, and sold for use, it being then malleable, although somewhat brittle. The mass is extremely porous, and irregular in its shape, and has never formed what chemists call a button; that is to say, the liquefaction produced on the iron has only been partial, sufficient to cause the particles to adhere in a mass, but not adequate to form a fluid that expels all matters of a different specific gravity. In fact, the mass, in its cavities, includes many pieces of charcoal enveloped by the iron. How these have not been consumed, I do not know; but this circumstance clearly shows, that combustible matter being contained in a <em>stratum</em> is no proof, that the particles of this have not been united by a fire capable of mollifying them, and of making them cohere.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Want of proper bellows.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I have already mentioned, that this process obtains only from 11 <sup>8</sup>/<sub>10</sub> to 17 <sup>8</sup>/<sub>10</sub> per cent. of iron from the ore, and that what is produced is very imperfect. Each man works a pair, consisting of two cylindrical leather bags, about 18 inches high, and 9 inches in diameter. The top has a slit, the edges of which overlap, and serve as a valve. Each pair is placed, on a small platform of clay, at a corner of the furnace; and a man, taking hold of the outer flaps of their upper ends in his two hands, alternately pushes them down to expel the wind, and draws them up to get a supply of air, the one hand going up while the other goes down. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">What I have called indurated clay is not the mineral so called by Mr. Kirwan, who has not described this of which I am now writing. It seems to be the <em>Argilla lapidea</em> of Wallerius I. 395, and is one of the most valuable materials for building. It is diffused in immense masses, without any appearance of stratification, and is placed over the granite that forms the basis of <em>Malayala</em>. It is full of cavities and pores, and contains a very large quantity of iron in the form of red and yellow ochres. In the mass, while excluded from the air, it is so soft, that any iron instrument readily cuts it, and is dug up in square masses with a pick-ax, and immediately cut into the shape wanted with a trowel, or large knife. I have never observed any animal or vegetable <em>exuvia</em> contained in it, but I have heard <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-467" data-page="467" data-folio="441" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n466_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 441">[p. 441]</button> that such have been found immersed in its substance. Value of rice estates.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">With regard to the <em>Porays</em> of land mentioned in the revenue accompts, a most fallacious opinion has been entertained, that they are so much land as will sow a <em>Poray</em> of seed, and this is defined to be <em>32</em> cubits square, which is still smaller than the allowance made by Mr. Warden. The fact at <em>Angada-puram</em> is, that, when the assessment was made by <em>Arshid Beg Khán</em>, so much land, good or bad, was called a <em>Poray</em> of land, as was supposed to produce to the landlord (<em>Jenmcar</em>) 10 <em>Porays</em> of <em>Vir'-Patom</em>, or of neat rent. The tax imposed on this was <em>5 Fanams</em>, which, at harvest, is nearly the value of the whole rent; so that, unless the proprietor reserved the grain for a favourable market, he had no profit left him from his rice-lands. This, the people say, has been actually the case; but as people are still willing to advance money in mortgage on rice-lands, we may safely conclude, that <em>Hyder</em> did not so far deviate from his usual policy and justice, as to lay on a tax that would entirely absorb the property of the subject. It is true, that the inhabitants of <em>Malabar</em> speak of <em>Hyder</em> as of a rapacious tyrant; but little attention can be paid to what such people say, as they are universally discontented with the government of the English, by whom they have been indulged like sick children. To illustrate the matter more fully, let us consider what is usually done, according to the acknowledgment of the natives. The lower parts of the vallies are called <em>Ubayum</em> lands; but the whole does not produce two crops. When\n the seed is sown without preparation, the cultivation is called <em>Podi-</em>\n <em>wetha</em>, i. e. dry-sowing; when, before sowing, it is sprouted, it is\n called wet-sowing, or <em>Chetu-wetha</em>; and when it is transplanted, it is\n called <em>Nearra</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Different\ncrops.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the months in which the crops ripen, the first is called\n <em>Canni</em>, and the second <em>Macara</em>. Between the 2d and 11th of June plough twice while the field is dry, and afterwards inundate the ground, which in the course of the following month must be ploughed eight times, the plough going over the field, at each time, first length-wise, and then across. The field is all the while kept inundated, and before the fourth or fifth ploughing is manured with leaves and twigs. After the eighth ploughing the dung is given, and ploughed down. The mud is then smoothed with the feet; and about the 13th of July, the prepared seed is sown, the water being two or three inches deep. In twenty or thirty days the weeds must be removed by the hand. It ripens without any farther trouble than confining the water to the proper depth. The ears only are cut off; and, the rain making it impossible to preserve the straw for fodder, the cattle are allowed to eat it on the ground. The seed is made to sprout by putting it in baskets, and wetting it with water. Thrice a day afterwards, for from four to six days, it is watered, and is then fit for use.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Ubayum</em> land producing two crops.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. When the ears have been removed, the straw is immediately ploughed in for the second crop, which is always transplanted. For this the field is ploughed five or six times. If the farmer be not pushed for time, he allows <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-475" data-page="475" data-folio="449" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n474_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 449">[p. 449]</button> for this operation from ten to twenty days; but, if the season be nearly over, he completes it in less than a week. Fig. 46.). The fields are always kept inundated, and require no weeding. The straw of this crop is cut down close by the ground, and kept four days in a heap. The grain is then rubbed off with the feet; for the <em>Hindus</em>, on such occasions, make as much use of their feet as we do of our hands. Twenty days afterwards the straw is beaten with sticks, and gives some more grain of an inferior quality. The seedlings are raised on a piece of high ground allowed for the purpose, and which pays no tax. Between the 14th of August and the 13th of September this is ploughed four or five times in the course of eight days, the field being in general inundated; this practice, however, is not always followed. The field is manured with leaves and dung; and the seed, after it has been prepared so as to sprout, is sown very thick. It seldom gets any water, except the rain, and before it is transplanted is often very nearly dead. Before the seedlings are pulled, the field must be well watered. After one month and a half, they are fit for transplantation, and continue in that state for fifteen days. The higher parts, not too steep or rocky, are converted into terraces, or might be so; and, according to their fertility, are once in three, four, or five years cultivated for hill-rice, and <em>Gingely</em> (<em>Sesamum</em>). In the eastern parts of <em>Velater</em>, a great extent of this kind of ground has been allowed to be over-run with bushes and long grass; and Mr. Wye does not suppose that it would repay the expense of clearing. He thinks that on this kind of ground the culture of cotton might be introduced, by furnishing the collectors with seed to distribute. He thinks that the Company might show an example, by cultivating a small spot in each district, to initiate the natives in the manner in which new articles might be managed. The expense would be trifling. He thinks that, if the natives knew <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-477" data-page="477" data-folio="451" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n476_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 451">[p. 451]</button> how to cultivate them, <em>Sholum</em> (<em>Holcus sorghum</em>), <em>Dhal</em> (<em>Cytisus cajan</em>), <em>Coolty</em> (<em>Dolichos biflorus</em>), and other dry grains, might be reared. I saw a field which Mr. Wye had sown with hill-rice mixed with <em>Bourbon</em> cotton seed. The crop of rice had been very good ; but, owing to his absence at the time, the weeds had been allowed to choke many of the cotton plants. Such of them as had struggled through were very thriving and productive. There can be no doubt, but that in this manner a very good crop of cotton might be obtained, if pains were taken, after the rice has been cut, to keep down the weeds by ploughing or hoeing. I have, however, some doubt, whether the cotton crop would equal in value that of <em>Sesamum</em>, which now always follows the hill-rice. Although the whole of the <em>Parum</em>, or hilly land, is private property, no one here prevents the cattle of his neighbours from feeding on his ground, or any person that pleases from cutting grass. The people of the country say, that it is customary for the landlords to grant hill land, free of rent for six years, to any person who will clear away the trees or bushes, and form terraces. Afterwards, when cultivated, it pays a rent to the landlord, but no land-tax has been exacted. The best soil on these hills is a red clay, like the soil used for <em>Ragy</em> in <em>Mysore</em>. <em>Ellu</em> or <em>Sesamum</em> is always sown immediately after the hill-rice has been reaped ; but, as this second crop is precarious, some fields are sown with <em>Ellu</em> alone.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Hill-rice.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Afterwards, for eight times, it is ploughed once a month. Between the 11th of April and the 11th of May it is ploughed four or five times, and before the last is manured with ashes and dung. At the end of this period, the seed unprepared is sown broad-cast, and covered with the plough. When the rice is about a foot high the weeds ought to be removed by the hand ; but in general this is much neglected. In ninety days it is ripe. The ears are then cut, and the straw is immediately ploughed down for <em>Sesamum</em>. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-478" data-page="478" data-folio="452" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n477_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 452">[p. 452]</button> The field, after the rice harvest, is ploughed eight or ten times, and before the last is manured. Before either crop is reaped, its amount is estimated, and the cultivator pays a certain share to the landlords. These consider themselves as entitled to a fifth part of the gross produce; but, in fact, a great many of the cultivators being Mussulmans, they will seldom give more than a tenth. The hill-rice is an extremely precarious crop, and five seeds are reckoned a good return; but this, if Mr. Wye be right in calling 32 cubits square a <em>Poray-candum</em>, is 30 bushels an acre; and as this rice comes in when that article is scarce and dear, it sells very high. One-sixtieth of a <em>Poray</em> of <em>Ellu</em> seed is required for a <em>Poray-candum</em>, and 24 seeds is a good crop; which makes the produce 2 <sup>4</sup>⁄<sub>10</sub> bushels an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Shamay</em>, or <em>Panicum miliare.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Shamay</em> is sown in the plantations, or in the ground that serves for raising the seedlings of rice for transplantation. This requires four or five ploughings. The seed is sown about the beginning of May, and is covered by having bushes drawn over the field. There is no regular <em>Warrum</em> or rent for <em>Shamay</em>, but the landlord always gets some share. It ripens in 60 days, and produces very little; but it is ripe at a season when grain is always scarce and dear, and keeps the cultivators alive until the rice harvest.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Improvidence of the natives of <em>Malabar.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The people of <em>Malabar</em> are indeed very improvident. As soon as the rice harvest is over, in order to drink and feast, they sell off their grain at a very low rate; and seven or eight months afterwards their stores are commonly exhausted, the price of rice is doubled, and they are reduced to eat many things which, while rice was in plenty, they would not taste.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote is-locus">Plantations.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The ground for plantations of palms, fruit trees, pepper, <em>Betel</em>-<em>leaf</em>, &amp;c. must be free of rocks, and near a supply of water. It pays <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-479" data-page="479" data-folio="453" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n478_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 453">[p. 453]</button> no land-tax; but a tax has been imposed on some of the articles that it produces. When a man wants to make a new plantation, he applies to some landlord, and gets, upon a land called <em>Cuey Canum</em>, a piece of ground fit for the purpose. According to the size of the garden, he advances from 30 to 50 <em>Fanams</em>, forms the plantation at his sole expense; and for two years after the garden or plantation becomes productive, in order to reward him for his trouble, he receives the whole profits. Afterwards he continues to cultivate the garden; and for his trouble, and for the interest of the money advanced to the landlord, and expended in forming the plantation, he receives one half of the produce. At any time, by paying up the money advanced, and the value of the trees planted, the landlord may entirely reassume the plantation. The value of the trees is fixed by long custom, and must be very nearly the real expense incurred in bringing them to maturity; otherwise no man would be so foolish as to advance money, or form plantations, on the tenure called <em>Cuey Canum</em>. A <em>Betel-nut</em> palm (<em>Areca</em>) is valued at three-tenths of a <em>Poray</em> of rice, worth about half a <em>Fanam</em>; a <em>Jack</em> tree (<em>Artocarpus integrifolia</em>) is valued at 8 <em>Fanams</em>; a coco-nut palm (<em>Cocos</em>) at 16 <em>Fanams</em>; a tree covered with pepper vines at 5 <em>Fanams</em>. These <em>Fanams</em> are the old <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanams</em>, worth ¼ <em>Rupee</em>, or about 6 <em>d.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Negadi</em>, or tax.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Before the Mussulman invasion, these gardens were to the landlords a very valuable property; but, when a tax was laid on, it was supposed to exhaust almost the whole of the landlord's share; and, where the garden is held by the tenure called <em>Cuey Canum</em>, he gets only from one to three <em>Fanams</em> yearly from the <em>Canumcar</em>, who pays the taxes. The plantations in <em>Velater</em> are on the decrease, which, like all other evils in <em>Malabar</em>, is usually attributed to the taxes, but it seems to me without justice. In the other districts under Mr. Wye, the plantations are increasing, and there is no reason to suppose that the tax is more oppressive in the one than in the other. Besides, although the tax, no doubt, bore excessively hard on the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-480" data-page="480" data-folio="454" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n479_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 454">[p. 454]</button> landlords, and may have nearly annihilated their property, as is alleged, it is not asserted, that it has infringed on the share of the persons possessing by the tenure called <em>Cuey Canum</em>; and it is by that class of people alone that gardens have been formed, and by them that they are most commonly kept in repair. But the oppression of the former government, and the turbulence and disorder that have prevailed in <em>Velater</em> since it fell under the Company’s management, are quite sufficient to account for the destruction of the plantations.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Produce of gardens.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Erimpanna</em>, or <em>Caryota urens</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The trees most commonly planted in <em>Velater</em> are, the Betel-nut (<em>Areca</em>), and Jack (<em>Artocarpus integrifolia</em>). Betel-leaf (<em>Piper Betle</em>) is raised in a greater quantity than is consumed in the country; but it is all in the small way; every garden has eight or ten vines, for which the occupant pays nothing. Here are also many of the palms called by the natives <em>Erimpanna</em>, the <em>Caryota urens</em> of Linnæus. Its leaves are the favourite food of the elephant; and the palm wine most commonly used here is extracted from its young <em>spadix</em>, or flowering shoot. The <em>Jagory</em> prepared from this juice is inferior to that which is prepared from the juice of the coco-nut, but superior to the <em>Jagory</em> of the <em>Brab</em>, or <em>Borassus</em>. The natives, however, for drinking, greatly prefer the palm-wine of the coco-nut tree, but it is expensive. The seeds of the <em>Erimpanna</em> are planted, but it pays no tax. In general, it pushes out only one <em>spadix</em> annually; but that is so productive, that the <em>Tiars</em> pay yearly a <em>Fanam</em> for liberty to extract the juice of each palm. When this is old, and has become unfit for producing juice, it is cut; and the heart of the upper quarter of the stem is converted into a kind of sago, which the poor eat in the scarce season. This heart is divided into small pieces, and is exposed for five or six days to the sun. The pieces are then beaten in a large wooden mortar, like that used for removing the husks of rice. By this method a powder is separated from the stringy part. This powder is dried for another day in the sun; and in the evening, to separate some remaining strings, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-481" data-page="481" data-folio="455" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n480_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 455">[p. 455]</button> it is again beaten. From one tree, about a <em>Poray</em> of clean powder, or of a very coarse sago, is procured. This having been washed in water, and the larger part of the water having been poured off, it is boiled with the remainder into a kind of pudding, which is eaten with salt and <em>tyre</em>, or milk curdled by having become sour.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Black pepper.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Wye says, that in the southern division of <em>Malabar</em>, the cultivation of black pepper (<em>Piper nigrum</em>), owing to <em>Tippoo’s</em> having destroyed the vines, is much less than it formerly was; but it is still considerable, and may produce 800 <em>Candies</em> of 640 lb. each, and worth at least 100,000 <em>Rupees</em>. All the gardens are small, and all the cultivators have other property. In June, July, or August, the traders go round to the cultivators, and advance them money, on condition, that in January or February the cultivators shall deliver their pepper at a given place. The money advanced is in proportion to the wants of the cultivator. If he be pressed for money, not above two thirds of the value will be given. In other cases, where the cultivator is not so necessitous, the money advanced is nearer the true value of the pepper. The condition of the bargain is also such, that, if the cultivator does not deliver the stipulated quantity of pepper, he must pay for the deficiency at the <em>Calicut</em> price, which is considerably greater than the common rate of the interior parts of the province. The advance is frequently made in cloth or other goods; but most commonly in old <em>Fanams</em>, worth ¼ <em>Rupee</em>. There are, however, several men, chiefly of the <em>Moplay</em> cast, who are prudent enough to be able to wait for their money until the produce of their gardens is ready for delivery. These, in general, let their gardens on <em>Vir’ Patom</em>, where the cultivator is in fact nothing more than a superintendant for the proprietor, who furnishes every expense, and allows a small per centage on the produce for the support of the tenant. The traders who make the advances to the farmers are mere factors for the merchants residing in the great towns, who furnish them with goods and cash to make the advances, and who have them in as great subjection as they <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-482" data-page="482" data-folio="456" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n481_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 456">[p. 456]</button> have the inconsiderate cultivators. In the southern districts, there being several merchants who make advances, and of course there being some competition, the farmers get for their pepper something that approaches to a fair price, and they are daily increasing the cultivation; but in the northern district every thing is so much under the control of <em>Mousa</em>, the great monopolist at <em>Tellichery</em>, that it would be of very little importance to the cultivator were all the vines to be destroyed; and few are willing to plant new ones in place of those that decay.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Wye's proposal for taking pepper in payment of the land-tax.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Wye thinks that the most ready way of encouraging the cultivation of pepper would be, to allow the proprietors to pay it to government, in lieu of revenue, at a certain fixed rate, which, he thinks, need not exceed 100 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>. The average price given by the merchants to those not in distress is 125 <em>Rupees</em>; but of late it has cost the Company much more. Those who could dispose of their pepper to greater advantage than the Company's offer, might sell it as they pleased; by far the greater part of it would, however, go to the Company; which would gain considerably, and could be put to no inconvenience, by the plan in the southern district, where the amount of the revenue always greatly exceeds the value of all the pepper produced. In the northern districts it might perhaps, at times, be inconvenient for the Company to advance money, and the value of the pepper exceeds the amount of the revenue; but even there the pepper, to the extent of each man's land-tax, might be received, at a fixed price, which, if fair and reasonable, would effectually protect the industrious cultivator from monopolists, whose influence has been known to affect even the commercial interests of the Company.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Plan for the purchase of the whole of the pepper by theCompany.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER. XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. <em>Dec.</em> 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Wye thinks that it might be very advisable for the Company to purchase the whole of the pepper raised in the province, and to make advances to the poorer tenants who may be in need of such assistance. Whatever the Company did not want for their own immediate commerce, might be sold by public sale at the great <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-483" data-page="483" data-folio="457" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n482_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 457">[p. 457]</button> marts, such as <em>Tannore</em>, <em>Calicut</em>, and <em>Tellichery</em>; and the price to be given for the pepper should be regulated by the proceeds of these sales. Mr. Wye thinks that this would be an effectual mean of preventing smuggling, by which the revenue is at present excessively defrauded, there being a duty of 10 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> per cent. <em>ad valorem</em> on all the pepper exported. The whole of this business might be managed by the collectors, so far as the delivery of the pepper into the Company’s store-houses, after which it would of course be under the management of the commercial resident. In the bonds taken by the traders when they make advances, there is no particular precaution taken for the delivery of the pepper of certain qualities; so that we may safely conclude, that difference of curing, or other similar circumstances, make no considerable difference in its value; the receiving it by the collectors, therefore, would be attended with little trouble, and require no particular skill. I have already mentioned this, as one of the means that might be adopted to obviate the difficulties that must always attend a tax which is imposed upon plantations by the number of trees that they contain. I have in my possession a manuscript concerning <em>Travancore</em>, in which it is stated, that the <em>Kerit Rám Rájá</em>, in the year 1757, having received some assistance from the English, was willing to favour their commerce. On this occasion Mr. Spencer, the English chief, took an account of the pepper produced in the dominions of that prince, where there was no land-tax, but where the <em>Rájá</em> monopolized all the pepper, and gave the cultivators a fixed price for whatever they could raise. As the pepper trade of <em>Travancore</em> has always been more flourishing than any other, we may fairly infer, that this is the way in which a tax may be levied from it with the greatest advantage to the extent of cultivation. The whole pepper raised in the dominions of <em>Travancore</em> amounted to 11,752 <em>Candies</em>. For this the <em>Rájá</em> gave to the cultivators 30 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>, amounting to 3,52,560 <em>Rupees</em>. The amount of the sales, even including 2000 <em>Candies</em> that were given to the English Company at the low price <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-484" data-page="484" data-folio="458" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n483_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 458">[p. 458]</button> of 82 <em>Rupees</em>, came to 13,12,260, or on an average 111½ <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>. The <em>Rájá</em> did not, therefore, allow the cultivators more than 27 per cent. of the produce; and yet we know that the cultivation was carried on with the greatest spirit. But were the Company to monopolize the pepper, and allow the farmers 50 per cent. of the value, or 60 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>, I am persuaded that their profit would greatly exceed the amount of any revenue which they can derive from the present plan. How far a similar monopoly might be extended to coco-nut and <em>Betel</em>, or <em>Areca</em>, with advantage, I am not certain. I think it probable, however, that it would contribute greatly to the benefit both of the revenue and of the cultivator. But these being bulky articles, and not easily smuggled, an excise on them might answer the purpose very well, and leave the trade more open to competition.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div If accurate, these details would show that every article of plantation was advancing in the districts under Mr. Wye, except the <em>Betel-nut</em>, which grows chiefly in the interior parts of the country, where the disturbances have prevailed; and this confirms my opinion, that it is owing to these disturbances chiefly, and not to the taxes, that the plantations in many parts of <em>Malabar</em> are on the decrease.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cattle and fodder.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This seems to be a country well fitted by nature for rearing cattle; but no place, in fact, rears worse, or fewer. The hills over a great part of <em>Malabar</em> are clear of wood or bushes, and in the rainy season produce a thick coat of grass, which, as it ripens toward the beginning of the fair season, and is then about a cubit high, is fit for making hay. By the natives this is entirely neglected. Some of the grasses are indeed very coarse; but Mr. Wye has made good hay from one of the species, which I take to be Dr. Roxburgh’s <em>Ischæmum geminatum</em>. It grows very commonly, and with a little pains might be made universal. At present, there being the greatest abundance of grass, the cattle of <em>Malabar</em> are in better condition than any that I have seen in India; but, as that will soon wither up from the drought, I am told, that for three months before the commencement of the rainy season their condition will be deplorable. On the present system, no more cattle can be kept than what can be supported from the beginning of January until the end of May upon the straw of the second or smallest crop of rice. The straw of the first crop, owing to its being cut in the rainy season, cannot be preserved. If hay could be collected, or if on the higher <em>Parum</em> lands <em>Ragy</em> (<em>Cynosurus corocanus</em>) could be cultivated, merely for the straw, a great many more cattle might be kept, and the increased quantity of manure would be an immense advantage to the farmer.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Want of markets.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 20, 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Every where in the interior parts of <em>Malabar</em> a prodigious inconvenience is felt, from the want of <em>Bazars</em> or markets. A little <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-486" data-page="486" data-folio="460" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n485_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 460">[p. 460]</button> encouragement given to the <em>Nazarens</em> might induce that industrious class of men to settle in small villages of 30 or 40 houses, at reasonable distances throughout the country, where they would keep shops greatly to the advantage of the natives.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The exports and imports, by sea and land, in the part of this province under Mr. Wye will be seen in extracts from the custom-house accompts, which were sent to me by that gentleman. See the <span class="sc">APPENDIX</span> at the end of the Third Volume.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Salt.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Wye says, that in his districts there are 18,544 <em>Cannies</em> or plots of ground employed for making salt, and that the quantity might probably be increased, were the inhabitants rich enough to incur the necessary expense. According to the calculations procured at <em>Calicut</em>, the quantity of salt made will be about 2096 Winchester bushels. It will be seen, that the quantity of salt exported, especially by sea, is considerable: in the two last years, after allowing for the importation of a small quantity, an excess of 40,000 <em>Parahs</em> has been exported. I am not acquainted with the contents of a <em>Parah</em> of salt. If it be the same with a <em>Poray</em> of rice, the annual exportation would be double the quantity which, according to my calculation, is manufactured. The <em>Canny</em> of salt ground may, however, be different here from that in use at <em>Calicut</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 22. Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 22.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">22d <em>December</em>.—In the morning I went a long stage to <em>Vencata</em>-<em>cotay</em>. The road, most of the way, passes along the ridge of a low hill, whence narrow vallies go off toward both sides, and are separated from each other by branches of the hill. These vallies are very beautiful; but the rest of the country, at this season, looks ill. It is only the declivities of the hills that are formed into terraces for cultivation; the level ground on the summits of the ridges, which occupies a large proportion of the country, is quite waste. The soil in some places is apparently good; but, in general, the <em>Laterite</em>, or brick-stone, comes very near the surface, and would impede the plough. In some places the granite appears. As usual in <em>Malabar</em>, it is entirely free from veins of quartz or felspar. The <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-487" data-page="487" data-folio="461" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n486_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 461">[p. 461]</button> whole, however, is covered with long grass used for thatch and pasture; but not a thousandth part of it is employed for these purposes; and in December and January the remainder is burnt, which destroys the bushes that spring up in the rainy season, and keeps the country clear.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Vencata-cotay</em> is in a district named <em>Shirnada</em>, and the land-tax is paid in kind. This appears to be owing to its having formerly been <em>Chericul</em> lands, that is, such as were appropriated for the support of a <em>Rájá</em>. When the <em>Rájá</em> of the <em>Tamuri</em> family, to whom it belonged, fled to <em>Travancore</em>, the whole was seized, and valued at the full rack-rent. By some error in the accompts, every <em>Patom</em> (portion equal to the quantity of seed sown) of rent was valued at a <em>Fanam</em>; which being more than its average value, the farmers would not have cultivated the ground, had the rent been demanded in money. It has therefore been taken in kind, as is usually done by landlords when they receive rack-rent. Last year the Company found a considerable deficiency from the amount rated in the public accompts, although the grain was kept until the dearest season before it was sold. A man has this year undertaken to pay the whole in money, on condition that the instalments of payment are made at favourable seasons. He is enabled to do this, by letting the ground to the most advantage; whereas the government allowed the farmers 100 seeds for every <em>ten-poray-land</em>, and agreed to receive what was given them as the remainder of the crop, in which, of course, they were enormously defrauded. The farmers of <em>Vencata-cotay</em> make annual agreements for their lands, and frequently change their possessions; but no rice grounds are unoccupied.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Yatam</em> of <em>Malayala.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 22.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I observed one of the machines for raising water called a <em>Yatam</em>, which was made on a better construction than those above the <em>Ghats</em>. It was wrought by one man, who stood on a plank running parallel to the lever, and placed on one side; so that the side of the lever passed parallel to his face, and he was in no danger of being hurt by the bucket coming up between his legs, as happens <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-488" data-page="488" data-folio="462" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n487_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 462">[p. 462]</button> where the man's face looks toward the end of the lever. The lever was made of a <em>Bamboo</em>, and the weight was a large stone fixed by a swivel. The bucket was made of an excavated piece of wood, shaped like the half of a cheshire cheese, and, when full of water, was lighter than the stone, which of course raised it without any exertion of the labourer. From a well 16 feet deep the man raised four buckets in a minute, each containing 209 <sup>3</sup>/<sub>10</sub> cubical inches, or about 178 ale gallons in the hour.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Family of the <em>Tamuri Rájá.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">At night I was visited by the sixth <em>Rájá</em> of the <em>Tamuri</em> family, who, as well as the third in rank, resides at <em>Vencata-cotay</em>. The third declined receiving a visit from me; and perhaps thought that his coming, as his relation did, would be derogatory to his rank. The <em>Rájá</em> who visited me was a good looking man of about 50 years of age. His suite was not numerous, but very well dressed. He said, that <em>Shirnada</em>, the country in which we then were, formerly belonged to the <em>Rájá</em> of <em>Velater</em>. About 400 years ago that chief, having been attacked by the northern <em>Nairs</em>, applied for assistance to the <em>Tamuri</em>, and by ceding <em>Shirnada</em> obtained the protection of that prince. The fort of <em>Vencata</em> was afterwards built by the <em>Tamuris</em>; but, although it was much enlarged by the late <em>Sultan</em>, it is still a place of no importance. The <em>Rájás</em> of <em>Malabar</em>, indeed, do not seem to have ever trusted to fortifications for the defence of their country. The <em>Tamuri</em> family, during the time they suffered exile in the <em>Travancore</em> dominions, were chiefly supported by the liberality of its prince. There are at present about 25 <em>Tamburettis</em>, or ladies of this noble family, and the males are nearly about the same number.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 23. <em>Tiruvana</em>-<em>Angady.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">23d <em>December</em>,—I went a short journey to <em>Tiruvana-Angady</em>, and passed through a country similar to that which I saw yesterday. <em>Tiruvana-Angady</em> is a small <em>Moplay</em> town on the southern bank of a river which comes from <em>Irnada</em>, and in the rainy season is navigable with canoes for 32 miles upwards. It has no communication with the <em>Baypour</em> (<em>Vaypura</em>) river, as represented in Major Rennell's <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-489" data-page="489" data-folio="463" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n488_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 463">[p. 463]</button> map. <em>Tiruvana-Angady</em> is the place which in our maps is called <em>Tervanagary</em>, and is remarkable for the decisive victory which in the year 1790 Colonel Hartley gained in its neighbourhood over the forces of <em>Tippoo</em>. Near the <em>Angady</em>, or market, there is a small fort, which was erected by the <em>Sultán</em> round a <em>Colgum</em>, or palace, belonging to the <em>Tamuri Rájá</em>. Both have now fallen into ruins; and the <em>Tamuri</em>, since his return from exile, has not visited the place.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Parum</em>, or high-land of <em>Shirnada</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this district of <em>Shirnada</em> the <em>Parumba</em> land may be divided into three kinds. First, that which is fit for plantations. This kind of land pays a rent to the landlord, when cultivated for ginger, turmeric, plantains, or other articles that are not taxed: the rent amounts to 1 <em>Fanam</em> for every <em>Poray-candum</em>, of which nine-twenty-fourths go to government as land-tax. The second kind is land near the villages, which is cultivated once in three years for hill-rice, <em>Sesamum</em>, and <em>Shamay</em> (<em>Panicum miliare</em>). It pays the same rent as the first kind, and the same share of this is taken as land-tax. The third kind is cultivated in the same manner; but, on account of its distance from the villages, poverty, or other causes, such as the officers who valued it having been corrupted, it pays no tax. To the landlord it ought to pay a fifth part of the produce; but some farmers refuse to give more than one seventh.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation of black-pepper, <em>Piper nigrum</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 23.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I here examined the cultivators concerning the manner of raising the pepper vine. They say, that it does not thrive where planted close together; and therefore every man, in the garden near his house, has five or six trees only, which are intended as supports for this valuable plant. The <em>Mango</em> tree (<em>Mangifera</em>) is reckoned the best for the purpose, and its fruit is not injured by the pepper. The <em>Jack</em> tree (<em>Artocarpus integrifolia</em>) is sometimes employed; but its fruit is diminished in quantity, and is said to be injured in quality, the pepper communicating its flavour. This circumstance I have heard confirmed by Europeans; but I confess that I am still sceptical. The pepper-vine thrives also very well on the <em>Erythrina</em>; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-490" data-page="490" data-folio="464" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n489_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 464">[p. 464]</button> and, where there are no trees in the garden, this is preferred; as a large branch of it, being put in the ground in the rainy season, will in the course of a year be fit for receiving the vine; and in the mean time <em>Mango</em> trees may be raised, to serve after the <em>Erythrinas</em> have died; for the pepper vines, after bearing from six to fifteen years, kill this tree. The <em>Mango</em> tree ought to be at least twenty years old before any pepper vines are put on it. Suppose a <em>Mango</em> tree be fit for receiving the vine, the following is the manner in which that is planted. Between the 11th of June and the 12th of July, or at the commencement of the rainy season, the soil round the tree is dug; and a small bank, surrounding the root at a cubit’s distance, is formed to confine the water. Then from 8 to 12 shoots of the vine, in proportion to the size of the tree, are laid down within the bank, and with two or three inches of one end standing up against the trunk. They are then covered with about an inch of fine mould; and, if any length of time occurs without rain, they must be watered; but this is seldom required. The shoots are about a cubit long. As the vines grow, they must be tied up to the tree, and rank weeds must be pulled up from near their roots. In the hot season they require to be watered with a pot; and at the commencement of the rainy season some leaves, ashes, and dung, must be spread on the ground near their roots. The pepper vine begins to bear at six years of age; in four years more it is in full perfection, and continues so for twenty years, when it dies. The young <em>amenta</em> begin to form at a feast called <em>Tiruvadaray Netvelly</em>, which is accompanied by a certain conjunction of the stars, the period of which none but astrologers can tell. It happened this year on the 17th of June. The beginning of the rainy season may therefore be considered as the flowering time of the pepper. When the fruit is intended for black pepper, it is not allowed to ripen; but is collected green, so soon as the berries become hard and firm, which happens between the 13th of December and the 11th of January. As the <em>amenta</em> come to a proper <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-491" data-page="491" data-folio="465" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n490_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 465">[p. 465]</button> maturity, they are pinched off by the fingers, placed on a mat, and rubbed with the hands and feet, until the berries separate from the stem. These are then spread out on mats, so that one does not lie upon another, and are dried two, or at the most three days in the sun; while at night they are collected in earthen jars, to keep them from the dew. The pepper is then put up in mat-bags, containing from 2 to 4 <em>Tolams</em>, or from 64 to 128 lb., and is fit for sale. The whole cost attending this process seems to be very trifling; and I have no doubt of Mr. Smee’s allowance, of one-ninth of the produce, being fully adequate to defray the annual expense. The original cost of planting can hardly be brought to an accompt, it is so small. What is intended for white pepper, is allowed to become quite ripe. The berries are then red, and, the pulp being washed off, the white seed is dried for sale. The vines in this case are very apt to die, and in this province little or none is now made.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures of pepper gardens.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The soil most esteemed here for pepper is red, and contains small stones. When a man wants to make a garden, he gives on a mortgage bond (<em>Cuey Canum</em>) from 15 to 40 <em>Fanams</em> to the landlord for three <em>Poray-candums</em> of a proper soil; and pays annually one <em>Fanam</em> for each <em>Poray-candum</em>, should he raise nothing in his garden but ginger, turmeric, plantains, <em>Mangoes</em>, or the like: but out of this the landlord pays the tax. Should the mortgagee plant any <em>Jack</em>, <em>Betel-nut</em>, or coco-nut trees, or pepper vines, as soon as these come into bearing, he must give an eighth part of the produce to the landlord, and pay the land-tax, which on pepper vines is 3 <em>Fanams</em> for every tree. This is only to be understood of the largest trees, supporting ten or twelve pepper vines. In the revenue accompts, a sufficient number of smaller trees are written as one, to make the tax equable, and not heavier than at the rate of three <em>Fanams</em> for a tree of the best quality. The three <em>Fanams</em> paid for the land-tax are new, of which 3½ are equal to 1 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>3½</sub> <em>Rupee</em>. But all other <em>Fanams</em> mentioned among the farmers are old, and equal to ¼ <em>Rupee</em>.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-492"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="492" data-folio="466" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n491_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 466">[p. 466]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 23. Produce of the pepper vine.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The produce of the vines growing on a tree paying full revenue, the cultivators estimate at half a <em>Tolam</em>, or 16 lb.; but the collector’s agent says, that this is the produce of a very ordinary tree; that a middling one produces three quarters of a <em>Tolam</em>, or 24 lb.; and that a good one in fact produces one <em>Tolam</em>, or 32 lb. The land-tax, the farmers acknowledge, amounts to about a third part of the produce. Now we shall see that they frequently sell their pepper at 15 old <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Tolam</em>, equal to 13¼ new ones; and by this estimate the average produce should be <sup>9</sup>/<sub>13¼</sub> parts of a <em>Tolam</em>, or about 21¾ lb. In the revenue accompts of Mr. Wye’s districts, the number of trees bearing pepper vines is rated at 8484; and the average quantity exported is 4270 <em>Tolams</em>, which gives very nearly half a <em>Tolam</em>, or 16 lb. for the produce of each tree; to which we must add what is consumed in the country, and what is smuggled; and then 21¾ lb. will not, I am persuaded, be thought too great an allowance. Each tree actually producing pepper with vigour is, however, by no means to be conceived as producing that quantity. This is the produce of the best; and in the revenue accompts, four, or even five, are written as one, as I have before mentioned; still I am at a loss to explain the very small quantity of produce stated by Mr. Smee as the average of the trees in the southern and middle parts of <em>Malabar</em>. After rejecting all trees covered with old or young vines, as not productive, he estimates the average produce of the remainder at 4½ lb. of dried pepper. I am still farther staggered by the quantity of pepper stated by Mr. Wye to be exported from the southern division alone, and which that gentleman has good opportunities of knowing: this he stated, in round numbers, at 800 <em>Candies</em> of 640 lb. Now the whole productive pepper vines, in the southern and middle division of <em>Malabar</em>, by Mr. Smee’s valuation, amount to only 45,803, and at his average rate of produce could give only 322 <em>Candies</em>. By Mr. Wye’s estimate of the export, each of them ought certainly to give more than 11 pounds, exclusive <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-493" data-page="493" data-folio="467" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n492_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 467">[p. 467]</button> of country consumption, smuggling, and the number of vines in the middle division; but, as these are very few, they need not be taken into consideration. The obstacles, indeed, that are thrown in the way of the most careful investigation of the produce of plantations are such, as should cause a mode of taxation founded on a supposed knowledge of its amount to be totally rejected. The present tax is three <em>Fanams</em> for every vine; that is to say, for as many vines as produce nine <em>Fanams</em> worth of pepper. Mr. Smee, in consequence of his finding the produce so small, proposes to reduce the tax to one half levied on the actual number of productive vines, which, by his account, amount to about one fourth of the whole. The present tax is certainly not oppressive, as the cultivation is extending; and the cultivators, besides giving a considerable loan on mortgage to the landlord, can afford to give him one eighth part of the produce, and a small ground-rent for the garden. It will be seen, that the principal evils attached to the cultivators arise from their own want of prudence.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Trade in pepper.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Farmers of prudence and substance, such as the <em>Moplays</em> mostly are, receive no advances; but, when their pepper is fit for market, sell it to the best advantage, and deliver it at the sea-port towns at from 24 to 25 old <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanams</em> a <em>Tolam</em>, or at from 120 to 125 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em> of 640 lb. The case, however, is very different with most of the <em>Hindus</em>, who in <em>Malabar</em> are as remarkable for a thoughtless profusion, as in other parts they are notoriously penurious. Between the 12th of June and the 13th of September, the Mussulman traders come from the coast, and enter into written agreements with those who are willing to receive advances. The cultivator agrees to deliver a certain quantity of pepper, for which the trader pays down immediately from 13 to 15 <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Tolam</em>, or from 65 to 75 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>. Should the cultivator, at the crop season, be unable to deliver the quantity for which he contracted, he must pay for the deficiency at the market price, which is generally 120 or 125 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>. As he is seldom or never able to <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-494" data-page="494" data-folio="468" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n493_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 468">[p. 468]</button> pay this in cash, he gives a note of hand, engaging to deliver pepper for the amount of the price of the deficiency, at the rate of one <em>Tolam</em> for from 13 to 15 <em>Fanams</em>; but no interest is charged. Indeed, the profits of the trader are immense; as for an advance of 15 <em>Fanams</em> for six months, he gets a profit of ten; and it is evident that the risk is very small. Should a merchant not consent to receive the pepper, on account of its being bad, the cultivator may sell it to any person that he pleases, and give the proceeds to the merchant. Should these not amount to the market price, he gives a note of hand for the balance, which is considered as part of the advance for the next year. It is evidently the interest of the merchant to keep up a high nominal price, even should he, in selling the pepper to foreigners, be obliged to allow a large discount; for all the balances due by the farmer are paid at what is called the market price. The present market price is 125 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>, or 2<em>l</em>. 1<em>s</em>. 5<em>d</em>. a hundred-weight. It is sometimes so low as 100 <em>Rupees</em>, and at others rises to double that sum.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Wona</em> festival.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cultivators, when questioned concerning the reason that can induce them to take up money on terms so disadvantageous, attribute it entirely to the land-tax; for every evil in <em>Malabar</em> is ascribed to that as its source. When asked, however, if they would be willing to pay in their pepper to the Company, in place of the land-tax, at 18 or 20 <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Tolam</em>, they start innumerable objections. At length I found out, that the real cause of the <em>Hindus</em> disposing of their pepper at this low rate, is a festival called <em>Wona</em>, which is celebrated in the month <em>Singhium</em>. At this the <em>Hindus</em> expend in drinking and finery every thing which they can raise. In their fits of intoxication there are constant scenes of fighting and abuse, which were a great source of revenue to the <em>Rájás</em>, as opening a way for law-suits and criminal prosecutions; and, with a view probably to encourage this source of revenue, the <em>Rájás</em> did not punish any murder that was committed during the festivities of <em>Wona</em>; but restricted themselves to fining those who gave abusive <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-495" data-page="495" data-folio="469" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n494_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 469">[p. 469]</button> language, in the vehemence and indecency of which the <em>Hindus</em> exceed all other nations. It is hardly necessary to mention, that such an excuse for murder would not be admitted in the courts which are at present established in the country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Ginger and turmeric.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the gardens of this neighbourhood much ginger and turmeric are cultivated. For this purpose a red soil, free from stones, is reckoned the best. Between the 11th of April and the 11th of May the garden is dug with the hoe, and formed into ridges, one cubit broad, one cubit high, and one cubit distant from each other, their sides being perpendicular. Two rows of the cuttings of ginger, or turmeric, are put in each ridge, and slightly covered with earth. The plot is then covered with bushes, to act as a manure, and to keep off the sun. Between the 12th of June and the 13th of July, the shoots appear above ground; and ten days afterwards the stems of the bushes are removed. Between every two rows of the plants, small twigs of trees are then put lengthwise and across, and weeds are removed by the hand. Between the 13th of December and the 11th of January, both roots are fit for pulling. Those intended for replanting are mixed with a little red mud, and immediately buried in a pit.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The ginger, intended for sale, is scraped with a knife to remove the outer skin; and, having been sprinkled with the ashes of cow-dung, is spread out on mats, and dried eight or ten days; when it is fit for sale.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The turmeric intended for sale, immediately after having been taken up, is boiled a whole day in water. The pieces that are large are then split, and for five or six days the whole is exposed on mats to the sun.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Cycas.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the gardens and plantations toward the sea coast of the southern parts of <em>Malabar</em>, a species of <em>Cycas</em>, called <em>Indu</em> by the natives (<em>Todda Panna Hort: Mal:</em>), is very common; but it grows spontaneously from the nuts that accidentally fall. The nuts are collected; and, having been dried for a month in the sun, are beaten in a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-496" data-page="496" data-folio="470" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n495_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 470">[p. 470]</button> mortar, and the kernels formed into a flour, which the natives eat, and call <em>Indum Podi.</em> It is reckoned superior to the flour prepared from the stem of the <em>Erimpanna (Caryota),</em> but it is only used by the poor, who between the 14th of July and the 13th of September are in danger of perishing. It is prepared during the former month, and cannot be preserved longer than the end of the latter.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">24th <em>December.</em>—I set out with an intention of stopping at <em>Pa</em>-<em>rupa-nada,</em> which in our maps is called <em>Perperengarde;</em> but, owing to the untowardness of my guides, I found, on my arrival there, that my tents had been carried on to <em>Vay-pura.</em> I was of course obliged to follow; but much of my baggage did not arrive until four in the afternoon, and the cattle were worn out with fatigue. From <em>Tiruvana Angady</em> to <em>Parupa-nada</em> is a plain, intersected in the middle by a deep creek running north and south, and forming the boundary between the <em>Shirnada</em> belonging to the <em>Tamuri,</em> and the dominions of a petty chief called the <em>Parupa-nada Rájá.</em> The country formerly belonging to this chief extended in a narrow tract along the sea side, from the river of <em>Calicut</em> to <em>Tannore.</em> At <em>Tannore</em> commenced another petty <em>Rájáship,</em> extending to the <em>Panyáni</em> river, and called <em>Bettutanada.</em> The family that governed this has become entirely extinct.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Parupa-nada.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The plain between <em>Tiruvana</em> and <em>Parupa-nada</em> consists partly of high (<em>Parum</em>) and partly of very low, or <em>Paddum</em> land. Both are at present very much neglected. The higher land, being no where steep, seems capable of being wholly converted into plantations, or of being cultivated with hill-rice, and other <em>dry-grains.</em> The <em>Paddum-land</em> is very low; and so much inundated, that a great part of it does not become dry until the hot season. It is said to be of a very good quality. The neglected state of agriculture is attributed to the want of people, and to the poverty of those who remain. Near the sea coast runs a narrow border, well filled with coco-nut gardens, and highly productive. Between this and the sea are barren downs, on which is situated the <em>Moplay</em> town <em>Parupa-nada.</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-497" data-page="497" data-folio="471" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n496_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 471">[p. 471]</button> This contains about 700 houses, and is the best built native town that I have yet seen. The houses are built of stone; and, were they better aired, would be comfortable even for Europeans.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Moplays.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Moplays</em> on the sea coast are a most industrious quiet people, and will scarcely acknowledge as brethren those ruffians who live in the interior part of the country, and who, having been let loose upon the <em>Nairs</em>, have acquired a blood-thirsty, rapacious disposition, which they justify by considering their adversaries as infidels.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rivers and surf.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At some distance north from <em>Parupa-nada</em>, I passed over, in a very bad ferry boat, the mouth of the river which comes from <em>Tiruvana</em>; and about two miles farther, I crossed a still larger river, and came to <em>Vay-pura</em>, which in our maps is called <em>Baypour</em>. When compared with that at Madras, the surf on this coast is trifling; and, except where rocky head-lands run a little way into the sea, boats of any kind might, without danger, land on every part of the coast. I met people tracking boats along the shore, and observed men, who were walking on the outside of the surf, and pushing along floats of timber, while the water did not reach up to their shoulders.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Parupa-nada Rájá.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Parupa-nada Rájá</em> resides at <em>Vay-pura</em>. He pretends to be of the <em>Kshatri</em> cast. Like those of the other noble families of <em>Malabar</em>, the ladies of this, who are called <em>Tamburettis</em>, cohabit with such of the <em>Namburis</em> as they choose, and all their male children are called <em>Unitamban</em>. The five eldest of these are called <em>Rájás</em>, each of whom has a peculiar title. That of the oldest, who is the head of the family, is <em>Taycapadu</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Vay-pura, and the Teak trade.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Vay-pura</em> was originally called north <em>Parupa-nada</em>. It is a small town, containing 120 houses of all casts, and has hardly any trade. The situation is most beautiful, on the north side of a river, where it enters the sea. Within, the river has deep water; but, like all those on the coast, has a bar at its mouth. At favourable seasons vessels drawing 14 feet may be floated over the bar by means of casks. Some projectors have here erected lofts, and other works <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-498" data-page="498" data-folio="472" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n497_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 472">[p. 472]</button> accommodated for building ships of war; for which purpose no place seems to be worse fitted. Small vessels, however, might be built to considerable advantage, a good deal of timber coming down the river, which affords fine situations for slips, or docks. The native collector says, that two years ago 10,000 <em>Teak</em> trees were brought down the river; but this was the produce of the forests for four years, none having been exported during that time. The native collector thinks, that between two and three thousand trees may be annually procured. The timber is cut on the <em>Ghats</em>, and conveyed by elephants to the part of the river which in the rainy season has water sufficient to float it. Many of the best trees are cut in two, to enable the elephants to drag them; and in this operation many of them are rent, or otherwise materially injured. <em>Teak</em> timber, of an ordinary quality for ship-building, sells at 9 or 10 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>, which measures 10¾ English cubical feet. The foot, therefore, costs from about 18<em>d.</em> to 2<em>s.</em> Choice timber sells as high as 16 <em>Rupees</em> a <em>Candy</em>, or 34 <sup>2</sup>⁄<sub>10</sub><em>d.</em> a cubical foot.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At <em>Vay-pura</em> some private gentlemen, with a view of supplying the dock yard at <em>Bombay</em> with <em>Teak</em> plank, have erected a saw mill; and a great deal of money has been expended in the project, with very little prospect of success. The power intended to have turned this mill was the wind; which appears to me to be by far too precarious a moving power for such heavy machinery.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No rice is exported from this river, the produce of the country being only sufficient for the maintenance of the inhabitants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 25. Coco-nut plantations.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec. 25.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. When <em>Cheruman Permal</em> had divided <em>Malabar</em> among his nobles, and had no principality remaining to bestow on the ancestor of the <em>Tamuri</em>, he gave that chief his sword, with all the territory in which a cock crowing at a small temple here could be heard. This place continued to be the chief residence of the <em>Tamuri Rájás</em> until the Mussulman invasion, and became a very flourishing city, owing to the success that its lords had in war, and the encouragement which they gave to commerce. <em>Tippoo</em> destroyed the town, and removed its inhabitants to <em>Nelluru</em>, the name of which he changed to <em>Furruck-ábád</em>; for, like all the Mussulmans of India, he was a mighty changer of old Pagan names. Fifteen months after this forced emigration, the English conquered the province, and the inhabitants returned with great joy to their old place of residence. The soil near the sea is poorer than in the interior of the province; but the grain being higher priced at the sea side, makes the value of the crops in both parts nearly the same.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Salt manufacture.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">All along the coast of <em>Malabar</em> salt is made by the natural evaporation of the sea water. In order to be satisfied concerning this subject, I first examined a set of people called <em>Vaytuvans</em>, who are those by whom the salt is made. Their account is as follows.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Account given by the <em>Vaytuvans</em>, or manufacturers.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. Dec.26—30.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Low grounds near the inlets from the sea, and surrounded by channels into which the tide flows, are chosen for the purpose. Between February the 10th and March the 11th, the preparatory steps must be finished. First, the tide is excluded, by damming up at low water the mouth of the channels. The field is then cleaned, by removing a slippery green <em>conferva</em> that grows in the rainy season. It is then ploughed twice, and the roots of any herbs that grow on it are carefully removed. The field is then allowed 20 days to dry, and the clods are broken with a wooden stake. The channels for admitting the tide are then cleared, and filled with sand, to be used as afterwards mentioned. Part of it is mixed with ooze, and of these materials is formed a square plot, or one in form of a parallelogram, which is raised about a foot above the level of the field, well smoothed, and then every day for a month rubbed with a stone, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-508" data-page="508" data-folio="480" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n507_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 480">[p. 480]</button> until it becomes solid and hard. It is divided into small squares, eight or ten cubits in extent, and surrounded by small banks of the same materials. This plot is used for evaporating the brine. It is surrounded by several small mounds, which are formed of sand taken from the channels and mixed with a little mud. In the summit of each is formed a small cistern, about a cubit in diameter. From the bottom of this a <em>Bamboo</em> spout conveys any water that may be put in the cistern, to a reservoir which is formed at the bottom of the mound. The whole field is then smoothed; and about the 12th of March the dams are removed from the channels, and the tides are daily admitted. Some earth is every morning scraped from the surface of the field, and with this the cisterns in the mounds are filled. Water from the channels is then poured upon this earth, and filters gradually through the pipe into the reservoir. A clear brine is thus procured, with which every day at noon the small squares in the plot are filled, to about the depth of a quarter of an inch. In the course of the afternoon the water is evaporated by the sun and wind, the salt remains quite dry, and is collected in the evening. This operation is repeated daily for 92 days, from the 12th of March to the 11th of June inclusive. The salt made in the beginning of the season is the best and cleanest. The grain is large and cubical; but it is never white, and has a strong attraction for water.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. The <em>Canny</em> ought to be twelve feet long by eight broad; and six of them pay one <em>Fanam</em> as land-tax. If they happen to be larger or smaller than the proper measure, they are taxed accordingly. A family of <em>Vaytuvans</em> can manage 40 <em>Cannies</em>. The proprietor of the field, whether he be a landlord (<em>Jenmcar</em>) or a mortgagee (<em>Canumcar</em>), either hires a <em>Vaytuvan</em> to work it, or employs his own slaves. In the former case, the <em>Vaytuvan</em>, during the two months that he is employed in the preparatory steps, receives half a new <em>Vir'-Ráya Fanam</em> a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-509" data-page="509" data-folio="481" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n508_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 481">[p. 481]</button> day, his wife gets a quarter of a <em>Fanam</em>, and the proprietor finds the ploughs and cattle. When the salt begins to be made, the <em>Vaytuvan</em> receives two-thirds, and the proprietor one-third, of the produce. A field of 40 <em>Cannies</em> produces daily 80 <em>Puddies</em>, of 121½ cubical inches, which at the time sells for from 20 to 30 <em>Puddies</em> for the <em>Fanam</em>. Allowing, therefore, that a man and woman manage a field of 40 <em>Cannies</em>, and that the salt is sold at the cheapest, in 92 days evaporation they would make 7360 <em>Puddies</em> of salt, or 416 bushels; but I am inclined to think, that, although the <em>Vaytuvan</em> did not mention this circumstance, an allowance must be made for rainy days that occasionally happen at this season, and must interrupt the process. I allow therefore only 84 days evaporation; producing 6720 <em>Puddies</em>, or almost 380 bushels of salt, worth 224 <em>Fanams</em>, or 64 <em>Bombay Rupees</em>, or 6<em>l</em>. 2<em>s</em>. 3<em>d</em>. When the salt sells high, one half must be added to all these gains.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Account given by a proprietor.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XII. They are of <em>Malayala</em> extraction. Besides making salt, they quarry stones, dig tanks, build mud walls; and, from the outer husk of the coco-nut, their women twist <em>Coir</em> rope. They can all eat together and intermarry. They have no hereditary chiefs, and refer all their disputes to the officers of government. A man marries only one wife, who lives in his family, and whose children are considered as his heirs. The ancient custom permitted a man to put his wife to death, if he detected her in adultery; but at present the cuckold contents himself with drubbing his wanton spouse. The reason of this seems to be, that none of the higher casts will touch a <em>Vaytuvan</em> woman, and none of the slaves can approach her house; so that she has no <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-511" data-page="511" data-folio="483" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n510_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 483">[p. 483]</button> opportunity of being corrupted, except by a man of the same cast; and a slip with such a person, among the lower <em>Hindus</em>, is looked upon as a very trivial matter. Some few of the <em>Vaytuvans</em> can read a little. They bury the dead, and seem to have no knowledge of a future life. As the deity peculiar to their cast, they worship <em>Nedamaly Bhagawatí</em>, a goddess who is represented by a stone, which is placed in a hut formed of coco-nut leaves. In March an annual festival is celebrated in honour of this idol. It lasts three days, during which a lamp is burned in the rude temple, and a fowl is sacrificed before the stone. The most venerable of the cast then takes some boiled rice, carries it thrice round the temple, offers it to the goddess, and divides it among the people, who eat it with reverence, considering it as holy. The <em>Vaytuvans</em> are not permitted to approach the temples of the great gods; but may send offerings by the hands of a person of pure descent. They have no <em>Guru</em>, <em>Puróhita</em>, nor priest of any kind.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dec. 31.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">31st <em>December</em>.—During the night there has been heavy rain, which at this season is very uncommon. It is situated in <em>Tamarachery Nada</em>, a district that formerly belonged to the <em>Cotay-hutty</em>, or <em>Pychi Rájá</em>. In this family the four eldest males assume the title of <em>Rájá</em>. The three eldest, like the other <em>Rájás</em> of <em>Malabar</em>, fled into <em>Travancore</em>, to escape from the violence of the late <em>Sultan</em>; but the fourth remained behind in defiance of the Mussulman, at times <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-512" data-page="512" data-folio="484" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra02hami_2/page/n511_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 484">[p. 484]</button> being forced to retire into the woods, and again at times issuing forth with a band of determined <em>Nairs</em>, overthrowing the troops of the <em>Sultan</em>, and levying contributions to a great extent. On the arrival of the English army, this chief joined them with a considerable body of <em>Nairs</em>, and expected perhaps that he would have been favoured in the same manner as the <em>Coorg Rájá</em> was. In this, however, he was disappointed. The eldest male of the house, indeed, to avoid disturbance, would not quit <em>Travancore</em>; but the two next in succession, and whose claim to the dominions of the house was undoubtedly founded in law, returned, and requested that they might be put on the same footing with the other <em>Rájás</em> of <em>Malabar</em>, by having the management of the territory that formerly belonged to their family. This was accordingly granted, so far as was practicable, and they are now in quiet possession of the fifth of the revenue in the <em>Nadas</em>, or districts of <em>Curumbara</em> and <em>Tamarachery</em>. This gave great offence to the fourth <em>Rájá</em>, who considered himself entitled to the whole, by his seniors having deserted their country, whilst he stood gallantly on the defensive. The leaves are the most useful part of this palm, and serve for thatch, for umbrellas, and as paper; but by no means correspond with the accounts given by travellers in <em>Ceylon</em>. The thatch is reckoned inferior to that of the coco-nut; as it requires to be changed twice a year, whereas the coco-nut leaves last twelve months. In books this leaf is very durable, and all valuable manuscripts are written on the leaves (<em>Ollas</em>) of the <em>Codda Panna</em>; those of the <em>Ampanna</em>, or <em>Borassus</em>, being in this country used only for accompts. Some fine palms of the <em>Corypha</em> produce folds five inches wide, and these sell very high.
