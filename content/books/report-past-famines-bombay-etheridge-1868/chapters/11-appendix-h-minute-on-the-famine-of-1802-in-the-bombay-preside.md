(By the Honorable Jonathan Duncan, Governor and President in Council of Bombay.)</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Bombay, the 10th June 1805.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Summary of Proceedings respecting grain from 1803 till the present period.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Abrogation in September 1802 of the Custom Master's powers respecting the restriction of the export of grain.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Custom Master authorised in September 1803 to put a stop to the export of grain till further orders.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Explanation of the materials whence the following narrative is deduced.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Liberal suggestions of Mr. Henshaw in respect to the means of alleviating the scarcity.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">1. Having had occasion to revise our proceedings on the matter of the Company's Counsel's Report of the 10th of May last, and of Mr. Henshaw's explanatory letter of the 8th instant, I have prepared the following summary, as well for the satisfaction of my own mind as to bring the whole subject more distinctly into a connected point of view. It will thence appear that after the abrogation of the Custom Master's general powers to regulate the export of grain, which took place by a Resolution of Council passed on the 3rd of September 1802, it was represented to us by Mr. Henshaw, in his capacity of Custom Master, on the 10th of September 1803, that in consequence of the failure of that season, unless a total prohibition were put to the exportation of rice, an inevitable famine would be the consequence; to which exportation he was accordingly authorised to put a stop, till further orders, by the Secretary's letter to him of the same month and year, after which he continued sometimes to address the Board, but more frequently the Governor, in his separate capacity; in consequence of which the records will show that I delivered in several of his letters to myself for the opinion of Government, whilst others of them that related chiefly to the execution and details incident to the powers with which he had been invested by Government appear to have been kept in my office, and replied to, as far as necessary, under my own signature, or by my order, of which separate correspondence, as well as of those notes and intimations that I have, in the like view of expediting the duties of Mr. Henshaw's charge in the grain department, had occasion to transmit to him; copies of such as have not already come before the Board are now delivered in for record, as far as I have been able to trace and collect them; and although every line or note may not be found in this series, yet all that is in the least degree material will, I fancy, be found to be so, or otherwise Mr. Henshaw can supply any that may be here deficient: and, meanwhile, I request that <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-207" data-page="207" data-folio="195" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n206_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 195">[p. 195]</button> these may stand entered on the face of our proceedings, immediately after Mr. Henshaw's letter of the 8th of June, and having thus rendered them of record, the following narrative will be taken from the former and present materials, according to the order of dates, after generally referring to several of these separate letters or notes between me, or my Secretary, and Mr. Henshaw, and Mr. Halliday, the Superintendent of Police, that may be in the Board's recollection as standing already on record, relating chiefly to the precautions taken on the first threatening appearances of scarcity; one of which (in a letter from Mr. Henshaw of the 27th September 1803, as recorded in our proceedings of the 30th of that month) adverts to the exemption from town duties of ghee, oil, turmeric, jagree, molasses, chelee-pepper, and pawn; and that letter is farther recommendatory of the taking off of all duties on imports from Caranja and Salsette to Bombay, as well as the duties on all imports from the Continent to those Islands, and of the abolition of the petty customs on several indispensibly necessary articles, used principally by the poorer classes on this Island; Mr. Henshaw concluding on this occasion with the following expression—"In respect to myself as Custom Master, I beg to assure you I have only the public good in view, that I will labour hard to that end, nor think any sacrifice of my emoluments or time too great or ill-applied when it can be promoted thereby."</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Abolition of certain duties thereon proclaimed.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">2. In pursuance of these suggestions, an advertisement was issued on the 27th of September 1803, announcing the several exemptions suggested in Mr. Henshaw's preceding recommendations.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Superintendent of Police appointed to dispose of grain for the consumption of the Islands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">3. Mr. Henshaw having farther recommended on the same occasion that the Garrison Storekeeper should be directed to bring to sale 200 bags of rice daily, in small limited quantities, and at a certain price, a committee was appointed—of himself, the Superintendent of Police, and of Captain Moor, the Garrison Storekeeper—to report thereon, which they did in recommendation of the plan, but preferring to delegate the charge of it to the Superintendent of Police, instead of the Garrison Storekeeper, as was accordingly sanctioned on the 30th of September 1803.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Means taken to procure rice from Mangalore, &amp;c.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Honorable Company's ship <em>Elphinstone</em> sent to Bengal for rice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">4. On the 18th and 19th of September 1803 applications had been made by Government to Mr. Ravenshaw, the Collector of Mangalore, respecting the sending up rice for this market, in pursuance of which the <em>Huddart</em> was on the 12th of October dispatched thither for its conveyance; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-208" data-page="208" data-folio="196" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n207_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 196">[p. 196]</button> and under the same date Major (now Lieutenant Colonel) Malcolm, the Resident at Mysore being then at this place, wrote to his Assistant, Mr. Peile, to promote this object, by corresponding with Mr. Ravenshaw, and by encouraging the transit of grain from Mysore to Mangalore, as far as might not interfere with the arrangements made by the Honorable General Wellesley for the supplies of his Army, or other military objects in that quarter; whilst, as a farther measure of precaution, the Board determined on the 23rd of September 1803 to dispatch the <em>Elphinstone</em> to Bengal for a cargo of rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw authorised to buy rice, dealing liberally with the importers.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Allusion to the state of the treasury.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Reliance expressed in Mr. Henshaw's honor, zeal, and judgment.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">5. In my separate letter to Mr. Henshaw of the 15th of October 1803, in answer to one from him of the preceding date, I expressed to him, in view to the measures that had been thus pursued, my hope that he would now be able to keep us clear of famine, and gradually to reduce the price of rice, adding that he might buy all the rice that came in, as long as he should find it useful; giving liberal prices, so as that the importers might not be discouraged in pursuing the traffic, and taking care not to force their inclinations even in obliging them to sell to Government when they might prefer to deal with others, since we could always afford to give as much as any first purchaser from the importer; and noticing that although our treasury was exceedingly low, yet if he wanted a small sum to go on with to let me know, and he should have it, for that the importers should be paid ready cash; and concluding by an assurance that I entrusted this great and delicate commission to his honor, zeal, and judgment, and relied on experiencing the best effects from the confidence thus placed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's proposition to buy the rice imported by individuals from Mangalore.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His assurances of an honourable discharge of this duty.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Directed to keep the shops of the Superintendent of Police supplied for the local consumption.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">6. On the same date, but before he had received my last mentioned letter, Mr. Henshaw wrote another, advising that 1,200 robins of rice had just entered from Mangalore, the owner of which had sold it to the second man, provided Government permitted it, such second man having been with Mr. Henshaw, and declared his willingness to give up his purchase for two annas profit on each robin, which Mr. Henshaw recommended as being likely to produce the best consequences, as well as that all future imports during the next thirty days should be purchased in the same manner; and concluding with these words —"I will manage it for your comfort and honor, believe me." In acknowledging which I, on the same day, expressed my entire <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-209" data-page="209" data-folio="197" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n208_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 197">[p. 197]</button> approbation of his purchasing the rice then imported, and selling it in such manner as he should see the most likely to produce the greatest general relief; adding “I have told Mr. Halliday that I leave it to you to supply his shops; he will speak to you on the subject.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's farther assurances of a conscientious discharge on his part of the duty thus reposed in him, and without calling on the treasury if he could avoid it.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">7.  In his next letter of the day following, after acknowledging mine of the 15th, he writes—“I shall conscientiously endeavour to meet your wishes in the discharge of the trust you have reposed in me, and to merit your regards, and you may depend on my exerting every means to save calling on the treasury for assistance.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Explanation as to what parts of the separate correspondence will be noticed in this narrative.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">8.  Under dates the 2nd and 3rd of December 1803 there will be found in the collection of separate correspondence now delivered in some notice of payments into the treasury, and of the straitened state of our resources, which not appearing very material to the subject under consideration, need not here be recapitulated; a rule which, for the sake of requisite brevity, will be observed in respect to all the other more irrelative notices it may be found to contain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw consulted about the growing scarcity at Poona, and the proposition from that quarter to send down their Brinjarries to purchase from Bombay.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Notice of Mr. Henshaw's having recommended a proportion of the imports to be exported; and query to him thereon, with caution to him to keep an eye on the market, so as to provide for our own exterior and interior wants.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His consequent suggestion to extend our purchases.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">9.  On the 14th of December 1803 I advised Mr. Henshaw of my having heard (no doubt from the Resident) at Poona that grain was getting very scarce at that capital, and that the Government there wished to send down the Native Brinjarries to purchase for the community at that place, upon which I asked him whether any rice had yet been re-exported out of the one-third proportion that he recommended; adding “I rely on your keeping your eye on the market with the view to provide always, <em>1st</em>, for our armies above the ghauts, to the extent of General Wellesley's commission of 1,000 garce; and <em>2nd</em>, for our population here;” and thereon suggesting to him the expediency of buying another ship's cargo; in answer to which Mr. Henshaw suggested the purchasing to the extent of one lac of bags, in addition to what he had previously engaged for, in view not only to the supply of the Poona Brinjarries, but to give us the means of exerting the proper checks over the markets in Bombay, and thereby to protect the poor from (according to Mr. Henshaw's expression) “those harpies, the grain dealers.”</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-210"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="210" data-folio="198" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n209_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 198">[p. 198]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's proposal in December 1803 to extend the indulgence of export to half of the import.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">10. Under date the 24th of December 1803 Mr. Henshaw addressed to me a further letter, which in view, no doubt, to its importance, I appear to have placed on the public record, as it suggested the propriety of extending, till further orders, the indulgence of export to half of the import, which would, he observed, increase the price to what would make the trip from Bengal a gain, and not, as was then the case, a loss ; Government's acquiescence in which proposition was on the same day signified to him by the Secretary.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's application to be allowed an Assistant and Purvoe in the Grain Department.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Answer.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">11. On the 29th December 1803 Mr. Henshaw wrote to me that he had hitherto managed to bear the Honorable Company harmless as to every expense for assistance, Purvoes, &amp;c., for the Grain Department, but that he now found it pressed very hard upon him, and to become even burthensome, so as to induce him to request I would permit him to employ Mr. Whitehill to assist him, with one good Purvoe ; and as they would have very full employment, he hoped I would not think him extravagant in recommending Mr. Whitehill for a monthly allowance of 200 Rupees, and a Purvoe for one of 50 Rupees ; to which I on the same day replied that as long as there was a gentleman in the service unprovided for, I could not consent to the employment of Mr. Whitehill, but that if he (Mr. Henshaw) liked to fix on any of the young men in the line of full employment, he should be named.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rise of the price of rice in the Bazar, and Mr. Henshaw's suggestion thereon to throw more into the market.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Answer : that he was left to judge the regulation of the market.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">12. On the 22nd of January 1804 Mr. Henshaw advised me that the price of Moongee rice had risen considerably since last week, whence he judged it would be advisable to throw from 4 to 6,000 bags into the market, in order to prevent its further rise, provided I approved of the measure ; in reply to which I informed him on the same day that, as I left him to judge of the regulation of the market, I approved entirely of his intention to throw from 4 to 6,000 bags into the local consumption, by way of keeping the rice from rising too high.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Proceedings in February 1804, in view to settling a reward for Mr. Henshaw's trouble.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">13. Under date the 7th of February 1804 there appears a letter from me to Mr. Henshaw, desiring his sentiments on what might constitute an eligible means, by commission or otherwise, of extending a fair and reasonable remuneration to him and to Mr. Ravenshaw, who purchased rice for us at Mangalore, without too great <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-211" data-page="211" data-folio="199" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n210_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 199">[p. 199]</button> any answer from Mr. Henshaw till the 15th of June 1804, wherein, after referring to an intermediate report of the 23rd of April, he states the purchase made by him up to the latest date to amount to the value of 8,50,000 Rupees, observing that their future amount can be but trifling, and the “ large supplies in expectance are all upon contracts, some for small agreements recently made excepted, whose amount can (he adds) scare reach to two lacs ;” upon which data the Board recorded it as their opinion on the 15th of June that Mr. Henshaw would derive a due and sufficiently liberal reward by being allowed to charge 2½ per cent. both on his purchases and on his sales, with which Mr. Henshaw declared, in his letter of the 16th of June, his highest satisfaction, as well as at the Board’s expression, on the same occasion, of approbation of his conduct in the important trust committed to him.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">14.   But to resume the narrative according to the order of the dates.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw’s suggestion in February 1804 to send the surplus rice intended for General Welles-ley for the local consumption of Poona, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Honorable Sir Arthur Wellesly having declined the further supply of rice intended to have been sent to Poona, Mr. Henshaw proposed, in a letter to the Secretary to Government of the 16th of February, to bring the</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Answer : Government leaving to his judgment to propose the apportionment of the home sales and exports.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">same, and also the arranged supply in aid of Poona, into market in regulated portions, and for exportation to Poona and the adjacent countries, “ having (as Mr. Henshaw concludes) ever in view the objects pointed out to him by Government ;” in reply to which it was, on the 18th of the same month and year, signified to him that</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Government left to his judgment to propose the appointment of the rice to be sold here, and of the quantity to be exported, having always in view to guard the Company from loss throughout the whole concern, and on the 22nd his proposed appropriations thereof, both for home consumption and for exportation, received the sanction of Government.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His proposed export to Surat.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">15.   The next notice that appears on the public records is a letter to me from Mr. Henshaw, proposing, under date the 21st February 1804, immediate transmission</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw’s suggestion agreed to on the 22nd February 1804, reducing the privilege of the export of grain to one-fourth of the imports of rice, &amp;c. except-ing to Surat, whither one moiety might be sent.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">of 10,000 bags of rice to the Magistrate at Surat, in consequence of the great alarm that had arisen there as to the probable extent of the increasing scarcity in that quarter, to which, on the day following, the Board agreed, as well as to a farther suggestion that in his aforesaid letter Mr. Henshaw had submitted to me of a reduction of the exports to one-fourth of the imports of rice and other grain, excepting to Surat, where they should be allowed, under regulation, to the extent of one-</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">half, which orders issuing from the Board, although the letter was addressed <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-212" data-page="212" data-folio="200" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n211_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 200">[p. 200]</button> to me, comes in further illustration of the principe that I have already explained to have generally regulated my conduct in respect to my intercourse on grain subjects with Mr. Henshaw.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's report on the 13th of March of a large quantity of grain being nearly smuggled off of the Islands, and proceedings thereon.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Connection of the subject with the charge against his Purvoe in the Court.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">16. On the 13<sup>th</sup> of March 1804 Mr. Henshaw reported his detention of a large quantity of grain on the eve of being smuggled off of the Island, such as rendered it liable to forfeiture; but as he truly thought the proprietors were not aware of the crime they committed, he proposed levying a fine of half a rupee per bag on the grain in question, which was agreed to on the 15th of the same month and year, accompanied by a requisition to the Custom Master to submit a more detailed report on the subject, which as he omitted to make till the circumstances became an object of animadversion in the late trial of his Purvoe, Appajee Bhow, in the Court of Oyer and Terminer, this matter remains yet, in some degree, undetermined.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Renewed notice from Poona of the scarcity.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Desire expressed to obtain assistance from Bombay.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Answer.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Instructions thereon to Mr. Henshaw.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Publication of the 30th of March 1804, permitting the exportation to the Continent of one moiety of all grain imported from the northward, and of one quarter of all imports from the southward.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">17. Under date the 24th of March 1804 a letter was received from Mr. Waring, the Assistant Secretary at Poona, signifying that His Highness the Paishwa had intimated the most earnest and anxious desire that the prohibition on the exportation of grain from the Island of Bombay should, if practicable, be withdrawn for the purpose of affording some supplies to that place, where numbers were dying daily from actual want; in acknowledging which communication the Secretary was, under date the 30th of March, instructed to advise Mr. Waring that there had not been any general prohibition on the exportation of grain from Bombay, which was allowed to be exported in certain specified proportions, the regulating or superintending of which was necessarily left to the Custom Master, who was at the same time informed of this correspondence with Mr. Waring, with instructions to be attentive that the purport of this notice to him should be attended to, so as to have due operation within the present prescribed limits of exportation in favour of the urgent necessities of the population at Poona; and a public advertisement was at the same time issued, announcing that Government had, until further orders, been pleased to permit the exportation to the Continent of one moiety of all grain imported by individuals from the northward, and of one quarter of all <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-213" data-page="213" data-folio="201" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n212_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 201">[p. 201]</button> which proportions permits would be granted on application to the Custom Master.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's report on the 3rd of April 1804 that 1,40,000 bags of rice might be exported to Poona.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Consequent order of the 5th of April 1804 to allow the Paishwa's Vakeels and the Poona Agents to export at once the amount of their purchases.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw directed to reserve always in store 50,000 bags over and above every order or permission to export.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">18. In acknowledging the above instructions, Mr. Henshaw reported, on the 3rd of April 1804, that the stock on hand and ascertained expectancies enabled him to suggest that, unless very unforeseen circumstances arose to prevent it, an exportation would be permitted to Poona of 1,40,000 bags of rice, of which Mr. Waring was accordingly advised on the 5th of the same month (through Amerchund Bedurchund, who had brought his letter); the Custom Master being at the same time informed that, as his report had shown so considerable a surplus of rice to be applicable for the relief of Poona, the Governor in Council was pleased to direct that he allow the Paishwa's Vakeels and the ascertained Agents from Poona to export at once what they had already purchased, provided our actual stock on hand would safely admit thereof, and noting it as so much in the whole quantity that he had calculated to be allowed for export to that capital that would thus experience some immediate relief; and on the 6th of April the Custom Master was, in continuation of the same general subject, desired to keep in store a quantity of not less than 50,000 bags, understanding that the exports already authorised were to take place from what was surplus to that quantity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Committee proposed to be appointed the better to ensure this last object.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Irritation and solemn declaration of Mr. Henshaw on that occasion.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Idea of the committee thereon not prosecuted.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">19. For greater security in that respect Government thought fit, on the date last quoted, to appoint a committee, composed of Mr. Cowart and Captain Moor, to make a monthly report of the quantity of rice under the charge of the Custom Master, which irritated Mr. Henshaw, who observes, in his letter of the 13th of April, that he thence views himself as a suspected character, and that it had caused him to "review the proceedings had since he was first intrusted with this delicate and important commission : the orders" (adds this gentleman) "transmitted to me, with their replies, have received a close revision from me; the purchases, the sales made by me, my cash transactions, the expenses I have incurred on account of the trust, all have passed a close and conscientious investigation, and I feel I should be wanting to myself if I for a moment longer scrupled to avow, in this most public manner, that I have been actuated solely by an anxious unremitted zeal for the public good; nor for a moment ever suffered a private <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-214" data-page="214" data-folio="202" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n213_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 202">[p. 202]</button> consideration to influence or draw me aside from that great object; <em>that I have neither derived for myself, or suffered others, directly or indirectly, to derive the smallest advantage from the trust reposed in me, or confided by me to them; that I have violated no order of Government, or ever presumed to act upon the suggestions of my own mind, unless first sanctioned by their approval of the measure</em>; this avowal thus publicly made, I must hope that the Honorable the Governor in Council will not be displeased at my humble request to be relieved from making any comment or remark on the reference, but will do me the justice to believe that the same zeal that first prompted me to offer my assistance to Government in this department still occupies my best faculties to the total exclusion of every other object;" which representation had such an effect upon Government that they dispensed with the institution of the Commission in question.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Free trade between Salsette and Bombay.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Complaint of Amerchund Boderchund, a merchant, of not being permitted to export his Poona purchases.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Stress laid by Mr. Henshaw on the Government Regulations.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Orders thereon.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">20. On the 27th of April 1803 it was notified to Mr. Henshaw by the Secretary to Government, in corroboration of a former order, that there was to be a free trade between Salsette and Bombay, unburthened with tolls or customs on rice, or any other article. On the 9th of June Mr. Henshaw delivered in his answer, dated the day preceding, expressing equal surprise and concern to perceive his conduct had been construed into an indisposition to let the charge against Abajee Gunnesh have all the necessary elucidation, as in the instances of the insufficient copy of the grain cash book and the confused state of the pass notes; observing, as to the first article, that the copy of the account in question is a faithful transcript from the original one, and that had he delivered any other he must have been blamed; and with respect to the pass notes they are, he remarks, vouchers necessary only for twenty-four hours, adding that they were formerly burnt once a month; and had in the present instance been preserved in the desk of a Purvoe, whence they were delivered, on being called for by Mr. Thriepland, in the state they were found.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">60. Was he (adds he) to consider the many and repeated instances of his possessing the Honorable the Governor's unlimited confidence, as expressed to him both by note and personally, as indicative of any curtailment of his former authority? Did not (continues Mr. Henshaw) these circumstances, on the contrary, fully confirm to me in the grain department all that discretionary authority already vested in me as Custom Master by the 4th Clause of Section VIII. of the Regulations above quoted?</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's avowal of having granted the irregular pass notes in reward for Abajee's services in the Grain Department.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">61. It is on this occasion asserted by Mr. Henshaw that at the period of granting the two and twenty irregular pass notes distinctly animadverted on in Mr. Thriepland's report, he (the Custom Master) had not been provided with the means of rewarding the very able services of Abajee Gunnesh in the grain department, nor the very efficient assistance which he more than once enabled him to render to the Public Treasury, and that having desired the said Abajee to point out to him any means that occurred whereby he (the Custom Master) might be enabled <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-233" data-page="233" data-folio="221" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n232_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 221">[p. 221]</button> to reward his (Abajée's services), the latter represented that the calls for grain for the adjoining continent were great, and that Government sold large quantities for exportation at a considerable profit ; that two merchants had spoken to him who had large stocks of grain on hand, but no privilege to export, and which they could not afford to sell so cheap as the Honourable Company did theirs for the consumption of the inhabitants of this Island, they had therefore applied to him, if he could procure permission to export the grain, they would allow him some of the profits they might derive thereby ; to this (continuing in Mr. Henshaw's words) I consented, and gave him the privilege to export the grain mentioned in the chits, conceiving thereby that I neither violated the spirit of any existing Regulation, or departed in the smallest instance from any of the great objects of Government confided to my management, and not without having first satisfied myself that the state of the markets could not be in any respect influenced thereby. If therefore (remarks the Custom Master) any blame can attach in consequence of these notes, that blame must attach to me alone, since I granted them to Abajee Gunnesh that he might share in the profit of exporting the grain as some remuneration for his important services in the grain department.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mr. Henshaw's representation of the great meritoriousness of his own conduct in the advising and providing against the threatened famine.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">62. Mr. Henshaw next proceeds to explain, in what seems a satisfactory manner, the rules observed by him in respect to the charging of interest on his sales of grain, and afterwards replies to the imputation conveyed in Mr. Thriepland's report as to the tearing out a leaf in one of the books of the Custom House, which book, he <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-234" data-page="234" data-folio="222" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n233_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 222">[p. 222]</button> says, was not of record, but merely a rough account, from which that of record is afterwards made; nor was it, he adds, a book called for by the Company’s Counsel, but delivered of the Custom Master’s own accord to the Company’s Counsel, and that as a rough or memorandum book, he will venture to affirm, it will be found, on inspection, to contain fewer alterations or erasures, than any other similar rough book belonging to any department of the service.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">64. Mr. Henshaw thereafter begs leave to submit some few questions to the Honorable the Governor in Council as intimately connected with the present subject, and on whose solutions by him I have (he adds) no hesitation of submitting the justice of my case, and my pretensions to his liberal construction of my proceedings throughout.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Queries proposed by Mr. Henshaw to the Governor and to Government.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>First</em>.—Did not every measure of limitation of export or of change in the system originate with me as the head of the department?</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Second</em>.—Was not the small portion of private grain allowed for export ever thought by the Honorable the Governor a hardship on the merchant, yet did he not, on my assurances of its being indispensible to ensure success to the great object in view, reject all their applications to remove the embargo, or increase the quantity for export?</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Third</em>.—The grand objects once secured, did not the Honourable the Governor continually press upon my attention to increase the exports to the utmost possible extent?</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Fourth</em>.—And, in the event I had ever pointed out to Government the expediency of quieting the merchants by a discretionary private indulgence to export any additional quantity, would not Government have acceded thereto, if satisfied every grand object was secured, and that no evil could result therefrom?</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Allusion by Mr. Henshaw to certain reports said to originate with Abajee Gunnesh.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">65. Having thus comprised within as short a compass as the nature and extent of the matter at issue would admit of a summary of all the proceedings that have appeared to me to be in the least material to guide to a judgment on the general subject, I should next offer my sentiments on the principal heads into which it naturally divides itself.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">As to the Cash Book for March.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">67. With respect to the insufficiency of the Grain Cash Book for March, Mr. Henshaw avers it to be a faithful transcript of the original one, finally closed by him many months before; whilst by Mr. Thriepland it is asserted that the book from whence this copy was taken was much more full and explicit as to details, differences that can only be reconciled, or clucidated, by a reference to the documents referred to.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">As to the manner of keeping the Pass Notes.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">68. Mr. Henshaw's explanation respecting the pass notes is not altogether satisfactory, since, whatever may have been the former practice, these notes are essential, and should as such be preserved as vouchers to the public accounts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Whether Mr. Henshaw's powers authorised his rewarding Abajee Gunnesh by the grant of irregular Pass Notes.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">69. Respecting the authority of Clause IV. Section VIII. Regulation VI. of 1799, which Mr. Henshaw notices as giving him, conjoined to the Board's and my own confidence, all the former authority of the Custom Master in respect to the export of grain, it must be within that gentleman's knowledge that the specific authority in question stood expressly withdrawn by the formal Resolution of Government of the 3rd of September 1802; at the same time that, admitting it to have been virtually restored on Mr. Henshaw's being again vested with the charge of the grain department, neither its letter nor spirit joined, even to the amplest confidence of the Governor, or of Government, can justify the mode thus taken to reward Abajee, which is, and must ever from its nature be pregnant with too many evil consequences to require to be more than mentioned to be reprobated. My notes and separate correspondence with Mr. Henshaw on this occasion (all, or all that is essential, in which, I am happy to believe, I have with more than ordinary care preserved) will show that I did indeed place in him very great, though quite unbounded, confidence; but inasmuch as I have done so the more aggravated fault would be his should that gentleman now, or hereafter, turn out to have made an <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-236" data-page="236" data-folio="224" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n235_w1400.jpg" class="prose-p">70: On that part of Mr. Henshaw's defence which respects the twenty-two irregular pass notes, I need scarcely remark that it will be found at direct variance with whatever Abajee
