<span id="page-241" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><h2 class="sec-h1">SEPARATE CORRESPONDENCE BETWEEN THE GOVERNOR AND MR. HENSHAW, FROM OCTOBER 1803 TO MARCH 1805.</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—Under every view of our situation, I conclude that from six to seven Rupees per bag may be offered for good clean cargo rice deliverable into the Honorable Company's warehouses in Bombay between the 15th December and 15th February.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I conceive that the best Mangalore rice cannot stand the Honorable Company in more than 18 Pagodas per corge, or Rs. 3-1-71 per bag at Mangalore, to which add even Rs. 1-2-0 per bag freight, it only comes to Rs. 4-3-71 per bag.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I mention this for your information and determination as to the quantity you may contract for of Bengal rice. Being with every sentiment of respect.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Honorable Sir, Yours, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) ROBERT HENSHAW.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>14th October 1803.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>15th October 1803.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">ROBERT HENSHAW, <span class="sc">Esq.</span></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—I hope you will now be able to keep us clear of famine, and gradually to reduce the price of rice. You must not, however, rely on the <em>Sadry</em>, as she unaccountably slipt away and proceeded to Calicut without Mr. Ravenshaw's permission. But what was intended for her will serve to fill up the <em>Huddart</em>. You may buy all the rice that comes in, as long as you find it useful, giving a liberal price, so that the importers may not be discouraged from pursuing the traffic, and taking care not to force their inclinations, even in obliging them to sell to Government where they may prefer to deal with others; but we can always afford to give as much as any first purchaser from the importer. Our treasury is exceedingly low, but if you want a small sum to go on with, let me know, and you shall have it. The importers should be paid ready cash. In short, I trust this great and delicate commission to your honor, zeal, and judgment, and rely on experiencing the best effects from the confidence thus placed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, yours,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-242"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="242" data-folio="230" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n241_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 230">[p. 230]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>15th October</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—Twelve hundred robins of rice are just entered from Mangalore. The owner has sold it to the <em>second</em> man at 5-2-50 the robin of best sort, and 4-2-50 the robin of the second sort. This sale was conditional that Government permitted it; the second man has been with me, and is willing to give up for two annas each robin profit; allow me, good Sir, to suggest that this be purchased for the Company, and instantly retailed; the best of consequences will result therefrom, and, if you see no solid objection, I would recommend all future imports during the next thirty days to be purchased in the same manner. I will manage it for your comfort and honour. Believe me,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Honorable Sir, Yours, &amp;c., (Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>15th October</em> 1803.</h4><h4 class="sec-h3">R. HENSHAW, <span class="sc">Esq</span>.,</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—I entirely approve of your purchasing the rice now imported, and selling it in such manner as you shall see most likely to produce the greatest general relief. I have told Mr. Halliday that I leave it to you to supply his shops. He will speak to you on the subject.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, yours, (Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>16th October</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—I was honored with your letter yesterday evening, and being in the carriage for an airing, I delayed its immediate reply.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I assure myself that every possibility of famine is now completely guarded against, and am happy to report to you that the entries of new Batta from the Mahratta countries, of rice from Canara and Malabar yesterday and this forenoon, meet my most sanguine expectations. In addition thereto I have notice of near one thousand candies of bajree being partly embarked, and ready to be embarked at Verowal, Gogo, Guzerat, Mangalore, and other Northern Ports for this place, and of which a small portion in a medley cargo of wheat, gram, dholl, &amp;c., is this instant entered. I shall conscientiously endeavour to meet your wishes in the discharge of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-243" data-page="243" data-folio="231" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n242_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 231">[p. 231]</button> trust you have reposed in me, and to merit your regards, and you may depend upon my exerting every means to save calling on the Treasury for assistance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The robin or morah of Mangalore weighs, when first packed, eighty-two No. pounds of rice, and from innumerable experiments made by me during my long residence in Canara, two thereof equalled one Bengal bag of two Bengal maunds (Factory, not Bazaar maunds). The Canara candy is exactly the same as that of Bombay, of twenty maunds each of twenty-eight pounds. The cargo of rice varies as to number of robins; to the Northward of Cundapore it consists of thirty robins; but the quantity of rice contained in the thirty equals exactly that in the cargo of forty-two robins, which measure prevails at Cundapore and to the southward.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Honorable Sir, Yours, &amp;c., (Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>2nd December</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—I have a very large supply of copper pice received from Mr. Halliday, the produce of the sales of the rice at the Sirkar's shops, amounting to Rupees 20,000. This pice is only to be exchanged for a loss of two or three per cent., and the merchants from whom I purchased rice will not receive it, but require gold or silver to avoid loss. Could it not be received into the Treasury, and gold delivered me in return, to enable me to discharge the rice bills? I submit this for your determination: being with all respect.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Honorable Sir, Yours, &amp;c., (Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>2nd December</em> 1803.</h4><h4 class="sec-h3">ROBERT HENSHAW, Esq.,</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—Let the pice be sent to the Treasury. It is a pity you kept it till it had so far accumulated as to render it inconvenient to return the whole in gold immediately, but you shall receive a few thousands at a time as you may want.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">You should pay in every rupee or pice you can command into the Treasury, either from the sale of grain or other resource, such as the Customs, knowing how hardly we are run for cash.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-244"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="244" data-folio="232" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n243_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 232">[p. 232]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I thought you were to pay for the grain of Syed Seif's ship by Bills on Bengal. That would be desirable, as we should then have the money it fetches to dispose of here.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We have written to Mr. Ravenshaw as you suggested, but will he have tonnage to send it up, or must we furnish it hence ? In that case you will seasonably consider of the means of doing so, and always keep rice enough in hand, to have the supplies to Panwell (for our army there acting against Scindia) in constant flow. Let, in short, nothing interrupt them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, yours, (Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>3rd December</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—I had the honor of your note late yesterday, or would have instantly replied thereto. I beg you will be assured that I have never lost sight of the distress your Treasury is in, and to that alone is to be attributed the accumulation of the pice, which until these few days could be exchanged without more loss than ¼ or ½ per cent., and which the grain merchants took at par. My anxiety not to trouble you made me keep it in hopes the whole sum might be passed in payment, and yesterday evening, subsequent to my note to you, I paid away seven thousand Rupees, and have only thirteen thousand, which is going to the Treasury, and as I may find call for it, will apply for gold in lieu.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Our receipts in the Custom House are principally in Treasury notes, but you may rely on their being paid in on their receipt.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">My agreement with Syed Seif's ship's Nacquedah was for sixty thousand Rupees in Bengal Bills, and the remainder in cash here. ·</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I shall in the course of Monday submit to your inspection a statement of the purchase, receipt from Canara, and issues of rice, since the 15th October to this day inclusive, and also statement of batty and wheat received from the northward, and in future keep you fully advised thereon.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I shall pay every attention to your suggestions as to the means of transporting the Canara rice here, and submit my reflections to your judgment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Captain Moor has received from me for the army 9,000 bags of Bengal rice and 18,000 robins of Canara rice, and you may rely on every strenuous co-operation in part with him to preserve in full the supply of grain to the dispatch at Camp.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I have the honor to be, &amp;c. (Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-245"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="245" data-folio="233" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n244_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 233">[p. 233]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>7th December</em> 1803.</h4><h4 class="sec-h3">H. SHANK, Esq.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Shank</span>,—I pray you state to the Governor that I could not correctly till this morning answer his query as to the price at which good Bengal Moonghy rice is to be purchased here: some few thousand bags may be now purchased for five Rupees, two quarters, and twenty-five reas (5-2-25), exclusive of brokerage, payable in Bengal Bills at the exchange of the day; but I would not wish Government to beat down the price below six Rupees per bag, at which price if we can keep the bag there will be no hazard of a want, as it affords the trader a fair gain, and will prove inducement sufficient to bring it to market: whereas, if lower, the stimulus is destroyed, and the intelligence now reaching Bengal, not another vessel will load for this with grain. If the Governor sees no objection, I would suggest my now purchasing what good Bengal Moonghy rice offers for sale at six or under six for Bengal Bills, and this, I think, will be attended with the best effects.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Yours truly, (Signed) R. HENSHAW. .</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>14th December</em> 1803.</h4><h4 class="sec-h3">ROBERT HENSHAW, Esq.,</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—I do not find that any boats are arriving from Mr. Ravenshaw. Perhaps it would be best to hire a vessel at once and send down there. I wait, however, your opinion. They write from Poona that grain is getting very scarce there, and they wish to send down the native Brinjaries to purchase here for the community of that place. Has any rice been yet re-exported out of the one-third proportion that you recommended? I rely on your keeping your eye on the market with a view to provide always—first, for our armies above the Ghauts to the extent of General Wellesley's commission of 1,000 garce; and secondly, for our population here. Perhaps it would not be amiss to buy even now another ship's cargo, provided they will accept payment by Bills on Bengal at the exchange of the day, <em>i.e.</em> the Company's and not the Bazaar exchange.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, yours, (Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-246"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="246" data-folio="234" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n245_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 234">[p. 234]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>16th December</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—I have reflected very seriously on what you mentioned in your last note to me of the scarcity at Poona; if you are satisfied as to the truth thereof, and convinced that the Government there will expect your sanction for their Brinjaries purchasing grain here, I am, on every view of this matter, convinced that it will be no more than barely prudent in Government to purchase to the extent of one lac of bags, if to be done at six Rupees the bag of Moonghy rice, and so in proportion of other grain—this independent of prior arrangements. By this measure the market will be sure to be kept supplied to answer every emergency, and Government will by having such store reserve to themselves the proper check over the markets and prevent monopolizing, having the means at any time when they see fit to lower the price, and thereby protect the poor from those harpies the grain dealers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, Honorable Sir, &amp;c.,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>29th December</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—I have hitherto managed to bear the Honorable Company harmless as to every expense of Assistants, Purvoes, &amp;c., for the grain department, but I now find it presses very hard upon me, and becomes overburdensome, so as to induce me to request you will permit me to employ Mr. Whitehill to assist me, with one good Purvoe; and as they will have very full employment, I hope you will not think me extravagant in recommending Mr. W. for a monthly allowance of two hundred Rupees, and a Purvoe for one of fifty Rupees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>29th December</em> 1803.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">ROBERT HENSHAW, Esq.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—As long as there are gentlemen in the service unprovided, I cannot consent to the employment of Mr. Whitehill, but if you like to fix on any of the young men in the line of public employment, he shall be named.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, yours, . .</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-247"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="247" data-folio="235" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n246_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 235">[p. 235]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>8th January</em> 1804.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—I was in hopes to have been able to go to Town this morning to have sent in to Government a new statement of the grain wanted to the end of November next, and our ways and means. It was finished before I quitted office last night, but was not fair transcribed, neither were my observations thereon quite finished in elucidation thereof: but I am much affected with a severe headache, and unable to do any business therefrom. Our deficit by this statement (after admitting <em>every</em> ship that has laden or is expected from Bengal, and of which most accurate mention is made) will be ninety-three thousand bags, and I write this principally to suggest the expediency of engaging with any ships that may be now or soon leaving this for Bengal for a full returning cargo of good Moonghy rice, and I would further suggest it to be done at seven Rupees per bag, payable on delivery in Bengal Bills at the exchange of the day. The <em>Tay</em> is the only ship now on the wing, and, if you approve, I will arrange to-morrow with Captain Meek if to be done, and advise you the result.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">You will, I am confident, be fully satisfied with the accuracy of my statement, but apprehensive the <em>Tay</em> may sail, and unwilling to lose the opportunity of so good a ship, I thought it advisable to write you on the subject.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, Honourable Sir, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>8th January</em> 1804.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">ROBERT HENSHAW, Esq.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—We are under an implied engagement to take whatever more rice we may require from the house of B. F. &amp; Co., and Forbes and Co., on the same terms as our existing positive engagement with them of which you have been advised.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am yours,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>22nd Junuary</em> 1804.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—The price of Moonghy rice has risen considerably within this last week. I judge it would be advisable to throw from four to six thousand bags into the market, in order to prevent its farther rise, provided you approve the measure.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-248"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="248" data-folio="236" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n247_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 236">[p. 236]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To-morrow I shall apply for an order to the Sub-Treasurer to receive into the Treasury the sum of forty-thousand Rupees in cash on account of my sales of grain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I have no answer respecting the warehouses, which I am anxious to receive.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>22nd January</em> 1804.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Robert Henshaw</span>, Esq.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>,—As I leave you to judge of the regulation of the market, I approve entirely of your intention to throw from 4 to 6,000 bags into the local consumption, by way of keeping the price from rising too high.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The aid you mention to the Treasury will be highly acceptable. The Military Board have been written to about the warehouses, and their answer may be daily looked for.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>1st February</em> 1804.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Robert Henshaw</span>, Esq.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir</span>.—I feel much at a loss to account for the different results of the comparisons made here and at Poona of the Bengal and Canara rice. Perhaps the assortment or parcels whence Colonel Coleman's Committee made theirs was from the first rice despatched by Mr. Walkenshaw, whilst yours may have been from the last: the former consisted, I fancy, of old rice not very good, but still sound; the latter may be the new rice of the growth of the late harvest, or otherwise to account for so great a difference I should suspect something unfair was done to the grain on its passage from Bombay to Poona. Your further opinion will therefore oblige</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Yours,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-249"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="249" data-folio="237" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n248_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 237">[p. 237]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>2nd February</em> 1805.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Honorable Sir</span>,—Though I personally submitted my sentiments to you this morning on the subject of your note of yesterday, yet, conceiving you would prefer having them on paper, I will here recapitulate them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Adverting to the report from Colonel Coleman on the subject of the Canara rice in the Depôt at Poona, and also to the trials made by me of the same sort of grain, as stated in my address to Government of the 30th ultimo, the enormous loss resulting to the Honorable Company on the comparison, calls, as I humbly conceive, on the part of this Government, for still further investigation of the subject, to ascertain, if possible, how this loss arises, and on whom the responsibility should lay to account for such glaring deficiency; the first step to which I presume to suggest should be to call upon the Garrison Storekeeper for his opinion, furnishing him at the same time copies of the letter from Colonel Coleman, of the order to me, and of my report to Government in reply under date the 30th ultimo, directing him at the same time to state if any, and what, progress the Canara rice undergoes when he repacks it to forward it to the Depôt, with every minute information in his power that can possibly lead to a discovery whence this great deficiency can arise.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This answer received, I would then appoint a committee, transmitting to them all the before mentioned papers, together with the report from the Garrison Storekeeper, directing them to make a series of trials with every sort of Canara rice in store, to ascertain the extent of every possible loss by tare, dust, &amp;c., and to report their opinion as to the causes that could operate to produce such a glaring deficiency as appears by the letters from Colonel Coleman to be reported to him on the Canara rice in the Depôt at Poona.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Military Board, if I may suggest, should be this Committee, and myself and the Garrison Storekeeper directed to attend to any requisition they may make, or to attend them, if they call on us, when they go through the series of trials.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">By thus pursuing this investigation, Government will stand fully justified, and exonerated from every shadow of censure and of want of attention to the calls of the Army; and if the deficiency proves to be really as reported, the proceedings had here and forwarded to General Wellesley will serve as grand leading marks for a further investigation at Poona, and lead most probably to a discovery of the real cause of the deficiency.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am, &amp;c.,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) R. HENSHAW.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-250"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="250" data-folio="238" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924091209449/page/n249_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 238">[p. 238]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>7th February</em> 1804.</h4><h4 class="sec-h3">R. HENSHAW, <span class="sc">Esq.</span></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Dear Sir,</span>—I enclose for your private information the letter I have yesterday received from Mr. Ravenshaw, of whose pretensions I am, in an equitable point of view, inclined to think favorably, but by granting two commissions to you and to him, I apprehend that we shall either too much enhance the value of the rice to the consumer, or subject the Company to loss, both which I wish to avoid. I will, therefore, be glad to receive your sentiments on what may constitute an eligible means of fair and reasonable remuneration to both of you, without too great enhancement of the price of the article. Now that more Canara rice is come up, I wish the sepoys to have their rations from it at the usual price to them, supposing it will cover the Company's cost and charges.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am yours, (Signed) J. DUNCAN.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>17th February</em> 1804.</h4><h4 class="sec-h3">R. Seeing that it was for the benefit of the Honorable Company, as well as for the good of the community at large, I obeyed his commands, and continued the receipts and issues of grain accordingly; the sales effected and issues from January to April last amounting to Rupees 11,55,500,—a net profit clear of all charges of Rs. 2,10,000 accrued to the Honorable Company in four months which will appear by the books balanced to the 30th April last; Matters being thus circumstanced, and conceiving the best expedient to resort to for the purpose of raising the sum required was to borrow money from the Shroffs in the bazaar, I accordingly applied to such of them as I was interested with for the loan of about fifty thousand Rupees, promising to repay the same to them in the course of fifteen days by a further sale of grain, but not being able, owing to a general scarcity of cash in town, immediately to procure that sum, I then had recourse to Ramdass Munoherdass, and having intreated him particularly to assist me on the present occasion with any sum he could spare to fulfil my promise to my employer, I could only obtain from him the sum of 20,000 Rupees, which having received from him on executing a receipt in my own name, I sent it immediately to the Honorable Company's Treasury, to make up the sum of of 2 lacs of Rupees which Mr. Henshaw promised. This transaction relative to the raising of this loan will appear in the Grain Cash Book, and I beg leave to refer your Honour to the same for your satisfaction, that no imputation might attach to me in this correct statement thereof;
