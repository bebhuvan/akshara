<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-81"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="81" data-folio="57" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n80_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 57">[p. 57]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">ON THE</h3><h3 class="sec-h2">REVENUE SYSTEM OF INDIA.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1. Question. <em>By what tenure is land held in the provinces with which you are acquainted?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Answer. In the provinces of Bengal, Behar, and part of Orissa (<em>Midnapoor</em>), land is now held by a class of persons called <em>Zamindars</em> (<em>i. e.</em> landholders), who are entitled to perpetual hereditary possession, on condition of paying to government a certain revenue, fixed on their respective lands. This is termed the <em>Zamindary system</em>. But in the ceded and conquered provinces belonging to the Presidency of Fort William, no fixed agreement has yet been made with the <em>Zamindars</em> as to the amount of assessment. Consequently their estates are not in their own hands, but under the immediate management of government, and subject to fresh assessments from time to time at its discretion.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-82"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="82" data-folio="58" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n81_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 58">[p. 58]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the Madras Presidency, the revenue is, for the greater part, collected directly from the cultivators, (called Ryots), by the government revenue officers, according to the rate fixed on the different descriptions of land in various situations. These cultivators may retain possession as long as they pay the revenue demanded from them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">2. Q. <em>By what tenure was land held under the former government?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Under the Mohammedan government, lands were held by hereditary right on the Zamindary system (though the revenue was sometimes arbitrarily encreased); and the Zamindars were considered as having a right to their respective estates, so long as they paid the public revenue. They were at the same time responsible for any breach of the peace committed within the limits of their estates. In this manner many estates, some of which can yet be referred to, such as Vishnupoor, Nuddea, &amp;c., continued in the same family for several centuries.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">3. Q. <em>Do persons of all religious sects hold by the same tenure?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. No religious or other distinctions were observed under the former government in regard to the holding of land; at present, Europeans are interdicted by law from becoming proprietors of land, except within the jurisdiction of the British courts of law at the three presidencies, Calcutta, Madras and Bombay.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-83"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="83" data-folio="59" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n82_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 59">[p. 59]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">4. Q. <em>Are the estates most usually large or small?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. In the Bengal presidency the estates are many of them considerable, and there are many others of various smaller dimensions; but in the Madras presidency, where the revenue is collected directly from the cultivators, the district is generally divided into small farms.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">5. Q. <em>Do the proprietors cultivate their own estates, or let them to tenants?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. To the best of my knowledge, almost all the land in the Bengal presidency is let out by the proprietors in farms, on a larger or smaller scale.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">6. Q. <em>On what terms are the farms rented?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The farms are frequently rented by the <em>Zamindar</em> himself to cultivators, often on lease, for payment of a certain fixed rent, and frequently the <em>Zamindar</em> lets the whole, or a great part of his Zamindary to respectable individuals, who realize the rents from the cultivators according to the contracts previously made with them by the Zamindars, or subsequently by these middlemen.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">7. Q. <em>Does the ordinary rate of rent seem to press severely on the tenants?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. It is considered in theory that the cultivator pays half the produce to the landholder, out of which half, 10-11ths or 9-10ths constitute the revenue paid to government, and 1-10th or 1-11th the net rent of the landholder. This half of the produce is a very heavy demand upon the cultivator, after he has borne the whole expense of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-84" data-page="84" data-folio="60" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n83_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 60">[p. 60]</button> seed and labour; but in practice, under the permanent settlement since 1793, the landholders have adopted every measure to raise the rents by means of the power put into their hands.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>8. Q. <em>Under the former government had the cultivator any right in the soil to cultivate in perpetuity on paying a fixed rent not subject to be increased?</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. In former times <em>Khud-Kasht Ryots</em> (i. e. cultivators of the lands of their own village) were considered as having an absolute right to continue the possession of their lands in perpetuity on payment of a certain fixed rent, not liable to be encreased. But under an arbitrary government, without any regular administration of justice, their acknowledged rights were often trampled upon. From a reference to the laws and the histories of the country, I believe that lands in India were individual property in ancient times. The right of property seems, however, to have been violated by the Mohammedan conquerors in practice; and when the British power succeeded that of the Mohammedans, the former naturally adopted and followed up the system which was found to be in force, and they established it both in theory and practice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>9. Q. <em>Are the tenants now subjected to frequent increases of rent?</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. At the time when the permanent settlement was fixed in Bengal (1793), government recognized <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-85" data-page="85" data-folio="61" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n84_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 61">[p. 61]</button> the <em>Zamindars</em> (landholders) as having alone an unqualified proprietary right in the soil, but no such right as belonging to the cultivators (<em>Ryots</em>). (Vide Reg. I. &amp; VIII. of 1793, the foundation of the perpetual settlement.) But by Art. 2. S. 60. of Reg. VIII. of 1793, government declared, that no one should cancel the <em>Pattāhs</em> (i. e. the title deeds), fixing the rates of payment for the lands of the <em>Khud-Kasht Ryots</em> (peasants cultivating the lands of their own village), "except upon proof that they had been obtained by collusion," or "that the rents paid by them within the last three years had been below the <em>Nirkh-bundee</em> (general rate) of the <em>Purgunnah</em>," (particular part of the district where the land is situated) or "that they had obtained collusive deductions," or "upon a general measurement of the <em>Purgunnah</em> for the purpose of equalizing and correcting the assessment." In practice, however, under one or other of the preceding <em>four</em> conditions, the landholders (<em>Zamīndārs</em>), through their local influence and intrigues, easily succeeded in completely setting aside the rights, even of the <em>Khud-Kasht</em> cultivators, and encreased their rents.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">10. <em>Q. In what manner was the revenue assessed by Government upon each estate, and upon what principle at the time of the permanent settlement?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. In the province of Bengal at the time of the permanent settlement, (in 1793) the amount of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-86" data-page="86" data-folio="62" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n85_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 62">[p. 62]</button> the revenue which had been paid on each estate, (<em>Zamindary</em>) in the preceding year, was taken as a standard of assessment, subject to certain modifications. Estates (<em>Taaluks</em>) which had paid a revenue directly to Government for the twelve years previous without fluctuation, were to be assessed at that rate, and the principle of that assessment was considered to be nearly one half of the gross produce. In Behar and other places the gross amount of the rents arising from an estate was fixed upon as the rate of government assessment, allowing however a deduction of ten per cent. to the landholder (<em>Zamindar</em>), in the name of proprietors dues (<em>Malikanah</em>), and also something for the expense of collecting the rents, &amp;c. In the upper provinces attached to the Bengal presidency, as before observed, no settlement has yet been concluded with the Zamindars (<em>landholders</em>.) The estates (<em>Zamindarys</em>) are sometimes let out by government to the highest bidder, to farmers of revenue on leases of a few years, and in other cases the rents are collected from the cultivators by the government officers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">11. <span class="sc">Q.</span> <em>On what principle do the proprietors of land regulate the rate of rent paid by the tenants?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">A.</span> The different fields or plots of ground on an estate are classed into 1st, 2d, 3d, and 4th quality, and certain rates per <em>bigah</em> (a well known land measure in India) are affixed to them respectively, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-87" data-page="87" data-folio="63" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n86_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 63">[p. 63]</button> agreeable to the established rates in the district. These rates are considered as a standard in settling the rent to be paid by the cultivators. But as the precise quantity of land is always liable to dispute, and fields may be classed in the first, second, third, or fourth quality according to the discretion of the Zamindars or government surveyors, and the measurement is also liable to variation through the ignorance, ill will, or intentional errors of the measurers—there is <em>in practice</em> no fixed standard to afford security to the cultivators for the rate or amount of rent demandable from them, although such a standard is laid down <em>in theory</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">12. Q. <em>Is the rent any specific proportion of the gross produce of the land?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. In theory the rent is estimated, as I before observed, at half the gross produce of the land; it is often encreased however much beyond that amount by various means; but in places peculiarly subject to have the crops destroyed by sudden inundation, or any other casualty, villagers cultivate generally on condition of receiving half the gross produce and delivering the other half to the landlord (<em>Zamindar</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">13. Q. <em>Is the rent paid in money, in agricultural produce, or in labour?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The rent is generally paid in money, except under peculiar circumstances, when the agreement is to pay half the gross produce as rent. And it is sometimes paid by labour, when some of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-88" data-page="88" data-folio="64" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n87_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 64">[p. 64]</button> villagers enter the service of the landlord (<em>Zamindar</em>) on condition of holding certain lands in lieu of their services.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">14. Q. <em>If in money or produce, at what period of the year, and in what proportion?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The money rent is usually paid by monthly instalments, the heaviest payments being made when the harvest is realized: and the payment in produce is of course exclusively at that season.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">15. Q. <em>Is the revenue in many instances collected by government directly from the cultivators, and not from the proprietors, or any set of middlemen?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Yes; very commonly in the Madras presidency, and sometimes in the ceded and conquered upper provinces, as above observed (Question 10.) Also when lands advertized for sale, in order to realize arrears of revenue, do not find purchasers, they may remain temporarily in the hands of government.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">16. Q. <em>In the event of a proprietor or cultivator falling into arrear in his instalments of revenue, what means are adopted by the government for realizing it?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Various modes have been adopted, but the usual mode now followed, with respect to landholders (<em>Zamindars</em>) is, that at the expiration of every third month of the revenue year, should any balance of revenue remain unpaid, the estate in arrear may be advertized for sale.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-89"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="89" data-folio="65" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n88_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 65">[p. 65]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">17. Q. <em>Is the person of the proprietor liable to be arrested for the revenue?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Should the arrear of revenue due not be realized by the sale of the estate, the person of the proprietor may be seized.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">18. Q. <em>What proportion of the revenue may fall into arrear in one year, or what proportion of the land may be subject to legal process by the public authorities for its recovery?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Perhaps two fifths, or one half of the whole revenue are usually in arrear, on an average, taking the whole year round, and more than one half of the estates are advertized for sale every year, but comparatively few are actually sold, as many of the proprietors contrive, when pressed by necessity, to raise the money by loan or otherwise.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">19. Q. <em>In the event of the tenants falling into arrear with their rent, what means do the proprietors adopt for realizing it?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. They distrain their moveable property with some exceptions by the assistance of the police officers, and get it sold by means of the judicial authorities.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">20. Q. <em>Do the courts afford the same facilities to the proprietors for recovering their rents, as to the government for realizing its revenue?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. When the revenue of an estate falls into arrear, the government by its own authority sells the property. But the proprietor cannot sell the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-90" data-page="90" data-folio="66" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n89_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 66">[p. 66]</button> property of a cultivator, except by the means of the judicial authority, which however generally expedites the recovery of such balances.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">21 Q. <em>In the event of a sale of land for revenue, what mode does the collector adopt in bringing it to sale?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. When, at the end of the revenue quarter or year as before explained, a balance remains due, a notice is put up in the collector's office (<em>Cutcherry</em>) announcing that the lands are to be sold, unless the balance of revenue be paid up within a certain period. On the expiration of this period the lands may be sold to the highest bidder at public auction by the collector, under the sanction of the Board of Revenue.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">22. Q. <em>What period of indulgence is given to the defaulter before the sale takes place;</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. A space of from one month to six weeks, and not less than the former period from the time of advertising, is allowed for paying up the arrears before the sale can actually take place.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">23. Q. <em>What previous warning is given to him to pay up his arrears, what length of notice of the intended sale is given to the public, and in what mode is the notice published.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. First the collector sends a written order to the defaulting landholder, demanding payment of the arrears due. Failing this, a catalogue of the various estates for sale is inserted in the government gazette, and the particulars of each are advertised <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-91" data-page="91" data-folio="67" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n90_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 67">[p. 67]</button> in the office of the collector, and of the judicial court and the Board of Revenue.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">24. Q. <em>What class of persons become the principal purchasers?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Frequently other landlords become purchasers, and sometimes the proprietors themselves in the name of a trusty agent. Sometimes persons engaged in trade, and sometimes the native revenue officers in the name of their confidential friends.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">25. Q. <em>What proportion of the land is purchased by the revenue officers?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The proportion purchased by the revenue officers is now, comparatively very small.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">26. Q. <em>Do they conduct the sales fairly or turn their official influence to their own private advantage?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. As such publicity is not given to the notices of sales as the local circumstances require, native revenue officers have sometimes an opportunity, if they choose, of effecting purchases at a reduced price; since the respectable natives in general, living in the country, are not in the habit of reading the government gazette, or of attending the public offices; and in respect to estates of which the business is transacted by agents, by a collusion with them, the estates are sometimes sold at a very low price.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">27. Q. <em>Can you suggest any plan for obviating abuses of this kind?</em></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-92"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="92" data-folio="68" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n91_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 68">[p. 68]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. 1st, The advertisements or notices of sale should first be regularly sent to the parties inter-rested at their own residences, not merely delivered to their agents. 2dly, They should be fixed up not only in the government offices, but at the chief market places and ferries (ghats) of the district ; also in those of the principal towns, such as Calcutta, Patna, Murshedabad, Benares, Cawnpore, 3dly. The police officers should be required to take care that the notices remain fixed up in all these situations from the first announcement till the period of sale. 4thly, The day and hour of sale being precisely fixed, the biddings for an estate should be allowed to go on for a specific period—not less than five minutes—that all intending purchasers may have an opportunity of making an offer ; and the lapse of that period should be determined by a proper measure of time, as a sand-glass placed on the public table for general satisfaction.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">28. Q. <em>When a cultivator fails to pay his rent, does the proprietor distrain or take possession of the tenant's moveables by his own power, or by applying to any legal authority?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Already answered. (See Ques. 19.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">29. Q. <em>Does the legal authority seize upon both the moveable and immoveable property, and the person of the tenant for his rent?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. 1st, On a summary application to the police, the moveable property of the tenant, with some exceptions, is distrained by the help of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-93" data-page="93" data-folio="69" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n92_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 69">[p. 69]</button> police officers; 2ndly, by the ordinary judicial process, the immoveable property of the tenant may be attached, and his person arrested for the recovery of the rest.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">30. Q. <em>What is the condition of the cultivator under the present Zamindary system of Bengal, and Ryotwary system of the Madras Presidency?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Under both systems the condition of the cultivators is very miserable; in the one, they are placed at the mercy of the <em>Zamindars'</em> avarice and ambition; in the other, they are subjected to the extortions and intrigues of the surveyors and other government revenue officers. I deeply compassionate both; with this difference in regard to the agricultural peasantry of Bengal, that there the landlords have met with indulgence from government in the assessment of their revenue, while no part of this indulgence is extended towards the poor cultivators. In an abundant season when the price of corn is low, the sale of their whole crops is required to meet the demands of the landholder, leaving little or nothing for seed or subsistence to the labourer or his family.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">31. Q. <em>Can you propose any plan of improving the state of the cultivators and inhabitants at large?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The new system acted upon during the last forty years, having enabled the landholders to ascertain the full measurement of the lands to their <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-94" data-page="94" data-folio="70" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n93_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 70">[p. 70]</button> own satisfaction, and by successive exactions to raise the rents of the cultivators to the utmost possible extent, the very least I can propose, and the least which government can do for bettering the condition of the peasantry, is absolutely to interdict any further increase of rent on any pretence whatsoever; particularly on no consideration to allow the present settled and recognized extent of the land to be disturbed by pretended remeasurements; as in forming the Permanent Settlement (Reg. I. of 1793. Sec. 8. Art. 1.), the government declared it to be its right and its duty to protect the cultivators as being from their situation most helpless," and "that the landlord should not be entitled to make any objection on this account." Even in the Regulation (VIII. of 1793. Sec. 60. Art. 2.), the government plainly acknowledged the principle of the <em>Khud-Kasht</em> cultivators having a perpetual right in the lands which they cultivated, and accordingly enacted, that they should not be dispossessed, or have their title deeds cancelled, except in certain specified cases applicable, of course, to that period of general settlement (1793), and not extending to a period of forty years afterwards. If government can succeed in raising a sufficient revenue otherwise by means of duties, &amp;c., or by reducing their establishments, particularly in the revenue department, they may then, in the districts where the rents are very <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-95" data-page="95" data-folio="71" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n94_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 71">[p. 71]</button> high, reduce the rents payable by the cultivators to the landholders, by allowing to the latter a proportionate reduction. On this subject I beg to refer to a paper (Appendix A.) which I drew up some time before leaving Bengal, which, with some additional hints and quotations, is subjoined.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">32. Q. <em>Are the Zamindars in the habit of farming out their estates to middlemen in order to receive their rents in an aggregate sum, authorizing the middlemen to collect the rent from under-tenants; and if so, how do the middlemen treat the cultivators?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Such middlemen are frequently employed, and are much less merciful than the <em>Zamindars</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">33. Q. <em>When the cultivators are oppressed by the Zamindars or middlemen, are the present legal authorities competent to afford redress?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The judicial authorities being few in number, and often situated at a great distance, and the landholders and middlemen being in general possessed of great local influence and pecuniary means, while the cultivators are too poor and too timid to undertake the hazardous and expensive enterprize of seeking redress, I regret to say that the legal protection of the cultivators is not at all such as could be desired.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">34. Q. <em>Can you suggest any change in the revenue or judicial system which might secure justice and protection to the cultivators against</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-96" data-page="96" data-folio="72" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n95_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 72">[p. 72]</button> <em>the oppression of the Zamindars, middlemen, or officers of government.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">A.</span> I have already suggested (see Q. 31.) that <em>no</em> further measurement or encrease of rent on any pretence whatever should be allowed; 2ndly, Public notices in the current languages of the people, stating these two points, should be stuck up in every village, and the police officers should be required to take care that these notices remain fixed up at least twelve months; and to prevent any infringement thereof, on receiving information of any attempt at remeasurement on the part of any landholder (<em>Zamindar</em>), &amp;c. 3rdly, Any native judicial commissioner for small debts (<em>Munsif</em>) who is authorized to sell distrained property for the recovery of rent, should be required not to proceed to sale unless fully satisfied that the demand of the <em>Zamindar</em> had not exceeded the rate paid in the preceding year; and if not satisfied of this, he should immediately release the property by application to the police. 4thly, That the judge or magistrate be required to hold a court one day in the week for cases of this kind, and, on finding any <em>Zamindar</em> guilty of demanding more than the rent of the preceding years, should subject such offender to a severe fine; and on discovering any police officer or native commissioner guilty of connivance or neglect, he should subject them to fine and dismissal from the service. 5thly, The judge or magistrate in each district <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-97" data-page="97" data-folio="73" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n96_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 73">[p. 73]</button> should be directed to make a tour of the district once a year, in the cold season, in order to see that the above laws and regulations for the protection of the poor peasantry are properly carried into effect. 6th, and lastly, The collector should be required to prepare a general register of all the cultivators, containing their names, their respective portions of land, and respective rents as permanently fixed according to the system proposed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">35. Q. <em>Is the condition of the cultivators improved within your recollection of the country?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. According to the best of my recollection and belief, their condition has not been improving in any degree.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">36. Q. <em>Has the condition of the proprietors of land improved under the present system of assessment?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Undoubtedly: their condition has been much improved; because, being secured by the permanent settlement against further demands of revenue, in proportion to the improvement of their estates, they have in consequence brought the waste lands into cultivation, and raised the rents of their tenantry, and thus encreased their own incomes, as well as the resources of the country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">37. Q. <em>Has the government sustained any loss by concluding the permanent settlement of</em> 1793 <em>in Bengal, Behar, and part of Orissa, without <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-98" data-page="98" data-folio="74" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n97_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 74">[p. 74]</button> taking more time to ascertain the net produce of the land, or waiting for further encrease of revenue.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The amount of assessment fixed on the lands of these provinces at the time of the permanent settlement (1793), was as high as had ever been assessed, and in many instances higher than had ever before been realized by the exertions of any government, Mohammedan or British. Therefore the government sacrificed nothing in concluding that settlement. If it had not been formed, the landholders (<em>Zamindars</em>) would always have taken care to prevent the revenue from encreasing by not bringing the waste lands into cultivation, and by collusive arrangements to elude further demands; while the state of the cultivators would not have been at all better than it is now. However, if the government had taken the whole estates of the country into its own hands, as in the ceded and conquered provinces, and the Madras Presidency, then, by allowing the landholders only ten per cent. on the rents (Mālikānah), and securing all the rest to the government, it might no doubt have encreased the revenue for a short time. But the whole of the landlords in the country would then have been reduced to the same wretched condition as they are at present in the ceded and conquered provinces of the Bengal Presidency, or rather annihilated, as in many parts of the Madras territory; and the whole population <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-99" data-page="99" data-folio="75" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n98_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 75">[p. 75]</button> reduced to the same level of poverty. At the same time, the temporary encrease of revenue to government under its own immediate management would also have soon fallen off, through the misconduct and negligence of the revenue officers, as shewn by innumerable instances in which the estates were kept <em>khās</em>; <em>i. e.</em> under the immediate management of government.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">38. Q. <em>Why are lands so frequently sold for arrears of revenue, and transferred from one set of hands to another?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. For ten or twelve years after the introduction of the permanent settlement, the old <em>Zamindars</em>, from adhering to their ancient habits of managing their estates by agents, and neglecting their own affairs, very soon lost a great part of their lands, and some the whole; the purchasers, by their active exertions and outlay of capital, improved many of their estates, and encreased their own fortune: but many of their heirs and successors again becoming less active and more extravagant, by rivalry with each other in nuptial entertainments, funeral rites, and other religious ceremonies, frequently run into debt, and brought their estates again into the market.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">39 and 40 Q. <em>Do the lands sold for arrears usually realize the revenue claimed by government, and fetch their full value? If not, what is the cause of the depreciation?</em></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-100"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="100" data-folio="76" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n99_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 76">[p. 76]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. They generally realize the revenue due from them; not always, however, as they are sold sometimes even below the amount of arrears due by the proprietors, owing to the want of due publicity and consequent absence of competitors; or to collusive sales of the estates as before observed (see Ans. to Quest. 26).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">41. Q. <em>After the sale of the lands, should the arrears not be realized, does the government seize upon the person of the proprietor?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Yes: the government seizes his person, and any other property government may discover him to be possessed of, is sold.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">42. Q. <em>If so, is there any limit to his confinement, except payment of the debt?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. There is no specified limit to the best of my recollection; but after government is satisfied that he has given up all his property, he may obtain his release from its humanity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">43. Q. <em>Have the cultivators any means of accumulating capital under the present system?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Certainly not: very often when grain is abundant, and therefore cheap, they are obliged, as already observed, to sell their whole produce to satisfy the demands of their landlords, and to subsist themselves by their own labour. In scarce and dear years they may be able to retain some portion of the crop to form a part of their subsistence, but by no means enough for the whole. In short, such is the melancholy condition of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-101" data-page="101" data-folio="77" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n100_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 77">[p. 77]</button> agricultural labourers, that it always gives me the greatest pain to allude to it.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>44. Q. <em>When the government makes an assessment on the fields of the cultivators by means of numerous subordinate officers, is there any effectual mode of preventing collusion, embezzlement or oppression in the valuing and measuring of the lands?</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I think it is almost impossible under that system, carried on, as it must be, by means of a vast number of individuals who are generally poor, and have no character to support. From their mismanagement not only the cultivators suffer, but ultimately the government itself, from the falling off in the revenue, under a system that at once presses down the people and exhausts the resources of the country. However, if the government would take the survey and assessment of one of the preceding years as a standard, and prevent any future measurement and assessment, it would relieve the cultivators, from the apprehension of further exactions,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-001" href="#sec-05-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> and the collector or the register of the district should be authorized to grant reduction to any cultivator subjected to overmeasurement <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-102" data-page="102" data-folio="78" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n101_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 78">[p. 78]</button> on being petitioned, and on personally ascertaining such to have occurred.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">45. Q. <em>Are collectors generally competent to superintend personally the revenue affairs of the district ?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. From the heat of the climate, and from the difficulty of transacting business in a language which is foreign to them, the collectors in general for the above reasons, must stand in need of aid from others, whom they employ as instruments in conducting the details. At the same time they have so little intercourse or acquaintance with the native inhabitants, that they must naturally depend chiefly on two or three persons who are around them, in whom they generally place confidence, and consequently these few who have no chance of bettering their condition from the trifling salaries allowed them, sometimes consult their own interests, rather than those of the government or the people.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">46. Q. <em>Are the collectors vested with sufficient power to perform effectually the duties attached to their office, or do they enjoy authority of an extent to be injurious to the public ?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Their powers are amply sufficient. The judicial authorities also are always required by the regulations of government to afford them promptly every necessary assistance in the discharge of their duties ; and many collectors are even invested with the additional office and powers of magistrates; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-103" data-page="103" data-folio="79" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n102_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 79">[p. 79]</button> contrary to the judicious system established by Lord Cornwallis, and to the common principles of justice, as they thus become at once parties and judges in their own case; consequently such powers very often prove injurious to those who attempt to maintain their own right against the claims of government, whose agents the collectors are. I much regret such a wide deviation in principle from the system of Lord Cornwallis; as I think that system, with such modifications and improvements as time may suggest, should be maintained as the basis of the revenue and judicial system of India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">47. <em>Q. Can you suggest any improvement which might secure the revenue to government and protection to the people?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. The regulations already in force are fully adequate to secure the government revenue. But to secure the people against any unjust exactions on the part of the revenue officers, I would propose, first, that the collectors should not by any means be armed with magisterial powers. Secondly, that any charge against the revenue officers should be at once investigated by the judicial courts to which they are subject, without reference to the number of cases on the file of the court, as has been the practice with regard to causes in which the collectors are prosecutors; so that both parties may have an equal chance of legal redress. This, under existing circumstances, seems to be the best <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-104" data-page="104" data-folio="80" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n103_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 80">[p. 80]</button> remedy that presents itself; but with the present system, I must repeat my fears that redress will not always be attainable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">48. Q. <em>Would it be injurious or beneficial to allow Europeans of capital to purchase estates and settle on them?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. If Europeans of character and capital were allowed to settle in the country, with the permission of the India board, or the Court of Directors, or the local government, it would greatly improve the resources of the country, and also the condition of the native inhabitants, by shewing them superior methods of cultivation, and the proper mode of treating their labourers and dependants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">49. Q. <em>Would it be advantageous, or the reverse, to admit Europeans of all descriptions to become settlers?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. Such a measure could only be regarded as adopted for the purpose of entirely supplanting the native inhabitants, and expelling them from the country. Because it is obvious that there is no resemblance between the higher and educated classes of Europeans and the lower and uneducated classes. The difference in character, opinions and sentiments between Europeans and the Indian race, particularly in social and religious matters, is so great, that the two races could not peaceably exist together, as one community, in a country conquered by the former, unless they were gradually assimilated by constant intercourse, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-105" data-page="105" data-folio="81" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n104_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 81">[p. 81]</button> continued and encreased for a long period of years, under a strong and vigorous system of police, in every village, large or small; an establishment so expensive however, that the present revenues of India could not support it. Such assimilation has in some measure taken place at Calcutta, from the daily communication of many of the respectable members of both communities. Yet even in that capital, though the seat of government, and numerous police officers are placed at almost every hundred yards, the common Europeans are often disposed to annoy the native inhabitants. By the above statement I do not mean to convey that there are not any honest and industrious persons among the European labourers. On the contrary I believe that amongst the very humblest class of society such characters are numerous. But even in justice to them, I deem it right to state that without capital, they could not, in a hot country, compete with 'the native labourers, who are accustomed to the climate, and from their very different habits of life with regard to food, clothes and lodging, can subsist on at least one sixth, if not one tenth of what is required by an European labourer. Consequently the latter would not find his situation at all improved, but the very reverse by emigrating to India.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">50. Q. <em>Would the judicial system as at present established, be sufficient to controul the European settlers in the interior of the country?</em></h4><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-106"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="106" data-folio="82" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n105_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 82">[p. 82]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. At present British-born subjects are not amenable to the Company's courts, except as regards small debts under 500 rupees (about £50) and for petty cases of assault. Consequently under the present regulations, the courts as now established, are by no means competent to exercise any adequate control over British-born subjects in the interior.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">51. Q. <em>Would it be advisable to extend the jurisdiction of the king's courts already established at the presidencies, or to augment their number; or to give greater power to the Company's judges over the European settlers?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. If the expenses attending the king's courts could be reduced to a level with the costs of the Company's courts, it would be useful and desirable to encrease the number of such courts to the same extent as that of the Company's courts of appeal at present; if Europeans of respectability are permitted freely to settle in the interior. But should such reduction of expense be impracticable, it seems necessary in that event to extend the power of the Company's courts under the judicial servants of the Company. In the latter case these judicial servants should be regularly educated as barristers in the principle of British law; or the British settlers must consent to be subject to the present description of judicial officers, under such rules and regulations as the local government of India has established for the rest of the inhabitants <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-107" data-page="107" data-folio="83" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n106_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 83">[p. 83]</button> of the country. With regard to the extension of the jurisdiction of the king's courts already established at the presidencies, although in these courts, justice is, I think, ably administered, yet it is at an expense so enormous to the parties, and to the community, that even so wealthy a city as Calcutta is unable to support its exorbitant costs, to which two successive grand juries have called the attention of the judges without any effect.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">52. Q. <em>How would the settlement on a large scale of Europeans of capital in the country improve its resources?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. As a large sum of money is now annually drawn from India by Europeans retiring from it with the fortunes realized there, a system which would encourage Europeans of capital to become permanent settlers with their families, would necessarily greatly improve the resources of the country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">53. Q. <em>Is there any portion of land in the provinces with which you are acquainted free from public assessments?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. There is land of this description, and in some districts to considerable extent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">54. Q. <em>Have any measures been adopted by government to ascertain the validity of the title by which such lands are held free from assessment, or have any of them been resumed, and under what circumstances?</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A. In Reg. XIX. of 1793, Lord Cornwallis, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-108" data-page="108" data-folio="84" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n107_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 84">[p. 84]</button> the Governor-General in Council, directed the revenue collectors to enquire into the validity of the titles of such lands : and in case of there being any doubt as to their validity, to institute prosecutions so as to have them judicially investigated ; and in the event of the parties in possession of the land failing to establish a valid title in the court, the lands might, by a decree of the court, be resumed by the collectors on behalf of government. But the government declared, in the preamble of that regulation, that no holder of such tax-free (<em>lakiraj</em>) lands should be deprived of them, or subjected to revenue, until his title should be judicially investigated and “adjudged invalid by a final judicial decree.” However, I feel bound to add, that in 1828, by Reg. III. of that year, the revenue collector in each district was authorized to dispossess the holders of such tax-free lands by his own authority, without reference to any judicial courts, if the collector should be of opinion, after such enquiry as might satisfy himself that the title of the proprietor was not valid. It is therein enacted (Sec. 4. Art. 1.) that “such decision of the collector shall have the force and effect of a decree.” Also (Art. 2.), that “it shall not be necessary for him to transmit his proceedings to the Board of Revenue,” but “the party dispossessed might appeal ;” and by Art. 3., whether an appeal be filed or not, “that it shall and may be lawful for the collector immediately <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-109" data-page="109" data-folio="85" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/expositionofprac00ramm/page/n108_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 85">[p. 85]</button> to carry into effect his decision by attaching and assessing the lands." This regulation produced great alarm and distrust amongst the natives of Bengal, Behar, and Orissa, many of whom petitioned against the principle of one party, who lays claim to the land, dispossessing an actual possessor at his own discretion; and Lord William Bentinck, though he has not rescinded the regulation, has suspended the immediate execution of it for the present.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Signed) <span class="sc">RAMMOHUN ROY</span>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">London</span>, <em>August</em> 19, 1831.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-05-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-05-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">*.</a> <span class="fn-body">Since writing the above, I happened to meet with a gentleman from Madras, of high talents and experience, who maintained that no further measurements or assessments are at all allowed in the provinces belonging to that presidency. I felt gratified at the intelligence, and shall feel still more so to find it confirmed by the Regulations of government.</span></li></ol></section>
