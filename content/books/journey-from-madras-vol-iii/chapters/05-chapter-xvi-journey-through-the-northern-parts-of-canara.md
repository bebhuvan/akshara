Feb. 18.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">FEBRUARY</span> 18th.—I went four cosses to <em>Batuculla</em>, which means the <em>round town</em>. A very steep barren ridge separates <em>Beiduru</em> from a fine level, which is watered by the <em>Combara</em>, a small slowrunning stream, that in several places is dammed up for the irrigation of the fields. Here was formerly a market (<em>Bazar</em>) named <em>Hosso-petta</em>, which General Mathews destroyed. After passing this level, I came to a very barren country, but not remarkably hilly. It is covered with stunted trees, and intersected by a small rapid stream, the <em>Sancada-gonda</em>, and farther on by a narrow cultivated valley. <em>Batuculla</em> stands on the north bank of a small river, the <em>Sancada-holay</em>, which waters a very beautiful valley surrounded on every side by hills, and in an excellent state of cultivation. At the public expense eight dams are yearly made in order to water the rice grounds. They are constructed of earth, and are only intended to collect the stream in the dry season. In the rains they would be of no use, and the violence of the stream would then sweep away the strongest works. The dams are repaired between the 17th of November and the 16th of December, and are carried away in the two months which precede the summer solstice. There are here many coco-nut gardens, and these in the best condition of any that I have seen in <em>Canara</em>. They are well inclosed with stone walls. Their produce is partly shipped for <em>Mangalore</em>, or <em>Rája-pura</em>, and partly sent to the country above the <em>Ghats</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Batuculla.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 18.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Country called <em>Haiga</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Money levied for the support of public worship.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Batuculla</em> is a large open town containing 500 houses. It has two mosques; one of which receives from the Company an allowance of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-151" data-page="151" data-folio="131" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n150_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 131">[p. 131]</button> 100 <em>Pagodas</em>, and the other half as much. These places of worship are situated in a quarter of the town inhabited by Mussulmans alone. Many of these are wealthy, and go on commercial speculations to different parts of the coast; but this is their home, and here they leave their families. In this part of the country there are no <em>Buntar</em>, nor does the language of <em>Tulava</em> extend so far to the north. In fact, <em>Batuculla</em> is properly in a country called <em>Haiga</em>; and the most common farmers are a kind of <em>Bráhmans</em>, named <em>Haiga</em> after the country, and a low cast of <em>Hindus</em> called <em>Halepecas</em>. There are here 76 <em>Gudies</em>, or temples belonging to the followers of the <em>Vyása</em>. Last year the officers of revenue, being all <em>Bráhmans</em>, began by their own authority to levy money, under pretence of applying it to the support of these places of worship; but some of them having been flogged, and dismissed from the service, a stop was put to this dangerous practice, and the priests (<em>Pújáris</em>) must content themselves with voluntary contributions. Major Monro does not seem to have thought it necessary to be so liberal to the temples, as Major Macleod and Mr. Hurdis have been. I do not perceive that his economy has been attended with any bad effect; and his conduct, on the whole, seems to have gained the good opinion of every honest industrious man that lived under his authority.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Account of the country by the <em>Bráhmans</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 18.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Account by the Jain.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Thinking to obtain some information from the <em>Bráhmans</em> in a place where they were so numerous, I sent for some of them. They denied having ever been subject to the <em>Jain</em>, and said, that this and four other districts were each governed by an independent officer, sent immediately from <em>Nagara</em>, meaning the capital above the <em>Ghats</em>; for the present <em>Nagara</em> is a name of very recent origin. These four territories were <em>Shiraly</em>, <em>Chindawera</em>, <em>Garsopa</em>, and <em>Mirzee</em>, and each occupied the whole country from the sea to the <em>Ghats</em>. They afterwards confessed, however, that this was only during the government of the <em>Sivabhactars</em>; and that <em>Batuculla</em> formerly belonged to <em>Byra Devi</em>, a <em>Jain</em> princess, whose dominions extended <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-152" data-page="152" data-folio="132" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n151_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 132">[p. 132]</button> almost to <em>Barcuru</em>, which belonged to a <em>Jain Rájá</em> of the name of <em>Budarsu</em>. These <em>Bráhmans</em> having told me that at all their temples I should find inscriptions, I set out in search of them, and was a good deal disappointed to find none at the two chief <em>Gudies</em>; and I inquired at several others, but was informed that they had no such thing. In the course of my walk I met with two <em>Jain</em> temples of the kind called <em>Busties</em>, the only remains of sixty-eight that were formerly in the place. The one had an inscription dated in the year of <em>Sal.</em> 1468, <em>A. D.</em> 154 <sup>5</sup>/<sub>6</sub>, in the reign of <em>Runga-ráya</em>. He is not mentioned in the <em>Ráya Paditti</em>, but in the inscription is said to have been brother’s son of <em>Krishna Ráya</em>, by whom he was probably employed as a deputy. The date is toward the end of the time assigned by <em>Ramuppa</em> for the reign of <em>Krishna Ráya</em>. At the other <em>Busty</em> is an inscription, dated <em>Sal.</em> 1479, <em>A. D.</em> 155 <sup>6</sup>/<sub>7</sub>, in the reign of <em>Sri Víra Sadásiva Ráya</em>. A copy of this has been delivered to the Bengal government. From the <em>Pújári</em> of the <em>Busty</em>, one of the few <em>Jain</em> now remaining in the place, I obtained the following account.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 18. A fine stone.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">All the country between <em>Carcul</em> and <em>Cumty</em> belonged to a family of <em>Jain Rájás</em>, called by the common name of <em>Byrasu Wodears</em>; but each had a particular name, several of which the <em>Pújári</em> mentioned. The founder of this family, as we have already seen, was <em>Jenaditta</em>, a fugitive prince from the north of India. The last of these <em>Wodears</em> having no son, the greater part of his dominions was divided among his seven daughters, all of whom were called <em>Byra Devi</em>; and it is concerning them, that <em>Ferishta</em> has related an absurd fable. From these ladies <em>Barcuru</em> was taken by a <em>Jain</em> prince, whom the <em>Bráhmans</em> called <em>Budarsu</em>. The <em>Byra Devi</em> of this place built a fort, the ruins of which may still be traced. In her time the town was very large. During the war conducted by Lord Cornwallis it suffered much from a plundering band of <em>Marattahs</em>, but is again recovering fast. The <em>Pújári</em> showed me the ruins of a <em>Busty</em> built by one of the <em>Wodears</em>. The workmanship of the pillars and carving <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-153" data-page="153" data-folio="133" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n152_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 133">[p. 133]</button> is superior to any thing that I have seen in India, probably owing to the nature of the stone, which cuts better than the granite in common use, and preserves its angles better than the common pot-stone, of which many temples are constructed. The quarry is four cosses to the eastward. The stone is what Mr. Kirwan calls <em>Sienite</em> in a slaty form, and consists of hornblende slate, with layers of white quartz, and a little felspar interposed. In some pieces these are occasionally wanting, and the plates of hornblende are connected only by fibres of the same nature crossing the interstices between plate and plate. In some places again, the plates are waved, somewhat like the layers of timber at a knot, and there the quantity of quartz and felspar generally exceeds that of the hornblende.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Errors in the accounts of the <em>Bráhmans</em> and <em>Jain.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 18.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">As the <em>Bráhmans</em> err in denying their former dependance on the <em>Jain</em>, and endeavour as much as possible to conceal the former existence of such odious infidels; on the other side the <em>Jain</em> go into the contrary extreme, and deny altogether the dependance of their <em>Rájás</em> on the kings of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>, which from many inscriptions, and other circumstances, is quite indubitable. The <em>Belalla</em> family, who, till the time of <em>Vishnu Verdana Ráya’s</em> conversion, were undoubtedly <em>Jain</em>, probably governed their dominions, like other <em>Hindu</em> princes, by chiefs paying tribute, and holding their lands by military tenure. We have seen that, when their sovereign changed his religion, these chiefs threw off their allegiance, and continued in an independent anarchy, till subjected by <em>Buta Panda</em>, and soon after by <em>Hari-hara</em>. The princes of the throne of <em>Vijaya</em>-<em>nagara</em>, although favourers of the <em>Bráhmans</em> who follow <em>Vyása</em>, did not venture to dispossess the <em>Jain Rájás</em>, but employed them as their vassals, both in the civil and military government of the country. When the government at <em>Vijaya-nagara</em> became weak under <em>Sadásiva</em>, and fell into utter contempt by the death of <em>Ráma Ráya</em>, the <em>Jain Rájás</em> again asserted their independence; and in the inscription here, dated in the year 155 <sup>6</sup>⁄<sub>7</sub>, the <em>Byra Devi</em> no longer <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-154" data-page="154" data-folio="134" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n153_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 134">[p. 134]</button> acknowledges any superior. It was at this time that <em>Sadásiva Náyaka</em> of <em>Killidi</em> obtained a grant of <em>Tulava</em> from the king; and, taking advantage of the weakness of a female reign, he attacked the <em>Jain</em> without mercy. It must be observed, that the <em>Jain</em> are extremely obnoxious to the <em>Sivabhactars</em>, as they altogether deny the divinity of <em>Iswara</em>; but the <em>Bráhmans</em> who serve as priests (<em>Pújáris</em>) in his temples are favourites, although among the <em>Sivabhactars</em> they are not the order dedicated to the care of religin. In this part of the country the princes of <em>Ikeri</em> seem to have almost extirpated the <em>Jain</em>; but toward the south they met with a more obstinate resistance, and made no considerable conquests there, until the government of <em>Sivuppa</em>, who reigned from 1642 till 1670, and had the management of public affairs from about the year 1625. Even he was obliged to permit the <em>Jain Rájás</em> of the south to retain their authority as his vassals; and until the more vigorous government of <em>Hyder</em> they continued in power.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 19. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">19th <em>February.</em>—<em>Honawera</em> being too far distant for two days journey with my cattle, I went a short stage of one coss and a half to <em>Shiraly</em>. The country, after ascending the little hill above <em>Batuculla</em>, is not steep; but much of the soil is very poor, in many places the <em>Laterite</em> being almost entirely naked. In some other places the soil is very good; and, although not level, a part of it has been formed into <em>Betta</em> land for the cultivation of rice; which confirms the account given by the people of <em>Haryadiká</em>, concerning the possibility of rendering all the hills of <em>Canara</em> arable. In general, however, they are considered as not fit for this purpose. At <em>Shiraly</em> is a river called <em>Shiraly-tari</em>, which comes from a temple on the <em>Ghats</em> that is named <em>Bhimeswara</em>. The tide comes up to <em>Shiraly</em>, a mile from the sea, and forces the traveller to swim his cattle. The banks at the ferry are rather stony; but round the village, there is much rice land, and good plantations of coco-nut trees. A great quantity of salt is made in the neighbourhood. <em>Shiraly</em> is a poor village, with three or four shops.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-155"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="155" data-folio="135" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n154_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 135">[p. 135]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 20. <em>Poon</em>, or <em>Puna</em>, the <em>Inophyllum</em> of Linnæus.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">20th February.—I went three cosses to <em>Beiluru</em>, which signifies the <em>cleared place</em>, and is a common name in countries where the dialect of <em>Karnata</em> prevails. My tents were, however, pitched in a very stately grove of the <em>Calophyllum inophyllum</em>, which in this part of the country is much planted near the villages. It grows to a large size, especially in sandy places near the sea. The common lamp oil of the country is expressed from its seed, by means of a mill turned by oxen. It is here called <em>Hoingay</em>, the name by which above the <em>Ghats</em> the <em>Robinia mitis</em> is known. In <em>Tulava</em> and <em>Malayala</em> it is called <em>Puna</em>, by us commonly written <em>Poon</em>. I suspect that the <em>Poon</em> of the eastern islands is different.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">From <em>Shiraly</em> to <em>Beiluru</em> the plain, between the sea and the low hills, varies in breadth from half a mile to a mile and a half. Its soil is in general good, and almost the whole of it is cultivated for rice; but few parts yield two crops annually. The sea-shore is skirted with groves of coco-nut palms, and the view is very beautiful. This plain is only watered by two small streams, the one of which is a branch of the <em>Shiraly</em>. Among the low hills are said to be, as usual, many narrow rice vallies. About three-quarters of a coss from <em>Beiluru</em> is <em>Murodéswara</em>, a temple standing on a lofty promontory that has been fortified, and at high water is insulated by a narrow channel. To the south of the promontory is a small bay sheltered by some rocks, which appear above the water, and afford protection to boats. Near this is a small village containing shops (<em>Bazars</em>). South-west from the promontory is a peaked island, which I suppose is what our seamen call Hog Island: the natives call it <em>Jaliconda</em>. In the offing from <em>Murodéswara</em> is a very large rock; and still farther west an island, which I suppose is what the seamen call Pigeon Island. It seems to be five or six leagues from the continent, and is pretty high, with a flat top. By the natives it is called <em>Naytrany Guda</em>, which last word signifies a hill. They say, that it has trees, with a small stream of fresh water, and good landing on its western side. Its caves are frequented by many wild <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-156" data-page="156" data-folio="136" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n155_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 136">[p. 136]</button> pigeons, whence the European name is probably derived. It is frequented also by boats for coral, with which its shores abound; and they likewise supply all the neighbouring continent with quick lime.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Worship of <em>Jetiga</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">To this island many people also go to pray, offer coco-nuts, and sacrifice to a stone pillar called <em>Jetiga</em>, which represents a <em>Buta</em>, or male devil. As this spirit is supposed to destroy the boats of those who neglect him, he is chiefly worshipped by traders and fishermen. On the continent there is another pillar called <em>Jetiga</em>; but as this devil is less troublesome than the one on the island, he receives fewer marks of attention.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Beiluru</em> the inhabitants, living in scattered houses unprotected by forts, suffered much in the <em>Marattah</em> invasion; and there is not remaining above one half of the people that would be requisite to cultivate the ground. Owing to this cause, a great part of the coco-nut palms have died. A good tree is reckoned to produce annually 50 nuts. The rice lands near the sea, contrary to the common rule in <em>Malayala</em>, are reckoned more productive than those inland; but the soil here near the sea is not so sandy as that to the south, and the beach is quite firm; whereas to the south it is very heavy. The roads here are in general good; but that is entirely owing to the nature of the country, no pains having been bestowed on them by the natives. Every now and then the traveller comes to a river, hill, or rock totally impracticable for a carriage of any kind, and very difficult even for cattle that are carrying back loads.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 21.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">21st <em>February.</em>—I went four cosses to the south side of the <em>Honawera</em> lake, and encamped in a coco-nut grove close by the ferry, which is above a mile wide, and without previous notice it is impossible to procure a conveyance capable of transporting cattle. The country from <em>Beiluru</em> to <em>Cassergoda</em>, about two miles from the ferry, is one of the most barren that I ever saw. It consists of low hills of <em>Laterite</em>, which extend down to the sea, and are almost <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-157" data-page="157" data-folio="137" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n156_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 137">[p. 137]</button> destitute of soil. In some places a few stunted trees may be seen; but in general the rock is thinly scattered with tufts of grass, or of thorny plants. On the whole route there are only two narrow vallies. In these there are a few inhabitants, and a little good rice-land. On descending to <em>Cassergoda</em> the traveller enters a plain, which, after having been in the desert, looks well; but its soil is very poor, and it wants cultivators, especially to plant coco-nut palms, for which it is best fitted.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lake of <em>Honawera</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The lake is of great extent, and, like that at <em>Kunda-pura</em>, contains many islands, some of which are cultivated. It reaches almost to the <em>Ghats</em>, and in the dry season is quite salt; but it receives many small streams, which during the rainy monsoon become torrents, and render the whole fresh. By the natives it is commonly called a river, but lake is a more proper term. The lake abounds with fish; but many more are taken in the sea, and, when salted, form a considerable article of commerce with the inland country. Each fishing-boat pays annually to government from four to six <em>Rupees.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Garsopa.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Garsopa</em> is a district including all the lands on the south side of the lake, and part of those on the north. The chief town, of the same name, stood at the extremity of the lake on its south side. This is now in ruins, and ought to be distinguished from a fort of the same name above the <em>Ghats</em>, which is laid down by Major Rennell.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Honawera</em>, or <em>Onore.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Honawera</em>, or <em>Onore</em>, as we call it, was totally demolished by <em>Tippoo</em> after he had recovered it by the treaty of <em>Mangalore</em>. It was formerly a place of great commerce, and <em>Hyder</em> had established at it a dock for building ships of war. In the lake remain the wrecks of some which were sunk by our troops, after the fort was taken by assault. There is now a custom-house at the place, and some poor people have made offers of rebuilding the town if government would assist them. Five shops only have been rebuilt, and these are not in the situation of the former town. Boats now come from <em>Goa</em> and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-158" data-page="158" data-folio="138" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n157_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 138">[p. 138]</button> <em>Raja-pura</em>; and from merchants who live scattered near the bank of the lake, they purchase rice, pepper, coco-nuts, <em>Betel-nuts</em>, salt-fish, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pirates.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The piratical boats from the <em>Marattah</em> coast are a great impediment to commerce; they hover especially round Pigeon Island, and have even the impudence to enter the rivers and inlets of the coast. Eight days ago they cut out from this place two boats; fifteen days ago one boat from <em>Manky</em>; and five days previous to that a fourth from <em>Batuculla</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Fortified Island.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A little way north from the entrance into <em>Honawera</em> lake is <em>Baswa Rasa Durga</em>, called by us Fortified Island. Its works were erected by <em>Sivuppa Náyaka</em> of <em>Ikeri</em>, and it contains coco-nut palms and plantain trees, with abundance of fresh water. Boats can occasionally go to it in the south-west monsoon; I imagine that vessels might even then find shelter in the channel between it and the continent. It produces the best quality of <em>Cavi</em>, or reddle, which is used by the natives for painting their houses.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The country called <em>Haiga</em>, or <em>Haiva</em>, formerly belonging to <em>Rávana</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">All the country, as far as <em>Gaukarna</em> inclusive, is called <em>Haiga</em>, and seems formerly to have been under the influence of <em>Rávana</em>, king of <em>Lanca</em>, or <em>Ceylon</em>. <em>Tritchenopoly</em> is said to have been the station of his most northern garrison on the eastern side of the peninsula. It is probable, that on the west side his dominions extended much farther. Although a king governing the <em>Racshasa</em>, or devils, he seems to have been a pious <em>Hindu</em>; and four temples, dedicated to <em>Siva</em> in <em>Haiga</em>, are said to have been erected by him. Their names are <em>Mahaboléswara</em> at <em>Gaukarna</em>; <em>Murodéswara</em>, which I passed yesterday; <em>Shumbéswara</em>, on the south side of the lake; and <em>Daréswara</em>, half a coss from <em>Hulledy-pura</em>. He also built <em>Sujéswara</em>, which is in <em>Kankána</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 22. Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb.</em> 22.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">22d <em>February</em>.—I crossed the inlet or lake, and went two cosses to <em>Hulledy-pura</em>, where the <em>Tahsildar</em> of <em>Honawera</em> resides. The road leads over a plain of rice-ground. The soil is poor, and much intersected and spoiled by creeks containing salt-water; this, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-159" data-page="159" data-folio="139" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n158_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 139">[p. 139]</button> however, might be easily excluded by dams. <em>Hulledy-pura</em> is an open town containing 352 houses, and is situated east from a considerable creek that runs through the plain. Its present name, signifying turmeric-town, was given to it by <em>Hyder</em>; for its original appellation, <em>Handy-pura</em>, signifying hog-town, was an abomination to the Mussulman.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Feb.</em> 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">23d <em>February</em>.—I remained at <em>Hulledy-pura</em>, with a view of taking an account of the agriculture of the country, as an example of that which prevails in <em>Haiga</em>. Is found most of the cultivators to be <em>Bráhmans</em>, cunning as foxes, and much alarmed concerning my intentions in questioning them on such subjects. Great reliance, therefore, cannot be placed on what they said, especially as their answers were very contradictory.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Most of the cultivated lands in <em>Haiga</em> are private property; but the hills and forests belong to the government. Every man pays a certain <em>Shistu</em>, <em>Caicagada</em>, or land-tax, for the whole of his property <em>in cumulo</em>, and cultivates it in whatever manner he pleases. This prevents a traveller from being able to ascertain how far the tax is reasonable or oppressive. The proprietors are called <em>Mulugaras</em>, and are chiefly <em>Bráhmans</em>. Most of them cultivate their lands on their own account; but some let a part out to <em>Gayniguras</em>, or renters; for <em>Gayni</em> signifies rent. Very few are encumbered with mortgages; the <em>Bráhmans</em> of <em>Haiga</em>, like most <em>Hindus</em>, being in many respects good economists.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Size of farms, and quantity of stock.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Those who keep twenty ploughs are reckoned very wealthy; men in moderate circumstances have from four to six; but a very great number possess only one plough. The <em>Bráhmans</em> perform no labour with their own hands. One of them says, that he has four ploughs, with eight oxen, and keeps four male and four female servants. The <em>extra</em> expenses of harvest and weeding amount to 20 <em>Morays</em> of rough rice. He sows 20 <em>Morays</em> on low land, and 2 <em>Colagas</em> on hill land, and has a coco-nut garden containing 200 trees.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-160"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="160" data-folio="140" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n159_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 140">[p. 140]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 23. Allowance for slaves.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the farms of the <em>Bráhmans</em> most of the labour is performed by slaves. These people get daily 1½ <em>Hany</em> of rice: a woman receives 1 <em>Hany</em>. Each gets yearly 2½ <em>Rupees</em> worth of cloth, and they are allowed time to build a hut for themselves in the coco-nut garden. They have no other allowance, and out of this pittance must support their infants and aged people. The woman’s share is nearly 15 bushels a year, worth rather less than 14¼ <em>Rupees</em>; to this if we add her allowance for clothes, she gets 16¾ <em>Rupees</em> a year, equal to 1<em>l</em>. 16<em>s</em>. 8½<em>d</em>. The man’s allowance is 22½ bushels, or 23¾ <em>Rupees</em>, or 2<em>l</em>. 3<em>s</em>. 0½<em>d</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wages of free servants.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A male free servant, hired by the day, gets 2 <em>Hanies</em> of rice. Both work from seven in the morning until five in the evening; but at noon they are allowed half an hour to eat some victuals that are dressed in the family as part of their allowance; and every cast can eat the food which a <em>Bráhman</em> has prepared.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Leases, rent, and land-tax.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The leases granted to tenants (<em>Gaynigaras</em>) are in general for from four to ten years. For each crop of rice they pay, for every <em>Moray</em> sown, 2 <em>Morays</em> of rice for land of the first quality; 1½ for middling land; and 1 <em>Moray</em> of rice for the worst land: out of this the proprietor pays the taxes. The proprietor ought to find security for the payment of the land-tax. If he does not, a revenue officer is sent to superintend the harvest, to sell the produce, and to deduct the revenue from the proceeds. This is a miserable system, and one of a true <em>Hindustany</em> invention; as the person sent to collect the harvest received an allowance from the farmer; and thus one of the idle tatterdemalions that formed part of the clamorous suite of some great man had for a while the cravings of his appetite satisfied. If a man has given security, and fails in payment, on the third day after the term the security is called upon, and confined until the revenue is paid. The estate is never sold on account of arrears; and where the crop has failed from bad seasons, or other unavoidable causes, a deduction from the rent is generally allowed.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-161"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="161" data-folio="141" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n160_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 141">[p. 141]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 23. Value of estates.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Estates that pay 20 <em>Pagodas</em> as land-tax, sell for about 100 <em>Pagodas</em>. The same quantity of land may be mortgaged for 50 <em>Pagodas</em>. The lender gets the whole profits of the estate for interest; but, whenever the borrower pleases to repay the debt, he may resume his land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Both these circumstances, of estates being saleable, and capable of being let on mortgage, show, that they are of more value to the proprietors than what might be esteemed as an adequate reward for the labour and expense of cultivation. This is also evinced by the number of disputes that happen concerning succession. These, in the first instance, are determined by the <em>Tahsildar</em>, with the assistance of a <em>Panchaity</em>, or assembly of respectable neighbours. The decision is sent to the collector, who, as he sees reason, either confirms it finally, or investigates farther into the matter. Here a man’s sons generally divide the estate equally among them; but the eldest manages the whole, and they live all together. When it comes to be divided among a number of cousins, owing to more than one brother of a family having children, the estate is commonly let, and the rent divided.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Quantity of seed required for an acre.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I measured three fields. The first containing 76,280 square feet, was rated in the public accompts at 3 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> <em>Morays</em> sowing, which would make the seed at the rate of 2,<sup>447</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushels an acre. The next plot measured 10,135 square feet, and was said to sow 8 <em>Hanies</em>, which is at the rate of 1,<sup>4</sup>/<sub>10</sub> bushel an acre. The third plot measured 21,356 square feet, and was said to require 20 <em>Hanies</em> of seed, which is at the rate of 1,<sup>668</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushel an acre. These agree so ill, that much dependance cannot be placed on the estimate; but, having no better grounds to proceed upon, I must take the average, or 2 <sup>194</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushel as the seed required for one acre. This is nearly the same quantity with that used in the southern parts of <em>Malabar</em>; but much greater than would appear to be the case in Mr. Ravenshaw’s district.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Divisions of rice-grounds.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this neighbourhood there are three kinds of rice-ground; <em>Mackey</em>, <em>Bylu</em>, and <em>Caru</em>. The first is the higher ground, which <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-162" data-page="162" data-folio="142" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n161_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 142">[p. 142]</button> gives only one crop in the year. The <em>Bylu</em> ground gives either two crops of rice, or one of rice and one of pulse. The <em>Caru</em> in the rainy season is so deeply inundated, that it cannot then be cultivated; and in the dry season gives one crop. The crop of rice produced in the rains is called <em>Catica</em>; that which grows in the dry season is called <em>Sughi</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Quality and price of different rices.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the accompanying Table, several particulars, relative to the cultivation of rice are detailed. The rice raised on <em>Mackey</em> ground is of a very inferior quality to that raised on the lower fields, and is that which is given to slaves and day labourers. Its average price is 12 <em>Pagodas</em> a <em>Corge</em>, or 21¼ pence a bushel; while that of the other is 20 <em>Pagodas</em> a <em>Corge</em>, or 35½ pence a bushel.</p></div><div class="ledger-table-block" id="tbl-015"><div class="table-toolbar"><span class="table-id-badge">TBL-015 · FOLIO 162</span><span class="table-csv-links"><a href="/downloads/journey-from-madras-vol-iii/datasets/tables/tbl-015_p0162.csv" download>Diplomatic CSV</a> · <a href="/downloads/journey-from-madras-vol-iii/datasets/tidy/tbl-015_p0162.tidy.csv" download>Tidy CSV</a></span></div><div class="ledger-table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Statistical table" tabindex="0"><table class="ledger-table"><caption>Table explaining the cultivation of Rice at <em>Hulledy-pura</em>.</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col" rowspan="3">Kinds.</th><th scope="col" rowspan="3">Soils for which each is fitted.</th><th scope="col" rowspan="3">Crop in which each is sown.</th><th scope="col" rowspan="3">Months each requires to grow.</th><th scope="col" colspan="6">Produce after deducting Seed.</th></tr><tr><th scope="col" colspan="3">Of one <em>Moray</em> sown.</th><th scope="col" colspan="3">Of one Acre.</th></tr><tr><th scope="col">Good crop.</th><th scope="col">Middling crop.</th><th scope="col">Poor crop.</th><th scope="col">Good crop.</th><th scope="col">Middling crop.</th><th scope="col">Poor crop.</th></tr><tr><th scope="col"></th><th scope="col"></th><th scope="col"></th><th scope="col"></th><th scope="col"><em>Morays</em></th><th scope="col"><em>Morays</em></th><th scope="col"><em>Morays</em></th><th scope="col">Bushels</th><th scope="col">Bushels</th><th scope="col">Bushels</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr class="row-data"><td><em>Hany Samy</em></td><td><em>Mackey</em> - Feb. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only mode of cultivation used here for <em>Mackey</em> land is that called <em>Mola</em>, or sprouted-seed. In the month preceding, and that following the summer solstice, when the rains commence, the field is ploughed five times in the course of fifteen days, and all the while the water is confined. Before the last ploughing it is manured with dung from the cow-house. After the ploughings the field is smoothed with the <em>Noli-haligay</em>, or plank drawn by oxen (Plate XXII. Fig. 58.). It is then harrowed with the <em>Haligay</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-163" data-page="163" data-folio="143" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n162_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 143">[p. 143]</button> which is the same with the <em>Halivay</em> of <em>Seringapatam</em> (Plate IV. Fig. 9.); and at the same time roots and weeds are pulled out by the hand. The water is then allowed to run off, and the prepared seed is sown broad-cast. If in three days any rain fall, the seed is lost, and the field must be sown again. For a month the water is allowed to run off as fast as it falls, after which it is confined on the rice until the crop is ripe. At the end of one moon and a half the weeds are removed by the hand.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Management of the grain.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The straw is cut with the grain. That intended for seed is immediately thrashed, and dried seven days in the sun. That intended for eating is put in heaps for eight days, and defended from the rain by thatch. The grain is then either beaten off with a stick, or trodden by oxen; and for three days is dried in the sun. The whole is preserved in <em>Morays</em> or straw bags, and kept in the house, till it can be boiled, and cleaned from the husks; for the farmer here never sells rough rice (<em>Paddy</em>). All the grain that is cut in the rainy season is boiled, in order to facilitate the separation of the husks.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Catica</em> crop of rice on <em>Bylu</em> land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Catica</em> crop on <em>Bylu</em> land is mostly sown sprouted-seed: a very little only is transplanted. The manner of preparing the seed here is, to steep the straw bag containing it in water for an hour twice a day. In the intervals it is placed on a flat stone which stands in the house, and it is pressed down by another. The largegrained seeds require three days of this treatment, and are sown on the fourth day. The small-grained seeds are steeped two days, and sown on the third. For the <em>Catica</em> crop on <em>Bylu</em> land the five ploughings are given at the same season as for that on <em>Mackey</em> land. After the fifth ploughing the field in the course of five days is manured, and ploughed again twice, having all the while had the water confined on it. The mud is then smoothed with the rake drawn by oxen; the water is let off, and the prepared seed is sown broad-cast. It is managed afterwards exactly like the crop on <em>Mackie</em> land; and, as it ripens toward the end of the rainy season, the straw <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-164" data-page="164" data-folio="144" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n163_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 144">[p. 144]</button> is in general well preserved. The rice however, to enable the husks to be easily removed, must be always boiled.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 23. <em>Sughi</em> crop of rice on <em>Bylu</em> land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Sughi</em> crop on <em>Bylu</em> land is entirely sown sprouted-seed. In the two months following the autumnal equinox, the field is ploughed eight times, then manured with cow-house dung, and ploughed a ninth time. It is then smoothed with the rake drawn by oxen, having been all the while inundated. The water is then drawn off by an instrument named <em>Cainully</em> (Plate XXV. Fig. 70.), which is wrought by a man like a rake. Small furrows are then made in the mud, to allow the water to drain off thoroughly, which is done by a small wooden instrument named <em>Shirula</em> (Plate XXV. Fig. 69.). In the month preceding the winter solstice the seed is sown. On the ninth day a little water is given; and, as the rice grows, the quantity is gradually increased. Till the end of the first month, the rain water in general is not expended; afterwards, by means of the machine called <em>Yatam</em>, the fields are supplied from small reservoirs and wells, or still more commonly from rivulets or springs, the water of which is raised by dams, and spread over the fields. These dams are very simple, consisting of earth and the branches of trees, with a few stones intermixed. The government in general is at the expense of making the reservoirs and dams.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation of rice on <em>Caru</em> land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the rainy season the <em>Caru</em> land is covered with water to the depth of from three to six feet; and on that account cannot be then cultivated. Afterwards it is cultivated exactly in the same manner as the <em>Bylu</em> land for the <em>Sughi</em> crop; and, although it yields only one crop in the year, the produce is not greater.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation of dry grains on <em>Bylu</em> land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Upon some of the <em>Bylu</em> land, where there is not a supply of water for two crops of rice, a crop of some of the dry grains is taken in the <em>Sughi</em> season. The quantity of seed for all the kinds is the same, 2 <em>Colagas</em> for a <em>Moray</em> land, or 0,<sup>292</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushel an acre.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-165"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="165" data-folio="145" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n164_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 145">[p. 145]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-004"><img src="/figures/journey-from-madras-vol-iii/fig-004_p0165.webp" alt="Vol III. p. 144. — PLATE XXV. — Warner sc." loading="lazy"><figcaption><em>Vol III. p. 144.</em> — <em>PLATE XXV.</em> — <em>Warner sc.</em></figcaption></figure><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-167"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="167" data-folio="145" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n166_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 145">[p. 145]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Of the grains cultivated, <em>Ellu</em>, or <em>Sesamum</em> produces 10 <em>Colagas</em>, or 1,<sup>463</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushel an acre. <em>Udu</em>, <em>Phaseolus minimoo</em> R: produces 12 <em>Colagas</em>, or 1,<sup>755</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushel an acre. <em>Hessaru Bily</em> (white) <em>Phaseolus mungo</em>, produces 14 <em>Colagas</em>, or 2,<sup>047</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushels an acre. <em>Pachy</em> (green) produces 10 <em>Colagas</em>, or 1,<sup>463</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> bushel an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For all these, the ground is ploughed five times in the month which precedes the shortest day; but the <em>Hessaru</em> is sown fifteen days later than the <em>Ellu</em>, and the <em>Udu</em> fifteen days later than the <em>Hessaru</em>. Before the last ploughing, the field is manured with ashes. The seed is sown broad-cast, and covered with the rake drawn by oxen. A month after seed time, the weeds are removed by the hand. Cattle will eat the straw of all the three pulses, but it is reckoned a worse fodder than the straw of rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb.</em> 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Sugar-cane is raised on <em>Mackey</em> land; but four years must intervene between every two crops; and for the first two years after cane, the rice does not thrive. The kind of cane used here is called <em>Bily-cabo</em>, which above the <em>Ghats</em> is called <em>Mara-cabo</em>. Inland they cultivate the <em>Cari-cabo</em>, which above the <em>Ghats</em> is called <em>Puttaputty</em>. In the month preceding the vernal equinox, the field is dug to the depth of ten inches with the hoe called <em>Cutari</em>. It is then ploughed five times, and smoothed with the rake drawn by oxen. Channels for conveying the water are then made, parallel to each other, and at the distance of three cubits. They are about nine inches wide, as much deep, and raised a little above the surface, the field being level. The intermediate beds are formed into ridges perpendicular to the channels, and resembling those of a potatoe field when it has been horse-hoed. The field is then covered with bushes, grass, dry cow-dung, and especially with dried parasitical plants, such as <em>Epidendra, Limodora</em>, &amp;c. and the whole of these are burned to ashes as a manure. On the third day after this the canes intended for planting are cut into pieces, each containing three joints, and these are soaked in water for two days. Then in each furrow between two ridges are placed longitudinally two rows of these cuttings. Each <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-168" data-page="168" data-folio="146" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n167_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 146">[p. 146]</button> piece leaves an interval of four inches between it and the next piece of the same row. The rows are placed near the bottom of the furrows, and are slightly covered with earth; and the furrows are then filled with water. All this must be performed before the new year commences at the equinox. Next day the furrows are again watered, and this is repeated on the eighth day, and afterwards once every four days. Two months after planting the field is weeded, and the ridges are repaired with a small hoe called <em>Halucatay</em>. The field is then manured with ashes, and with mud taken out of places where water lies deep. After this the watering is repeated once in four days till the commencement of the rainy season, when the ridges are thrown down, and new ones formed at the roots of each row of canes. In nine months these ripen without farther trouble. The water is in general raised, by the machine called <em>Yatam</em>, from wells in which it is found at the depth of from three to twelve feet from the surface. Three men are required to water and cultivate one <em>Moray</em> land, of which 1,<sup>79</sup>/<sub>100</sub> are equal to an acre; but at the time they are so employed the farm requires little other work. The canes are very small, being from 2 to 2 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> cubits long, and about the thickness of a man’s thumb. The juice is expressed by a mill, which consists of three cylinders moved by a perpetual screw. The force is applied to the centre cylinder by two capstan bars, wrought by six or eight men; and the whole machine is extremely rude. A <em>Moray</em> land produces 10 <em>Maunds</em> of <em>Jagory</em>, worth in all 5 <em>Pagodas</em>. This is at the rate of 4,<sup>33</sup>/<sub>100</sub> hundred-weight an acre, worth about 3<em>l</em>. 10<em>s</em>. My informants seem to have greatly under-rated the quantity of <em>Jagory</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the very satisfactory answers which Mr. Read, the collector, has been so good as to send to my queries, he observes as follows: “As the land on which the sugar-cane is reared is all rice-ground, its cultivation might be increased to a very considerable extent; but not without lessening the quantity of rice, because, the market for sugar being neither so extensive nor so profitable, by any means, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-169" data-page="169" data-folio="147" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n168_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 147">[p. 147]</button> as that for rice, few farmers would be at the expense of levelling and preparing ground for sugar-cane only. The only crops that it produces are <em>Sesamum</em> and <em>Udu</em> (<em>Phaseolus minimoo</em> Roxb:). On this kind of ground, after the soil has been ploughed three times, and manured with ashes, these grains are sown broad-cast in the second month after the summer solstice. The seed is covered with a hoe called <em>Ella-kudali</em>. The produce is much the same as on <em>Bylu</em> land; but there are no means by which the extent of <em>Betta</em> ground can be estimated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Cumri</em> cultivation.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. In the first season, after burning the woods, they sow <em>Ragy</em> (<em>Cynosurus</em>), <em>Tovary</em> (<em>Cytisus cajan</em>), and <em>Harulu</em> (<em>Ricinus</em>). Next year they have from the same ground a crop of <em>Shamay</em> (<em>Panicum miliare</em> Lamarck.). These hills are not private property, and pay no land-tax; but those who sow them pay, for the right of cultivation, a poll-tax of half a <em>Pagoda</em>, or nearly 4<em>s.</em> On account of poverty, many of them at present are exempted from this tax. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-170" data-page="170" data-folio="148" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n169_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 148">[p. 148]</button> I could here procure no satisfactory account of the garden cultivation, and shall not state what was said on the subject; but shall defer describing the gardens of <em>Haiga</em> until another opportunity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Implements and cattle.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only cattle in <em>Haiga</em> are buffaloes and oxen, about an equal number of each of which are used in the plough. This implement is here of the same form as that in the neighbourhood of <em>Seringapatam</em>. In <em>Haiga</em> they have no carts. Many of the cattle are imported from the countries adjacent to the <em>Gháts</em> near <em>Nagara</em>, and even these are of the poorest kind, nor are they larger than those of <em>Malayala</em> or <em>Tulava</em>. In the dry season, although fed with hay and straw, they are scarcely able to crawl. In the rainy season they grow fat, and strong, on the natural grass of the hills. Working oxen get the powder which separates from rice while it is beaten; buffaloes get the cake which is left after squeezing the oil from coco-nut kernels. The natives are ignorant that the cake which is formed in the same manner from <em>Sesamum</em> seed could be given to their cattle. Milk, and butter, or <em>Ghee</em>, are very dear, owing to the small number of cows, and their wretched condition.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manure.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. In the two months preceding, and in that following the winter solstice, the litter is dry grass, and the manure formed with it is called <em>Caradada Gobra</em>. About two cosses from <em>Hulledy-pura</em>, I came to a town named <em>Cumty</em>. It seems to have been formerly a place of some note. Its lanes are straight, and fenced with stone walls, and it has many coco-nut gardens. Twice it had the misfortune of having <em>Tippoo’s</em> army encamped in its vicinity; and on both occasions it was burned down by some of the irregulars. On its south side is a plain, intersected by a salt-water creek, which allows much salt to be made. The soil of the plain, which extends all the way from <em>Hulledy-pura</em>, is very sandy. For a coss north from <em>Cumty</em>, the ground is high, with very little cultivation; but a great part of it seems to be fit for being formed into <em>Mackey</em>, or at least into <em>Betta</em> land. Plantations.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">There being in this neighbourhood many palm gardens, I assembled the cultivators, and obtained from them the following account:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Coco-nut.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this part of the country the sandy downs near the sea are not much esteemed for the cultivation of the coco-nut. Here the farmers prefer the banks of salt-water inlets; and near these the rising grounds are generally planted, and the houses are built in the gardens. About towns, many gardens are enclosed with stone walls; in villages, the proprietors are contented with fences of earth, like those in <em>Malabar</em>. Once in two years the whole garden is dug, and fresh earth at the same time is spread throughout, by the industrious, to the depth of two inches; but lazy people allow only a little to the roots of each tree. The garden gets no other manure, except some salt to the young seedlings when these are transplanted. For six months in the year they must be watered once in four days. A young tree, fit for transplanting, costs two <em>Dubs</em> (about a penny), and is set in place of an old one which has died; so that the garden is never suffered to decay. In a good soil, the trees when ten years old begin to produce fruit, but in bad soils they are much later. Common reckoning says, that a coco-nut palm lives 100 years; but some die at 20, and many at all intermediate ages. At all times plantains and <em>Yams</em> (<em>Dioscorea</em>) are raised in the coco-nut gardens. Rich people never draw juice from their coco-nut trees, except in one year when they are young. For some years before the young palms can bring the fruit to maturity, they produce flowers; but, by extracting juice for one year, their coming to perfection is hastened. If any disease happen to the tree, rich men, to give relief to the sickly plant, do not extract juice, as is usual in some places, but with a sharp iron they bore a hole into the pith above the diseased part. Poor people, not being able to raise money to pay the wages of their workmen, give them <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-176" data-page="176" data-folio="154" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n175_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 154">[p. 154]</button> annually a certain number of trees for extracting juice, with which they can procure a daily subsistence. This compels the poor man, once in four or five years, to take juice from his trees. Besides, although this practice soon kills the tree, it gives much more immediate profit, especially in poor soils. In good soils, the nuts are of equal value with the juice; as a good tree in such a situation gives on an average, 80 full grown nuts, worth 25 <em>Rupees</em> a thousand; and 100 trees in such a soil, good and bad, young and old, produce 3000 nuts, which is at the rate of three quarters of a <em>Rupee</em> for each. In an indifferent soil the same number of trees produce only 1000 nuts, which is only at the rate of a quarter of a <em>Rupee</em> for each; but the coco-nut trees, good and bad, produce each a <em>Rupee</em> worth of juice, one-half of which goes to the extractor, and one-half is clear profit to the proprietor. One man can collect the juice of forty trees, and his share of the produce, being 20 <em>Rupees</em> (2<em>l</em>. 0<em>s</em>. 3½<em>d</em>.), is reckoned a sufficient maintenance for a man, his wife and children; for the people who extract the juice of palms are of a very low cast.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Betel-nut, or Areca.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Betel-nut</em> gardens are cultivated, at a distance from the banks of rivers, in the upper ends of narrow vallies, which contain <em>Bylu</em> land. The best soil is red, and contains shining particles, which I take to be mica. This soil is called <em>Câgadala</em>. Next in value to this is <em>Gujiny</em>, which is a black loose earth. The worst soil is called <em>Betta</em>, and is a hard earth composed of decayed or broken <em>Laterite</em>. The <em>Cagadala</em> is found in the bottoms of the vallies at their upper end, and is watered from a small reservoir, whence the water sometimes runs off by sluices, and sometimes is raised into the channels by the machine called <em>Yatam</em>. The <em>Gujiny</em> is found very low and level, where the hills forming the valley begin to recede a little from each other. In such land the water at all seasons of the year stands in the ditches, but is of a quality pernicious to the <em>Areca</em>, which must be watered from springs or rivulets. The <em>Betta</em> land forms the upper parts of the declivities of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-177" data-page="177" data-folio="155" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n176_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 155">[p. 155]</button> hills, and must be irrigated, by the hand, with water drawn from wells that are dug in the valley below. The garden must be fenced with a wall of stone or mud, on the upper side of which a deep drain must be formed to carry off the water, which during the rainy season descends from the hills in torrents. In this respect the <em>Cagadala</em> requires most trouble, and its watering is more expensive than that of the <em>Gujiny</em>; yet, owing to its being more productive, it is more profitable. The produce of the <em>Betta</em> land is still smaller than that of the <em>Gujiny</em>, and its cultivation is attended with much more trouble; yet it is worth while to plant the whole that is near a man’s house; for to a certain extent the family can perform the watering without great inconvenience.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Immediately before the winter solstice, the nuts for seed are cut, and are exposed three days to the sun, and three nights to the dew. In the mean time, a plot of <em>Cagadala</em> soil is dug for a seed-bed. In this the seeds are placed at four inches distance, and are half immersed in the ground. They are then covered with dung; and, that having been covered with straw, they are watered every other day until the second month after the vernal equinox. The rainy season then commences; and a drain must be dug to prevent the water from lying upon the bed. In the first or second month after the autumnal equinox, another piece of ground is hoed, and in this are placed the nuts which are then said to be <em>Mola</em>, as they have shoots sprouting from them a cubit long. The nuts in this bed are placed at about the distance of a foot from each other, and are buried an inch under ground. Every other day, during the dry season, they are well manured and watered. In this bed they remain fifteen months; and in the month preceding the winter solstice, they are manured with dung made from dry grass-litter; while in the month following the vernal equinox, the manure, which they receive, is that formed of dry leaves. During the month before and the month after the autumnal equinox, the young palms are (<em>Sussi</em>) fit for planting.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-178"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="178" data-folio="156" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n177_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 156">[p. 156]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 24.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Betel-leaf</em>, or <em>Piper Betle</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The garden having been properly inclosed, and secured from the torrents of the rainy season; and tanks, wells, or canals for supplying it with water, having been formed; the <em>Cagadala</em> soil is levelled into terraces like rice-ground, and formed into beds seven cubits wide. Between every two beds is a deep channel, to carry off the rain water; and in the middle of each is a small channel to convey the water that is to refresh the palms; and which, as it runs along, a man throws out on their roots with a <em>spatha</em>, that has fallen from the trees. On each side of the bed is planted a row of the <em>Arecas</em>, distant from each other five cubits, and between every two <em>Arecas</em> is set a young plantain tree. The garden is then manured with dung made from fresh leaves, and ever afterwards during the dry season it must be watered once in two days. For the first four years, it must be dug over in the month preceding the autumnal equinox, and at three different seasons must be manured with the three different kinds of manure. Afterwards, it is manured once a year only, in the second month after the autumnal equinox; and it is once in two years only that it requires to be dug. The <em>Betel</em>-<em>nut</em> is improved by the plantain trees, which keep the earth cool and moist; and therefore these are always continued, except where it is intended to train up the <em>Betel-leaf</em> vine upon the <em>Areca</em>, which is the manner wherein that plant is here cultivated. In this case, in the tenth year, the plantain trees are removed; and in the second month after midsummer, five cuttings of the <em>Betel-vine</em>, each containing three joints, are placed round every <em>Betel-palm</em>, while one of their ends is buried in the ground. They are then manured with the leaves of the <em>Nelli</em> (<em>Phyllanthus emblica</em>). Immediately after the autumnal equinox, the ground round the young vines must be hoed, and manured with dung made from fresh leaves. Ever afterwards, it must be manured three times a year. As the vines grow, they must be tied up to the palms. In eighteen months they begin to produce leaves fit for sale; in the third year they are full sized; two years they continue to give a full crop; in the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-179" data-page="179" data-folio="157" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n178_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 157">[p. 157]</button> following year the crop is bad, and then the vines are lifted, and new ones are planted in their stead. The <em>Betel</em>-palm, or <em>Areca</em>, in <em>Cagadala</em> soil begins to ripen fruit in ten years, is in full crop at fifteen, and continues in perfection for thirty years. They then die; and as the old ones decay, new ones are planted. Each tree yields two bunches, which ripen at different times between the autumnal equinox and winter solstice. The produce of a hundred trees, young and old, is reckoned five <em>Maunds</em> of boiled nut, or thirty-five <em>Bazar Colagas</em> by measure of nuts in the husk, as they come from the tree. The five <em>Maunds</em> are one fourth of a <em>Candy</em>, or 140 lb. The present price of boiled <em>Betel-nut</em> is fifteen <em>Pagodas</em>; each tree therefore, young and old, produces to the value of 3 <sup>626</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> pence, or a hundred trees produce fifteen <em>Rupees</em>. The cultivators boil the <em>Betel-nut</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In <em>Gujiny</em> ground, in order to remove the water off the soil, the drains between the beds must be one cubit and a half deep. It is irrigated once in seven days only, from the same sources that supply the <em>Bylu</em> rice-ground. In this soil, plantains and <em>Betel-leaf</em> grow in the same manner as in <em>Cagadala</em> gardens. A hundred trees, young and old, on <em>Gujiny</em> ground, are reckoned to produce four <em>Maunds</em> of boiled <em>Betel-nut</em>, worth twelve <em>Rupees</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On the <em>Betta</em> land no drains nor channels are required; but round the root of every palm a small bank is formed to confine the water, which is given once in two days. In such gardens, plantains, but not <em>Betel-leaf</em>, are reared. The trees in this soil do not come into full fruit till they are twenty years of age, and a hundred pro duce only two <em>Maunds</em> and a half of boiled nut, worth seven <em>Rupees</em> and a half. A man and his wife can manage a garden of 500 trees; some of which will grow on <em>Betta</em>, and a proportion on either <em>Cagadala</em>, or <em>Gujiny</em>, or on both. They require no assistance at crop season; but, unless the keeper be an active man, he will require some help when the garden is hoed. The expense of first planting a garden is commonly reckoned 100 <em>Rupees</em> for every 500 trees; but in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-180" data-page="180" data-folio="158" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n179_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 158">[p. 158]</button> level situations it will be much less, and in steep places much more. Some people go to 50 <em>Pagodas</em> for 100 trees, or 2 <em>Rupees</em> for each. No value is put upon the future expense, which is merely that of the keeper and his wife, who get 2 <sup>3</sup>/<sub>4</sub> <em>Hanies</em> of coarse rice daily, and 4 <em>Rupees</em> a year for clothing; that is to say, 37 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub> bushels of rice, worth 32 <sup>852</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> <em>Rupees</em>, and 4 <em>Rupees</em> in money; or in all 36 <em>Rupees</em> 13 <em>Anas</em> (3<em>l</em>. 14<em>s</em>. 3<em>d</em>.). It must be observed, however, that after the first year the plantains are adequate to the defraying of this expense, which is therefore not charged in the accompt. The farmer has therefore, on an average, 50 <em>Rupees</em> a year, for an original advance of from one to two hundred; but out of this must be deducted the revenue. His profit is much larger where he has a sale for <em>Betel-leaf</em>. It appears to me, that the gardens here are formed with more care, and at a greater expense, than in <em>Malabar</em>, where a colony of <em>Haiga Bráhmans</em> would be highly beneficial.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 25. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">25th <em>February</em>.—In the morning, having crossed the river, I took a circle of about six miles into the country east from <em>Mirzee</em>, in order to see some forests that spontaneously produce black pepper. The whole of the country through which I passed was hilly; but I met with several narrow vallies well watered, though not fully cultivated, owing to a want of inhabitants. Many of the hills were so barren, steep, and rocky, that I was soon forced to dismount from my horse, and proceed on foot. These hills consist entirely of naked <em>Laterite</em>. Other hills, which were those I sought after, were covered with stately forests.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pepper growing spontaneously.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 25.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb. 25.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The pepper-plant (<em>Piper nigrum</em>) seems to grow spontaneously on the sides of all the narrow vallies in the interior of <em>Haiga</em>, where the soil is so rich and moist as to produce lofty trees close to each other, by which a constant coolness is retained. In such places the pepper-vine runs along the ground and the roots of bushes, and propagates itself entirely by striking its roots into the soil, and then again sending out new shoots. The natives say, that without assistance it cannot ascend a tree; and that, unless it is exposed in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-181" data-page="181" data-folio="159" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n180_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 159">[p. 159]</button> such a situation to sun and air, it never produces flowers. In order to procure fruit from a hill which spontaneously produces the pepper-vine, the proprietor cuts all the underwood and bushes, and leaves only the large trees, and a number of the young ones sufficient to exclude the violence of sun, but to allow of a free circulation of air. Four cubits from tree to tree is reckoned a proper distance. The ends of the vines, which were lying on the ground, are then tied up to the nearest trees. Any kind of tree answers the purpose; but those of about eight inches or a foot in diameter are preferred, as it is easy to climb such for the purpose of gathering the pepper. A quantity of leaves are then placed round the root of the vine, to rot, and to serve as a manure. In the course of the year the vine, so far as it has been tied, strikes its roots into the bark of the tree; but the shoots above that, hang down. Twice a year afterwards these are tied up, and strike root, till they spread over all the large branches of the tree. In places where no vines have naturally sprung, the owner, after having dug a small spot round the tree to loosen the earth, propagates them by planting slips near the roots of the trees on which he wishes them to climb. The early part of the rainy season is the time proper for this operation. In five years, after having been managed in this manner, a hill begins to produce fruit, and in eight years is in full bearing. The vines live about thirty years; when others, that are found creeping on the ground in their natural state, are tied up in their stead; or, where these happen to be wanting, shoots or cuttings are planted near the trees. There is no difference in the quality between the pepper springing spontaneously from the seed, and that growing from cuttings; nor is the pepper growing in gardens either better or worse than that growing on a hill, managed as I am now describing. These hills producing pepper require no trouble, but the tying up of the plants, keeping the forest clear of underwood, and collecting the pepper. They are <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-182" data-page="182" data-folio="160" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n181_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 160">[p. 160]</button> manured in the following manner. In the month succeeding the vernal equinox, a hole three or four inches above the ground is made into the trunk of any very large tree that is situated near the top of the hill. Into this are put some burning coals, and, for an hour, a fire is kept up with fresh fewel. After this, the tree will burn inwardly for two days, and is then killed. A large insect immediately takes possession of the trunk, and works its nest into the wood. In the next rainy season, the whole falls down into a rotten dust, which the rain washes away, so as to disperse it over the face of the hill below. The crop season commences about the winter solstice, and it continues rather more than two months. A man can in one day gather three <em>Colagas</em>, farmer’s measure, or almost one peck of the <em>amenta</em>. These are dried three days in the sun, and then are rubbed with the feet on a piece of smooth ground, to separate the grains; which, having been cleared from the husks and foot-stalks, are again dried two days in the sun, and tied up for sale in straw bags or <em>Morays</em>. Seventy-five <em>Colagas</em> of <em>amenta</em> are required to make one <em>Bazar Moray</em> (bushel 1 <sup>6</sup>⁄<sub>10</sub>) of dry pepper, which weighs 3 <em>Maunds</em> (about 84 lb.); so that a man daily collects about 3 <sup>15</sup>⁄<sub>23</sub> lb. of dry pepper. These hills were formerly valued; and, according to their extent, each paid as a land-tax so many <em>Maunds</em> of pepper, the <em>Maund</em> containing 60 <em>Seers</em>. The same valuation is now continued; but the <em>Maund</em> is reduced to 40 <em>Seers</em>, and converted into money, at the rate of a <em>Pagoda</em>, which is in favour of the proprietor. Still one half of these hills is waste, owing to a want of hands to cultivate them; and on that account three-fourths of the revenue are remitted to the proprietors, who are also favoured by having all the rice-ground lying among these hills free from tax. This has been given them, on a supposition that its produce was only adequate to feed the people who are employed in cultivating the pepper.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Produce of the forests. <em>Teak.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 25.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">So far as I went, no <em>Teak</em> grows in these forests; but I am told, that <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-183" data-page="183" data-folio="161" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n182_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 161">[p. 161]</button> it is procurable farther inland. The landlords (<em>Malugaras</em>) pretend, that all the timber trees are their property, but that none of them are saleable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nutmeg.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The wild nutmeg and <em>Cassia</em> are very common. As the nutmegs ripen, the monkies always eat up the outer rind, and mace; so that I could not procure one in a perfect state. They are collected from the ground, after having been peeled by the monkies, and are sold by some poor people to the shopkeepers; but they have little flavour; and the demand for them is very small. Although they are, doubtless, of a distinct species from the nutmeg of <em>Amboyna</em>, it is probable, that by proper cultivation and manure their quality might be greatly improved; and that, in the situations where they now grow spontaneously, they might be reared as the supporters of the pepper vine; which would produce copiously, and of an excellent quality, were the same pains bestowed on it here as is done in the gardens above the <em>Ghats</em>, where by far the best pepper grows.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Laurus Cassia.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Cassia</em> belongs to government, and is in general given in lease; but at present no renter can be procured. Its quality also might, no doubt, be greatly improved; and by cutting the shoots, when of a proper size, and cleaning and rolling up the bark neatly, it might be made equal to the <em>Cassia</em> of <em>China</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Strata.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">On my return from the pepper hills to <em>Mirzee</em>, I passed a very fine plantation of <em>Betel-nut</em> palms, belonging to four <em>Bráhmans</em>, and containing many thousand trees. It was placed on the two steep sides of a very narrow valley, well supplied with water from springs. Here I observed the first regular <em>strata</em> since leaving <em>Pali-ghat</em>. They consisted of very soft pot-stone, probably impregnated with hornblende slate, as they seem to be a continuation of the quarries of slaty sienite, from which the temples at <em>Batuculla</em> have been supplied with stone. I have already noticed the affinity that prevails between the hornblende and pot-stone rocks in the dominions of <em>Mysore</em>. The <em>strata</em> at this garden are vertical, and run nearly north and south.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-184"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="184" data-folio="162" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n183_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 162">[p. 162]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 25. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Having returned to <em>Mirzee</em>, I went two cosses and a half to <em>Hirigutty</em>. Part of the country through which I passed was very barren, consisting of low hills covered with stunted trees. The soil of other parts was good; but, owing to a want of inhabitants, was much neglected. Near <em>Hirigutty</em>, there is on the northern side of the river a remarkably fine plain. It does not seem to be well cultivated, and has suffered lately from the breaking down of a dam, which has permitted a great part of it to be inundated with salt-water.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">History of <em>Haiga</em>, according to its <em>Bráhmans</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 25.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb.</em> 25.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 25.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Hirigutty</em>, I collected several <em>Haiga Bráhmans</em>, who were said to be the best informed men concerning the history of the country. The <em>Shanaboga</em>, or accomptant of the temple of <em>Daréswara</em>, produced a book called <em>Bahudunda</em>, which, they said, was written by a certain <em>Subahitta</em>, or <em>Bráhmany</em> chief, who will hereafterwards be mentioned. On the authority of this book the <em>Shanaboga</em> said, that <em>Parasu Ráma</em> created <em>Haiga</em> at the same time that he formed <em>Tulava</em> and <em>Malayála</em>, and he then also appointed certain <em>Bráhmans</em> to inhabit these lands. <em>Tulava</em> he gave to the <em>Mittu Bráhmans</em>, and <em>Haiga</em> to those called <em>Nagar</em> and <em>Mutchy</em>. These people were not true <em>Bráhmans</em>; but they kept possession of the country till after the commencement of the <em>Kali-yugam</em>. The country was then seized upon by two casts of impure origin, the <em>Mogayer</em> and the <em>Whalliaru</em>. The former are the fishermen of <em>Haiga</em>; the latter I have had frequent occasion to mention; and to this tribe the <em>Rájá</em> belonged. At length a <em>Sannyási</em>, who had visited the country, induced <em>Myuru Verma</em> to invade it. He was king of <em>Banawási</em> and <em>Gutti</em> in <em>Karnata</em>, and by cast a <em>Baydar</em>, which is a tribe of <em>Telingana</em>. His attack was successful, and he conquered <em>Haiga</em>, <em>Tulava</em>, and <em>Kankána</em>. He then brought a colony of five thousand true <em>Bráhmans</em> from <em>Ahichaytra</em>, a city in <em>Telingana</em>, and settled them in <em>Haiga</em>. He brought others of the same origin to <em>Kankána</em> and <em>Tulava</em>. A thousand of these <em>Haiga Bráhmans</em> lost cast immediately, having omitted the performance of certain prayers (<em>Mantrams</em>) <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-185" data-page="185" data-folio="163" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n184_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 163">[p. 163]</button> which were necessary to purify the country before they took possession.  The remaining four thousand obtained the whole lands of <em>Haiga</em>, and continued to enjoy them until <em>Myuru Verma</em> was obliged to fly by <em>Nunda</em>, the son of <em>Utunga</em>, one of the <em>Whalliaru</em>, who recovered the dominions of his ancestors.  This low fellow seized on the lands that had been granted to the four thousand <em>Bráhmans</em>, and forced them to retire to <em>Ahichaytra</em>.  He was succeeded by his son <em>Chanda Sayana</em>, whose mother, being a dancing girl from the temples of <em>Karnata</em>, had educated him so as to have a due respect for the sacred order.  Soon after his accession to power, he invited back the <em>Bráhmans</em>; and, having given up the whole of his authority to their <em>Subahitta</em>, or chief, the author of the book, he made all his <em>Whalliaru</em> the slaves of the sacred order.  So long as <em>Chanda Sayana</em> lived, he was called <em>Rájá</em>, and the <em>Subahitta</em> continued to govern in his name.  On his death without children, the <em>Subahitta</em> was at a loss what to do; as according to the laws of his cast he could not assume the regal title, and as there was no <em>Rájá</em> under whose authority he could act.  He therefore invited <em>Solva Krishna Devarasu Wodearu</em> of <em>Anagundi</em> to take possession of <em>Haiga</em>, which had never before been subject to <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>.  This prince accordingly came; but, far from allowing the <em>Subahitta</em> to enjoy any authority, he imposed a land-tax on the <em>Bráhmans</em>, and gave all the country to a <em>Jain Polygar</em>, <em>Itchuppa Wodear Rájá</em> of <em>Garsopa</em>.  No date is assigned in the book for these extraordinary events, which nobody but a <em>Haiga Bráhman</em> can possibly believe.  In order to conceal the long subjection to the infidel <em>Jain</em>, in which the <em>Bráhmans</em> of <em>Haiga</em> had been compelled to live, they bring down the time of <em>Myuru Verma</em> to that of the latter princes, or usurpers of the throne of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>.  Many inscriptions render it indubitable, that <em>Haiga</em> belonged to the kings of <em>Karnata</em> long before the time of <em>Krishna Ráyalu</em>.  Copies of all these, which I now quote, were in the possession of the very <em>Bráhmans</em> who gave me the foregoing account.  The temple at <em>Daréswara</em> has two grants engraved <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-186" data-page="186" data-folio="164" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n185_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 164">[p. 164]</button> on copper-plates. The one is dated <em>Sidarti</em> of <em>Sal.</em> 1422, on the 14th of the moon in <em>Bhádrapada</em>, in the reign of <em>Déva Ráya Wodearu Trilochia</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. This title of king of the three people (<em>Trilochia</em>) is said to be peculiar to the kings of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>, as is also the title of king of the three seas. The title of <em>Trilochia</em> seems well enough applied, as these princes governed the tribes who speak the <em>Telinga</em>, <em>Tamul</em>, and <em>Karnataca</em> languages. This date apparently does not agree well with the <em>Ráya Paditti</em>; for the last <em>Déva Ráya</em> which it mentions ended his reign in the year of <em>Sal.</em> 1377. But, as we shall afterwards see, this <em>Déva Ráya</em> may have been one of the names of the usurper who reigned in 1422. The other grant on copper is by <em>Solva Krishna Devarasu Wodearu Trilochia</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. and is dated <em>Sal.</em> 1481, on the 15th of <em>Ashádha</em>, in the year <em>Calayucti</em>. This agrees very well with the chronology of <em>Ramuppa</em>. A third grant to the same temple is by <em>Krishna Devarasu Wodearu Trilochia</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. in the year <em>Vicari</em> of <em>Sal.</em> 1462, on the 1st of <em>Kartika</em>. This also agrees with the chronology of <em>Ramuppa</em>. Another, in the time of <em>Trinetra Solva Narasingha Nayaka</em>, king of the three seas, and of <em>Anagundi</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. is dated in <em>Durmati Sal.</em> 1424, 14th <em>Bhádrapada</em>. Among other strange titles assumed by this prince, he is said to be able to pull all other potentates by the whiskers. In it he commands <em>Devarasu Wodear</em>, probably the lieutenant of <em>Haiga</em>, to grant such and such lands to the <em>Bráhmans</em>. It is clear therefore, that before the time of <em>Krishna Ráyaru</em> the kings of <em>Anagundi</em> were sovereigns of <em>Haiga</em>, and that all the lands did not belong to the <em>Bráhmans</em>. Another grant, for erecting an inn for travellers, is dated on the same day and year, and by order of <em>Solva Déva Ráya Wodearu</em>, <em>Rájá</em> of <em>Nagara</em> (not the present <em>Nagara</em> but <em>Vijaya</em>-<em>nagara</em>), <em>Haiga</em>, <em>Tulava</em>, <em>Kankana</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. We here find, that the second <em>Narasingha</em> of the usurping princes is sometimes called also <em>Déva</em>; and the same probably was the case with the first <em>Narasingha</em>, which will reconcile the chronology of the first grant with that of <em>Ramuppa</em>. The inscription on stone at the temple of <em>Gunavunti</em>, in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-187" data-page="187" data-folio="165" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n186_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 165">[p. 165]</button> <em>Garsopa</em> district, of which a copy has been presented to the Bengal government, mentions, that <em>Itchuppa Wodearu Pritani</em> (<em>Jain Rájá of Garsopa</em>) granted certain lands to that temple by order of <em>Pritápa Déva Ráya Trilochia</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. of the family of <em>Hari-hara</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. in <em>Virodi</em> Sal. 1332, on the 10th of <em>Márgasírsha</em>. This is <em>Déva Ráya</em> the First, and agrees very well with the chronology of <em>Ramuppa</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Account from a book of the <em>Jain</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 25.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A very intelligent <em>Bráhman</em> from <em>Batuculla</em> says, that he had consulted a book in the possession of a <em>Jain Sannyási</em>, which stated, that the <em>Byrasu Wodear</em> family of <em>Carculla</em> was descended from the <em>Belalla Ráyas</em>, the supreme kings of <em>Karnata</em>. The last male of this branch of the family had seven daughters, all called <em>Byra Dévi</em>. When the <em>Rájá</em> died, his country was divided among his daughters in seven portions; and <em>Krishna Ráyaru</em> was so gallant, as to remit the whole tribute to them, as being ladies. The eldest sister, <em>Doda Byra Dévi</em>, lived at <em>Batuculla</em>. The second sister married the son and heir of <em>Itchuppa Wodear</em> of <em>Garsopa</em>, who seems to have been the tributary <em>Rájá</em> of <em>Haiga</em>. This marriage produced only one daughter; and none of her aunts having had children, she united again in her person the sovereignty of all the dominions of <em>Carculla</em>. To these she added <em>Haiga</em>; and, during the weakness of the princes of <em>Anagundi</em>, in the reign of the last usurper, she seems to have refused all marks of submission to their authority. She lived sometimes at <em>Garsopa</em>, and sometimes at <em>Batuculla</em>, until she was destroyed by the <em>Sivabhactars</em> of <em>Ikeri</em>, who were assisted by an insurrection of the <em>Halypecas</em>; and who, in conjunction with that low, barbarous tribe, almost exterminated the <em>Jain</em> of <em>Haiga</em>, and the northern districts of <em>Tulava</em>. There is still a man living at <em>Dharmastilla</em>, six cosses distant from <em>Jamal-ábád</em>, who is named <em>Comara Hegada</em>, and who is looked upon as a descendant in the male line of the <em>Carculla</em> family, and legal representative of the <em>Belalla Ráyas</em>, who began to govern <em>Karnata</em> in the year of our Lord 684. This man may very probably be of the family of the <em>Carculla Rájás</em>; but, in fact, these were descended from <em>Jenaditta</em>, a fugitive from the north of <em>India</em>; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-188" data-page="188" data-folio="166" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n187_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 166">[p. 166]</button> and a desire of flattering the princes of the <em>Jain</em> sect, who were the most powerful in these latter days, probably occasioned the legend, in the book of the <em>Sannyási</em>, to trace up their origin to the <em>Belalla</em> family.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Hirigutty</em>, which has no market (<em>Bazar</em>), stands on a fine plain, about two miles from the river; and at some distance, toward the east and north, has rugged barren hills.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 26. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">26th <em>February</em>.—I went three cosses to <em>Gaukarna</em>. There was a thick fog, which prevented me from seeing the country; but near the road it was a plain, consisting mostly of rice fields; many of which, by the breaking down of the bank, had been inundated with salt-water. At the western extremity of this plain is a ridge of low barren hills, which bend round to the sea, and separate the plain on the banks of the river from that on which <em>Gaukarna</em> stands, about a coss north from the mouth of the river. The plain of <em>Gaukarna</em> is well cultivated, and consists of rice fields intermixed with coco-nut gardens.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Gaukarna.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Gaukarna</em>, or the cow’s horn, is a place of great note among the <em>Bráhmans</em>, owing to a celebrated image of <em>Siva</em> called <em>Mahaboléswara</em>. The image is said to have been brought from the mountain <em>Coila</em> by <em>Ravana</em>, king of <em>Lanca</em>. He wished to carry it to his capital; but having put it down here, the idol became fixed in the place, where it stands to this day. The building, by which the idol is at present covered, is very mean. <em>Gaukarna</em> is a scattered place, buried among coco-nut palms; but enjoys some commerce, and contains 500 houses, of which <em>Bráhmans</em> occupy one half.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Account of <em>Haiga</em> by the <em>Bráhmans</em> of <em>Gaukarna.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 26.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb.</em> 26.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I assembled the most distinguished of these <em>Bráhmans</em>, who informed me, that the book produced yesterday by the <em>Shanaboga</em> of <em>Daréswara</em> is not considered by them as of good authority. That every <em>Shanaboga</em> has a <em>Bahudunda</em>, containing the papers and deeds belonging to his office, and which are generally preceded by such an account of past times, as the first person of the family who enjoyed the office could obtain. These <em>Bahudundas</em> the <em>Vaidika</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-189" data-page="189" data-folio="167" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n188_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 167">[p. 167]</button> <em>Bráhmans</em> hold in great contempt; but, as the office of <em>Shanaboga</em> has in numerous instances continued for many generations in the same family, I am inclined to think that from this source much historical information might be procured. The <em>Bráhmans</em> here are all <em>Smartal</em>, of true <em>Panch Dravada</em> extraction, and despise the <em>Haiga Bráhmans</em>, as being greatly their inferiors. When I inter-rogated them concerning the history of the country, they said that it was contained in a book in their possession, called <em>Seinghadri Utracunda</em>, or the second volume of <em>Seinghadri</em>, a work composed by God in the form of <em>Vyása</em>, who wrote the eighteen <em>Puranas</em>. They suppose, that this was done long before the creation of this part of the world, and therefore look upon all the historical part as entirely prophetical. I found that none of them had ever been at pains to read the book, and they therefore spoke of its contents merely from report, or tradition. They say that it brings the history of <em>Kérala</em>, <em>Tulava</em>, <em>Haiva</em> (the <em>Sanskrit</em> name for <em>Haiga</em>), and <em>Kankana</em>, no lower down than the time of <em>Myuru Verma’s</em> grandson. It is written in the character of <em>Tulava</em>, which is the same with that of <em>Malayála</em>, and in the <em>Sanskrit</em> language. It contains no dates, and seems to be, as usual, an idle rhapsody, in which are foretold the great deeds of five princes of one family, who were to be great favourers of a certain sect of <em>Bráhmans</em>. These five princes are <em>Trenetra Cadumba</em>, <em>Hæmanga</em>, <em>Myuru Verma</em>, <em>Locadita</em>, and <em>Chanda</em>-<em>Sayana</em>; which last the <em>Haiga Bráhmans</em> suppose to have been a <em>Whalliaru</em>. The dominion of these princes extended all over the country created by <em>Parasu Ráma</em>, from Cape <em>Comorin</em> to <em>Surat</em>. In all this country, at the accession of <em>Myuru Verma</em>, there was no true <em>Bráhman</em>; but for each division of it that prince brought a colony from <em>Ahichaytra</em>. The <em>Namburis</em> formed one of these colonies, all of which have in some measure lost cast, or at least have been degraded, by a disobedience of the orders of <em>Sankara Achárya</em>. At that time, the <em>Rájá</em> of <em>Ahichaytra</em> was a <em>Jain</em>; but he favoured the <em>Bráhmans</em> who followed <em>Vyása</em>, his wife’s mother having been <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-190" data-page="190" data-folio="168" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n189_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 168">[p. 168]</button> very intimate with one of these persons, and having educated her daughter in a due regard for the sect. Shortly before that time, this sect had risen into considerable reputation in <em>Andray</em>, by the efforts of <em>Buta Achárya</em>, and was afterwards spread throughout the peninsula by the teaching of the three great doctors <em>Sankara</em>, <em>Rama Anuja</em>, and <em>Madua</em>. These <em>Smartal Bráhmans</em> possessed a grant of lands engraved on a plate of copper. It is dated <em>Servajittu</em> of <em>Sal.</em> 1450, in the 20th of <em>Maga</em>, and in the reign of <em>Krishna Ráya</em>, which agrees with the chronology of <em>Ramuppa</em>. Having been informed that there were here many inscriptions on stone, I went out in search of them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 26.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb.</em> 26.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The large tank is a very fine work, and the only structure in the place that is worth notice. Near this, in the yard of a small religious building called <em>Kaméswara Matam</em>, I found the most ancient inscription. The stone on which this is cut is at the top adorned with emblems, which indicate that its erectors have been worshippers of <em>Siva</em>. Much of it is buried under ground; only thirteen lines are at all legible, and parts of these are decayed. First come the titles of the sovereign <em>Cadumba Chicraverti</em>. These are quite different from those assumed by the kings of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>, which are known by almost every <em>Bráhman</em>, and facilitate greatly the reading of all the inscriptions that were made during their government. The titles given to <em>Cadumba Chicraverti</em> seem to be little understood. After the titles, and a defacement of half a line, mention is made of two sons, learned and heroic men, and <em>Rájás</em> by the favour of <em>Rajaya</em> (the goddess of the earth). Then follow some unintelligible words. Then the date of the <em>Kali-yugam</em> 120, being <em>Vikrama</em>, 15th <em>Maga</em>, there being then an eclipse of the moon. These two sons gave <em>Dharma</em> (charity), by building <em>Kaméswara Matam</em>, on the west side of the temple of <em>Sankara Narayana</em>, in the name of <em>Sri Mahaboléswura</em>; and for the performance of <em>Bunaneia</em> (worship and charity) in this <em>Matam</em>, they granted certain grounds, then overset, without proprietors, and become <em>Haraweri</em> (reverted <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-193" data-page="193" data-folio="169" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n192_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 169">[p. 169]</button> to the state) with the water-courses, house-steads, gardens, <em>Betta</em>fields, <em>Chitta</em>-fields high and low, and the rank formerly thereunto appertaining. Here the writing is totally defaced. It probably contained the extent, name, and boundaries of the lands. From their disposing of lands belonging to the government, it is probable that the two sons, mentioned in the inscription, were sons of the king. The first cypher of the date is defaced; but from some fragments of it the <em>Bráhmans</em> think that it must be either a 1 or a 3; and from their traditions they are inclined to think that it is the former. <em>Cadumba Chicraverti</em> is the ancestor of <em>Myuru Verma</em>. This date would make him to have reigned 534 years earlier than the time assigned for the commencement of his reign by <em>Ramuppa</em>; which, I have already said, is probably much more early than the reality. The 3120, supposing that to be the true reading, would make <em>Cadumba Chicraverti</em> to have been governing 149 years before the time in which (from an inscription that I afterwards procured) I found that his descendant <em>Trenetra Cadumba</em> actually reigned. I am persuaded, therefore, that this is the proper era of <em>Myuru Verma</em>, and the introduction of the <em>Bráhmans</em> from <em>Ahichaytra</em>; and that the <em>Banchica</em>, <em>Abhira</em>, and <em>Monayer</em> families of <em>Ramuppa</em>, are either names altogether fabricated, in order to increase the antiquity of <em>Myuru Verma</em>; or that, more probably, the order in the succession of the dynasties has been altered. This inscription, copied in imitation of the old character, has been delivered to the Bengal government. The image of <em>Sankara Narayana</em>, mentioned in this inscription, still remains in a small temple, on the east side of the <em>Matam</em>; and is a strong proof of the early prevalence of the doctrine which the <em>Smartal</em> now teach, namely, that <em>Siva</em> and <em>Vishnu</em> are different names for the same god, according to his different attributes, as destroyer and preserver of the world. A likeness of it is given in Plate XXIV.; from which it will appear, that, in order to show their identity, the same image has the emblems of both <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-194" data-page="194" data-folio="170" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n193_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 170">[p. 170]</button> gods. The names do the same; for <em>Sankara</em> is one of the titles of <em>Siva</em>, and <em>Narayana</em> one of the names of <em>Vishnu</em>.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-192"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="192" data-folio="168" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n191_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 168">[p. 168]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-005"><img src="/figures/journey-from-madras-vol-iii/fig-005_p0192.webp" alt="Fig. 67. — Vol. III. p. 169. — Image of Sankara Narayana at Gaukarna. — PLATE XXIV. — Warner sc." loading="lazy"><figcaption><em>Fig. 67.</em> — <em>Vol. III. p. 169.</em> — <em>Image of Sankara Narayana at Gaukarna.</em> — <em>PLATE XXIV.</em> — <em>Warner sc.</em></figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The next most ancient inscription that I found here was, like the others, in a private house, and exceedingly neglected. It is dated <em>Anunda</em> 1297, Friday 1st <em>Palguna</em>, in the reign of <em>Sri Vira Buca Ráya</em>, by the favour of the feet of <em>Virupacsha Devaru</em> (the <em>Siva</em> at <em>Humpay</em> opposite to <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>) king of the east, west, and south seas. This must be <em>Buca Ráya</em> the First, who would therefore appear to have reigned at least two years later than the time assigned for him by <em>Ramuppa</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Another is dated in <em>Sal.</em> 1308, and contains a grant of revenue for supporting an inn, by the son of <em>Hari-hara Ráya</em>; but his name is effaced. A copy of this has been delivered to the Bengal government.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The last that I visited is dated <em>Suabanu Sal.</em> 1472, on the 23d of <em>Srávana</em>. In this, <em>Solva Krishna Devarasu Wodearu</em>, son of <em>Sedásiva Ráya</em>, and king of <em>Nagara</em> (<em>Vijaya-nagara</em>), <em>Haiva</em>, <em>Tulava</em>, <em>Kankana</em>, &amp;c. grants lands situated in the <em>Ashtá-grám</em> of <em>Sashisty</em> district (<em>Desa</em>), in <em>Govay Ráyada</em> (principality of <em>Goa</em>). Hence it will be evident, that, while this powerful <em>Hindu</em> prince lived, the <em>Adil Shah Sultans</em> of <em>Vijaya-pura</em> were very much confined in their territories toward the south-west.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Feb.</em> 27. Account of a <em>Bahudunda</em>, or register, kept by the hereditary accomptant.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 27.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Valuation supposed to have been made by <em>Krishna Ráya.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>Feb.</em> 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">27th <em>February</em>.—It having been mentioned to me, that the books of the hereditary <em>Shanaboga</em> here contained much curious information, I determined to stay a day, and examine them. I found that he had a <em>Bahudunda</em> of two volumes. The first commenced with some verses on medicine. Then followed some rules for the performance of the ceremonies of religion. Then came an old list of the names of all the principal traders in <em>Mirzee</em>. They were 54 in number; but the ants had eaten up the date. This was followed by an old enumeration of the inhabitants of <em>Mirzee</em> district (<em>Taluc</em>), then divided into three divisions (<em>Maganas</em>) <em>Gaukarna</em>, <em>Nagara</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-195" data-page="195" data-folio="171" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n194_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 171">[p. 171]</button> and <em>Seiganahully</em>. Then came an account, without date, of a contribution which a vagrant <em>Bráhman</em> had raised for the repairs of a temple. Then came the copy of a grant, originally engraven on stone, dated in 1442, the year of <em>Sal.</em> 1441 having past. By this, <em>Rutnuppa Wodearu</em>, and his son <em>Vijayuppa Wodearu</em>, having been appointed <em>Rájás</em> of <em>Barcuru</em> by <em>Sri Vira Krishna Ráya</em> on the throne of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>, they granted to a certain <em>Bráhman</em> the <em>Shistu</em>, or land-tax, arising from certain grounds, and amounting to the annual value of 25 <em>Pagodas</em>. This year, according to <em>Ramuppa</em>, was the first of the reign of <em>Krishna</em>. Next follows a paper respecting the relief granted to a village by a Mussulman governor, under the <em>Sultan</em> of <em>Vijaya-pura</em>. Then comes a memorandum, which states that <em>Mahaboléswara</em>, the great <em>Pagoda</em> here, possessed lands to the value of 12000 <em>Pagodas</em> a year (4835<em>l.</em> 7<em>s.</em> 2½<em>d.</em>), from the time of <em>Madua Ráya</em> (probably the great doctor of the <em>Bráhmanical</em> laws) in the year of <em>Sal.</em> 138 <sup>8</sup>/<sub>9</sub>, until the time of <em>Byra Devi</em>. The memorandum then details all the lands, and appropriates the manner in which the revenue is to be expended. No date accompanies this memorandum; but it is looked upon by the <em>Bráhmans</em> as affording the temple a sufficient right to the specified lands, and as a clear proof that the rules for expenditure were prescribed by <em>Madua Ráya</em>. Next follows a grant of lands to the ancestors of the <em>Shanaboga</em>, from <em>Mahamundeléswara Krishna Devarasu Wodearu</em>, king of <em>Nagara, Haiva, Tulava, Kankana</em>, &amp;c. in the year of <em>Sal.</em> 1452, which also is agreeable to <em>Ramuppa’s</em> chronology. Then comes a copy of a <em>Shist</em>, or valuation, usually called that of <em>Krishna Ráyaru</em>; but there is nothing in the writing that shows when or by whom it was framed. It extends to the three divisions (<em>Maganas</em>) of <em>Mirzee</em> already mentioned, and includes a fourth named <em>Hirtitty</em>. From this it would appear, that those people who cultivated <em>Cumri</em> land paid 2 <sup>5</sup>/<sub>8</sub> <em>Fanams</em> a head. At present they pay 2½ <em>Fanams</em>. Gardens then were also taxed, and the government took one half of their supposed produce. Thus 1000 coco-nuts paid 3 <em>Pagodas</em>. It would <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-196" data-page="196" data-folio="172" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n195_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 172">[p. 172]</button> appear, that since that time the price of this article has not increased, 6 <em>Pagodas</em> being the present value of 1000 coco-nuts. This seems to me a clear proof of the flourishing state of the country when the valuation was formed; as there can be no doubt, that the value of gold has in general decreased greatly since the time of <em>Krishna</em>, owing to the great quantities procured from America. The difference, therefore, must be made up by the more flourishing state of the country, which introduced wealth, and enhanced the price of every thing valuable: the present decayed state of the country, notwithstanding the low value of money, keeps down the price. By this valuation the pulse sown as a second crop was taxed. It had been a custom for every proprietor of a garden, at a certain festival, to wait on the officers of government, and present them with 1 <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub> <em>Pagoda</em>. The valuation directs, that they should be exempted from this trouble, and that the money should be paid at the same time with their land-tax. The rice land paid 3 <em>Pagodas</em> for every <em>Cumbum</em> of produce. The <em>Cumbum</em> is two-thirds of a <em>Corge</em>, and at present is worth on an average about 12 <em>Pagodas</em>. Since that time an additional tax of 3 <sup>3</sup>⁄<sub>4</sub> <em>Fanams</em> has been laid on each <em>Cumbum</em>. In this manner each estate having been valued, the land-tax was fixed on it <em>in cumulo</em>; and the same continues still to be taken, with the addition above mentioned on the rice-lands; but a great deduction is made on account of lands not occupied. When the valuation was formed, there was no tax on houses, but shops paid a duty to the <em>Suncha</em>, or custom-house.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Chronicle.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The second volume of the <em>Shanaboga’s</em> register commences with a kind of chronicle. <em>Killidi Vencatuppa Náyaka</em> having destroyed <em>Byra Devi</em>, information of the event was sent to <em>Ibrahim Adil Shah Padishah</em>, by <em>Sherif ún Mulk</em>, the <em>Vazir</em> residing at <em>Ponday</em>, a place near <em>Goa</em>. This officer seems to have commanded in <em>Kankana</em>, after the Mussulmans had seized on it, during the decline of the <em>Hindus</em> of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>. The <em>Padishah</em> then ordered all the <em>Havildars</em> (military officers) commanding in <em>Kankana</em>, to join <em>Sherif ún Mulk</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-197" data-page="197" data-folio="173" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n196_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 173">[p. 173]</button> and to fight with <em>Vencatuppa Náyaka</em>. On the 5th of <em>Margasirsha Sal</em>. 1529, being the year <em>Parabava</em>, they advanced as far as <em>Chindawera</em>, where they were entirely defeated. They retreated beyond the <em>Mirzee</em> river, and, having there built a strong fort, the river continued to be the boundary between the <em>Sivabhactars</em> and Mussulmans. Next year <em>Sherif ún Mulk</em> returned to <em>Ponday</em>, leaving an officer (<em>Havildar</em>) in command at <em>Mirzee</em> to collect the revenues, and remit them to <em>Ponday</em>. In the course of thirty-five years, there were twelve governors (<em>Havildars</em>). These were succeeded by officers called <em>Mahal Mocasi</em>, of whom there were ten at <em>Mirzee</em> in the course of thirty years. A <em>Tannadar</em> then governed it for eighteen months. After which <em>Mammud Khan</em> held the government for a year and a quarter. <em>Abdul Hassein Havildar</em> then governed twenty-one months, another <em>Havildar</em> nine months, and <em>Murtiza Khan</em> a similar length of time. He was displaced by two Mussulmans, who rose up, and put him in confinement. These possessed the country for eighteen months. After this <em>Mirzee</em> became subject to the <em>Sivabhactars</em>, and continued to be governed by <em>Karnataca Parputties</em> till the year <em>Durmutti</em>, fourteen years after <em>Hyder</em> had reduced <em>Bidderuru</em>, now called <em>Nagara</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Valuation by Adil Shah.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Next follows a valuation (<em>Shistu</em>) which was made by the officers of <em>Adil Shah</em>, in the <em>Fusly</em> year, or year of the <em>Hejira</em> 1044, and includes the five districts, or <em>Mahals</em>, that were subject to <em>Vijaya-pura</em>, and were named <em>Mirzee</em>, <em>Ancola</em>, <em>Ponday</em>, <em>Cadawada</em> (<em>Carwar</em>), and <em>Sivéswara</em>; and which were probably the part of the dominions of <em>Byra Devi</em>, that fell to the share of the Mussulmans. This is the valuation now in use. <em>Hyder</em> imposed no new taxes, but resumed one half of the charity lands (<em>Enams</em>); <em>Tippoo</em> seized upon the remainder.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I have detailed the contents of these volumes, that a judgment may be formed, of what may be usually expected in such registers, which are very numerous throughout the Peninsula.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Inscription.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 27.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In my evening walk I examined an inscription on stone. It is <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-198" data-page="198" data-folio="174" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n197_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 174">[p. 174]</button> dated <em>Sal.</em> 1311, 1st <em>Phálguna</em>, and in the reign of <em>Buca Ráya Trilochia</em>, &amp;c. son of <em>Hari-hara Ráya</em>, king of <em>Haiva</em>, <em>Tulava</em>, <em>Kankana</em>, &amp;c. This must be <em>Buca Ráya</em> the First, and his reign must have continued much longer than is mentioned in the <em>Ráya Paditti</em>. He must also be the same prince mentioned in the inscription, page 170 (of this Volume), which shows that <em>Hari-hara</em> was not succeeded by his former companion <em>Buca</em>, but that he named his son and heir after that friend.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On my return, I met with an itinerant image of <em>Hanumanta</em>. He was in a palanquin, attended by a <em>Pújári</em>, and many <em>Vairagis</em>, and had tents, flags, <em>Thibet-tails</em>, and all other insignia of honour. He was on an expedition to collect the money that individuals in distress had vowed to his master <em>Vencata Ramanya</em>, the idol at <em>Tripathi</em>; and from his style of travelling seemed to have been successful. Many such collectors are constantly travelling about the Peninsula. Out of the contributions the <em>Pújári</em> (priest) defrays all the expenses of the party, and pays the balance into the treasury at <em>Tripathi</em>, which is one of the richest that the <em>Hindus</em> now possess.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dancing women.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At the temples here dancing girls are kept, which is not done any where on the coast toward the south; for in <em>Tulava</em> and <em>Malayala</em> many of the finest women are at all times devoted to the service of the <em>Bráhmans</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 28. <em>Gangawali</em> river.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">28th <em>February</em>.—I went three cosses to <em>Ancola</em>. Midway is the <em>Gangawali</em>, an inlet of salt water that separates <em>Haiga</em>, or <em>Haiva</em>, from <em>Kankana</em>. Its mouth toward the sea is narrow; but inwards it forms a lake, which is from one mile to half that extent in width, except at the ferry, where it contracts to four or five hundred yards. Boats of a considerable size (<em>Patemars</em>) can come over the bar, and ascend the river for three cosses. Canoes can go three cosses farther, to the foot of the <em>Ghats</em>. The boats of <em>Haiga</em> are the rudest of any that I have ever seen, and no where worse than on this river, which possesses no trade; and the country on its banks, although very beautiful, seems rather barren.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-199"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="199" data-folio="175" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n198_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 175">[p. 175]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Feb. 28. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Between <em>Gaukarna</em> and the river, the country consists of low hills, separated by rice grounds of very small extent. Where they are of any considerable size, the soil is very sandy. Soon after leaving the <em>Gangawali</em> I crossed a smaller salt water inlet, which by overflowing it at high water injures a good deal of land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Salt.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The salt made in this part of the country, where there are the same natural advantages as at <em>Goa</em>, is very bad, and scarcely saleable at any market; whereas at <em>Goa</em> vast quantities are made, and sent not only inland, but all over the coast. This seems to be an object that merits attention, so soon as the population shall have increased beyond what is adequate to cultivate the lands.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of <em>Kankana</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The part of <em>Kankana</em> through which I have passed resembles <em>Haiga</em>. The quantity of rice-land is pretty considerable. Most of it is what in <em>Malabar</em> would be called <em>Parum</em>, yet it produces annually a crop of rice, and much of it a second crop of pulse. Although this part of <em>Kankana</em>, which is subject to the British government, and forms the district (<em>Taluc</em>) of <em>Ancola</em>, is larger than either of the districts into which <em>Haiga</em> is divided, it produces only an annual revenue of 29,000 <em>Pagodas</em>; while <em>Honawera</em> produces 51,000, and <em>Kunda-pura</em> yields 50,000. This is not attended with any advantage to the inhabitants; for the houses of the proprietors and cultivators are greatly inferior in appearance to those in <em>Haiga</em>, <em>Tulava</em>, and <em>Malayala</em>. The low revenue is not therefore owing to the people being less burthened, nor is it owing to an inferiority in natural riches, but to a long unsettled state, which has occasioned a wonderful devastation. The officers of revenue say, that one-third of even the good lands are now waste. This devastation has been owing to the constant depredations of <em>Marattah</em> chiefs, and robbers of two casts which are called <em>Comarapeca</em> and <em>Halepeca</em>. A <em>Comarapeca</em> chief, named <em>Ghida Ganoji</em>, or the short <em>Ganésa</em>, having continued in his usual practices after the conquest by the English, Major Monro sent a party of <em>Sepoys</em>, who shot him; ever since which the country has been quiet.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-200"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="200" data-folio="176" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n199_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 176">[p. 176]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. Feb. 28. <em>Ancola.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Ancola</em> is a ruinous fort, with a small market (<em>Bazar</em>) near it. Robbers have frequently burned the market; but it is now recovering, and contains forty shops. It is not the custom here for the people to live in towns. A few shops are collected in one place; and all the other inhabitants of what is called a village are scattered upon their farms. Most of the people here are of <em>Karnata</em> extraction; and few of <em>Kankana</em> descent remain, except a particular kind of <em>Bráhmans</em>, who are all merchants, as those of <em>Haiga</em> are all cultivators. Being originally descended from <em>Pansh Gauda</em>, or <em>Bráhmans</em> of the north of <em>India</em>, those of <em>Kankana</em> are held in great contempt by the <em>Dravada Bráhmans</em>, or division of the south; one of the strongest reasons assigned for which is, that they eat fish.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 1.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 1.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">1st <em>March</em>.—I went five cosses to <em>Chandya</em>. At two computed cosses from <em>Ancola</em>, I crossed a considerable salt-water inlet called <em>Belicary</em>. The country between is level, but very sandy, and little cultivated. The banks of the <em>Belicary</em> are well planted with coco-nut gardens; and being broken into many islands and points are very beautiful. At the mouth, although it admits boats of some size (<em>Patemars</em>), it is not above two hundred yards wide. Small boats can ascend two cosses, to where the inlet receives from the <em>Ghats</em> a stream of fresh water. A little north from its mouth is a high island, called by the natives <em>Sonaka Guda</em>, which with a high promontory, projecting far to the west, forms a large bay, in which at this season there is scarcely any surf. Here the road for some way leads along the beach. At the head of the bay there is a fine plain between the hills toward the <em>Ghats</em>, and those forming the promontory which projects into the sea. The soil of this plain is good, but in many places is spoiled by the irruption of salt water creeks. Money has this year been advanced to make a bank, which which will be a great improvement. Toward the north the plain becomes narrower, and is overgrown with trees. Part of this has been formerly cultivated; and, if there were inhabitants, the whole might be rendered productive. Farther north the valley opens <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-201" data-page="201" data-folio="177" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n200_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 177">[p. 177]</button> again into a fine plain, which faces the sea on the north side of the promontory. From the sea on the south of this to that on the north, is computed three cosses, or about ten miles. On our maps this part of the coast appears to be very ill laid down. <em>Chandya</em> is in the plain at some distance from the sea. At this place there is no market (<em>Bazar</em>), but there are many scattered houses sheltered by groves of coco-nut palms.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Catechu.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 1.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this part of <em>Kankana</em>, a little <em>Cut</em>, <em>Catechu</em>, or <em>Terra Japonica</em>, is made by some poor people, who gave me the following account of the process. The tree, or <em>Mimosa Catechu</em>, is called here <em>Keiri</em>, and grows spontaneously on all the hills of <em>Kankana</em>, but no where else in the peninsula that I observed. It is felled at any season; and, the white wood being removed, the heart is cut into small bits, and put, with one half the quantity of water by measure, into a round-bellied earthen pot. It is then boiled for about three hours; and when the decoction has become ropy, it is decanted. The same quantity of water is again added, and boiled, until it becomes ropy; when it is decanted, and a third water also is given. This extracts all the substance from the wood. The three decoctions are then mixed, and next morning boiled in small pots, until the extract becomes thick, like tar. It is afterwards allowed to remain in the pots for two days, and then has become so hard, that it will not run. Some husks of rice are then spread on the ground, and the inspissated juice is formed into balls, about the size of oranges, which are placed on the husks, or on leaves, and dried seven days in the sun. For two months afterwards they are spread out in the shade to dry, or in the rainy season for twice that length of time, and are then fit for sale. Merchants who live above the <em>Ghats</em> advance the whole price four months before the time of delivery, and give 2 <em>Rupees</em> for a <em>Maund</em> of 40 <em>Cutcha Seers</em> of 24 <em>Rupees</em> weight; that is, for a hundred-weight 9 <sup>89</sup>⁄<sub>100</sub> <em>Rupees</em>, or nearly 1<em>l</em>. sterling. The merchants who purchase reside chiefly at <em>Darwara</em>, <em>Shanore</em>, and other parts in that neighbourhood, and are those who <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-202" data-page="202" data-folio="178" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n201_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 178">[p. 178]</button> supply the greater part of the peninsula with this article, which among the natives is in universal use. Their greatest supply comes from that part of <em>Kankana</em> which is subject to the <em>Marattahs</em>. The encouragement of this manufacture in British <em>Kankana</em> seems to merit attention. The tree is exactly the same with what I found used for the like purpose in the dominions of <em>Ava</em>, and does not agree very well with the descriptions in the <em>Supplementum Plantarum</em> of the younger Linnæus, nor in Dr. Roxburgh’s manuscripts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 2. Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 2.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">2d <em>March.</em>—I went three cosses to <em>Sedásiva-ghur</em>. The road passes over two steep ridges of hills, running out into two promontories, between which is a bay sheltered by the island of <em>Angediva</em>, belonging to, and inhabited by the Portuguese. South from the island are two small hummocks, and off the southern promontory are some high rocks. The appearance of the whole from land renders it probable, that shelter might be found here for ships, even during the south-west monsoon. In the plain round this bay the soil is tolerably good. On the plain north from the two ridges it is very sandy, and much spoiled by salt water, which soaks through any such banks as can be formed of the loose materials that are procurable. The coco-nut is perhaps the production which would thrive best; but a great part of the plain is waste, and covered with bushes of the <em>Cassuvium</em>, called <em>Govay</em> by the natives, from its having been introduced from America by the Portuguese of <em>Goa</em>. The river of <em>Sedásiva-ghur</em> is a very wide and deep inlet of the sea. The passage into it is intricate, but at the height of the tide contains 25 feet water. It is sheltered in a deep bay by three islands, one of which, called by the natives <em>Karmaguda</em>, is fortified. The entrance is commanded by the fort, which is situated on a lofty hill. Much land in this vicinity has fallen into the hands of government, and, owing to the depredations of the <em>Comarapeca</em> robbers, has become waste. One of their chiefs, named <em>Venja Náyaka</em>, was the terror of the whole country, and forced even <em>Bráhmans</em> to adopt his cast. Two of his sons were hanged by <em>Tippoo</em>; but, until <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-203" data-page="203" data-folio="179" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n202_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 179">[p. 179]</button> terrified by the firmness of Major Monro’s government, he continued obstinate in his evil practices. Soon after that gentleman’s arrival, he made his submission, and continues to behave like a good subject. I found him very ready to give me assistance in procuring supplies, and means to transport my baggage; and from the mildness of his manners, until informed by the officers of revenue, I had no idea of his disposition, which was barbarous in the extreme.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 3. Commerce.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">3d <em>March.</em>—I remained at <em>Sedásiva-ghur</em> taking some account of the state of British <em>Kankana</em>, and making preparations for my journey up the <em>Ghats</em>. The <em>Petta</em>, or town, here contains about twenty very wretched shops: all the other inhabitants live scattered on their farms. <em>Cadawada</em>, or as we usually pronounce it <em>Carwar</em>, stood about three miles above <em>Sedásiva-ghur</em>, on the opposite bank of the river. It was formerly a noted seat of European commerce, but during the <em>Sultán’s</em> reign has gone to total ruin. There are here at present some merchants from the <em>Marattah</em> dominions above the <em>Ghats</em>, who say that they came chiefly with a view of purchasing salt. They also procure here a considerable quantity of <em>Cut</em>, none of which grows above the <em>Ghats</em>. They purchase it for ready money from the merchants of the country, who make the advances to the manufacturers. It is of a very good quality; and they cannot afford to give more than 10 <em>Sultany Pagodas</em> for the <em>Candaca</em>, or <em>Candy</em> of 40 <em>Maunds</em> of 48 <em>Seers</em> each; that is, 40 <em>Rupees</em> for the <em>Candy</em> of 582½ lb., or 15 <em>s.</em> 5 <em>d.</em> a hundred-weight.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. <em>March</em> 3.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 3.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It would appear, that at one time all the lands of this district (<em>Taluc</em>) belonged to <em>Jain</em> landlords (<em>Mulagars</em>); but all these have either been killed, or so oppressed that they have disappeared. After their expulsion, part of the lands were annexed to the government, and part given to landlords (<em>Mulagars</em>) called <em>Hubbu Bráhmans</em>. These are of the <em>Pansh Dravada</em> division; but are considered as having been degraded by <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, and are now reduced to a miserable state of ignorance. None of them here can give any <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-204" data-page="204" data-folio="180" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n203_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 180">[p. 180]</button> account of the time when they came into the country, who brought them, or whence they came. They are the common <em>Panchangas</em>, or almanac-keepers of the country, and in some temples are priests (<em>Pújáris</em>); but <em>Sujéswara</em>, the most celebrated temple in the country, and one of those built by <em>Ravana</em> king of <em>Lanca</em>, is in possession of a colony of <em>Marattah Bráhmans</em>, who were introduced by <em>Mahomed Adil Shah</em> of <em>Vijaya-pura</em>. Of the history of the country these know nothing, except the legends concerning the foundation of their temple that are to be found in the eighteen <em>Puránas</em>. The lands formerly granted to the <em>Hubbu Bráhmans</em>, and which form by far the greater part of the country, are called <em>Mula</em> lands, and may be transferred by sale whenever the proprietor pleases. The <em>Hubbus</em> have indeed alienated a great part of it to <em>Marattahs</em>, <em>Kankana Bráhmans</em>, and <em>Comarapeca</em>. It may be also transferred on mortgage, resumable at pleasure by paying the debt. This tenure is here called <em>Adava</em>. The <em>Shist</em>, or assessment, now in use, was made by <em>Sherif un Mulk</em>, the <em>Vazir</em> of <em>Ponday</em> already mentioned; and was formed by laying so much on the land, according to its soil, and the quantity of rice seed that it was supposed capable of sowing. The proprietor may cultivate it with whatever he pleases, and may plant it with palms without any additional tax. Since the time of <em>Sherif un Mulk</em>, a small tax has been imposed on every coco-nut tree; and at different times, by imposing a per centage (<em>Pagadiputti</em>) on the amount of each person’s land-tax, an increase of revenue has been made. Major Monro, according to the account of the revenue officers, considerably reduced the rate of the land-tax; but owing to his care, and strictness in the collections, the revenue which he raised was much greater than was ever before realised. The proprietors allege, that they paid more to him than they did to <em>Tippoo</em>. The two accounts are very reconcileable; as under the inspection of Mr. Monro there was little room for the corrupt practices which in the <em>Sultán’s</em> government were very prevalent. Disputes about landed property are very common. An <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-205" data-page="205" data-folio="181" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n204_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 181">[p. 181]</button> estate paying four <em>Pagodas</em> of revenue can be mortgaged for a hundred <em>Pagodas</em>, and the mortgagee pays the taxes. The same estate will sell for one hundred and fifty <em>Pagodas</em>. The government lands are let at rack rent, which is of course higher than the tax (<em>Shist</em>) paid by the proprietors (<em>Mulagars</em>). The tenants on these lands, or <em>Circar Cutties</em>, cannot be turned out of their farms so long as they pay the rent, the leases being in perpetuity. They can neither sell nor mortgage their lease; but they may let it to an undertenant. By far the greater part of the cultivation is carried on by the proprietors (<em>Mulagars</em>) and tenants of the public (<em>Circar Cutties</em>), and very little by lease-holders. The sizes of the farms vary from one to five ploughs. Two oxen are required to each plough, which cultivates from five to seven <em>Candies</em> of land. In general, the family of the proprietor labours the farm, but a few rich men employ hired servants. There are here no slaves. Men servants get yearly from two to six <em>Pagodas</em>, or from 16<em>s.</em> 1½<em>d.</em> to 48<em>s.</em> 4½<em>d.</em>; but those, who get only the first sum in money, have daily one meal of rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cultivation of watered-fields, and of gardens, both on the same kind of land, is the only one known in British <em>Kankana</em>, except the <em>Cumri</em>, or <em>Cotu Cadu</em>, called here <em>Culumbi</em>. There is no ground from which two crops of rice in one year are taken; but, while most of the rice grows in the rainy season, some land called <em>Vaingunna</em> is so low, that in the rainy season it cannot be cultivated, and, after the water has evaporated, this yields a crop. All the other land is called <em>Surd</em>, and is mostly what in <em>Malabar</em> would be called <em>Majelu</em>, and what the people of <em>Tulava</em> would call <em>Betta</em>. In the accompanying Table may be seen several particulars relative to the cultivation of rice, which were taken from the accounts of the cultivators. The <em>Wala</em> requires a clayey soil, and its produce is great; but the quantity of this soil is very small. All the <em>Surd</em> land requires manure. The seed season for dry-seed is the month preceding midsummer; and that for sprouted-seed is the month following. In <em>Vaingunna</em>, or inundated land, according as the water evaporates, the seed season continues during the two months previous to and one month after the winter solstice. The fields are watered from small <em>Tanks</em>, which in such low situations do not suddenly dry up, and contain the water at from one to two feet below the surface. It is raised by means of a trough, which moves upon a pivot near the centre; so that one of its ends may be immersed into the water, while its lighter end hangs over the field.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-207"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="207" data-folio="183" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n206_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 183">[p. 183]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-006"><img src="/figures/journey-from-madras-vol-iii/fig-006_p0207.webp" alt="Diagram of a Yatam (water-lifting lever and trough)." loading="lazy"></figure><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. They then raise it by the <em>Yatam</em>, and the water runs out upon the field by the light end. Two men with a basket and ropes would throw out four times as much water, but it would be hard work.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pulse.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Upon good <em>Surd</em> land may be procured a second crop of the following leguminous plants:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li><em>Udied, Phaseolus minimoo</em> Roxb: MSS.</li><li><em>Mung, Phaseolus mungo.</em></li><li><em>Cultie, Dolichos biflorus.</em></li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These are cultivated in the same manner as the pulses in <em>Haiga</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manure.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the rainy season the cattle are kept in the house, and, to increase the quantity of manure, are littered with fresh leaves. In the dry season they are shut up at night in pens, which are placed on the <em>Surd</em> lands, and are shifted once in four days. Every morning some dry soil is mixed with the foregoing night’s dung, and the whole is made smooth, that the cattle may lie clean. The manure collected in the rainy season is given to the soil of the first and second quality, which are always sown with rice after the dry-seed cultivation. The ashes of the family are kept separate, but are used for the same kind of land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cattle.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 3.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cattle here are of the same small kinds that are to be found on the coast to the southward. A great many of them are brought <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-208" data-page="208" data-folio="184" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n207_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 184">[p. 184]</button> from above the <em>Ghats</em>. At this season they are in a most wretched condition, and are supported entirely on straw; for in <em>Kankana</em> no hay is made. In this part of the country few buffaloes are employed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Comarapeca</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 3.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Comarapeca</em> are a tribe of <em>Kankana</em> descent, and seem to be the <em>Súdras</em> of pure birth, who properly belong to the country; in the same manner as the <em>Nairs</em> are the pure <em>Súdras</em> of <em>Malayala</em>. By birth they are all cultivators and soldiers; and, as usual with this class of men among the <em>Hindus</em>, are all strongly inclined to be robbers. From the anarchy which has long prevailed in this neighbourhood, they had acquired an extraordinary degree of cruelty, and had even compelled many <em>Bráhmans</em> to assume their customs, and adopt their cast. They have hereditary chiefs called <em>Náyakas</em>, who, as usual, with the assistance of a council, can expel from the cast, and settle disputes among their inferiors. A man’s own children are his heirs. They can read poetical legends, and are permitted to eat meat and drink spirituous liquors. Their women are not marriageable after the age of puberty. Widows ought to burn themselves with the bodies of their husbands, but this barbarity is no longer in use. Widows, and women who have been divorced for adultery with a <em>Bráhman</em> or <em>Comarapeca</em>, may be taken into a kind of left-hand marriage; but their children are despised, and no person of a pure descent will marry them. A woman cannot be divorced for any other cause than adultery; if the crime has been committed with any man but a <em>Bráhman</em>, or <em>Comarapeca</em>, she loses cast. The men may take as many wives as they please. The <em>Sringa</em>-<em>giri Swamalu</em> is their <em>Guru</em>. He receives their <em>Dharma</em>, and bestows on them <em>Upadesa</em>, holy-water, consecrated ashes, and the like. The <em>Panchanga</em>, or astrologer of the village, is their <em>Puróhita</em>, and reads prayers (<em>Mantrams</em>) at marriages, <em>Namacurna</em> (the giving a child its name), <em>Tithi</em>, <em>Amávásya</em>, &amp;c. &amp;c. They worship the great gods, <em>Siva</em> and <em>Vishnu</em>, in temples where <em>Kankana Bráhmans</em> are <em>Pújáris</em>. They offer bloody sacrifices; and at the temples of the <em>Saktis</em>, or <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-209" data-page="209" data-folio="185" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n208_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 185">[p. 185]</button> destructive spirits, such as <em>Dava Dévaru</em>, and <em>Marca Devi</em>, whose priests (<em>Pújáris</em>) are called <em>Gurus</em>, they swing suspended by iron hooks which are passed through the skin of their backs. The spirits of children, whose mothers die during pregnancy, are supposed to become <em>Butas</em>, or devils, and to occasion much trouble to those unfortunate persons into whom they enter. The sufferers attempt to be relieved of them by prayer and sacrifice, and some village people are imagined to be possessed of invocations (<em>Mantrams</em>) capable of expelling these evil spirits. The <em>Comarapecas</em> suppose that the spirits of good men go to <em>Moesha</em>, a pretence that is looked upon by the <em>Bráhmans</em> as very impudent; for they think that such a place is far beyond the reach of a <em>Súdra</em>. For the spirits of bad men the <em>Comarapeca</em> do not know any place of punishment, nor do they know what becomes of such after death.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Bráhmans</em> of <em>Kankana.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Bráhmans</em> properly belonging to <em>Kankana</em>, and who alledge that they are the descendants of the colony to whom the country was given by <em>Parasu Ráma</em>, are of the <em>Pansh Gauda</em> division. <em>Goa</em>, called by them <em>Govay</em>, seems to have been their principal seat. After being expelled thence by the Portuguese, they dispersed, and have now mostly become traders. A few are still priests (<em>Pújáris</em>), and a very small number call themselves <em>Vaidikas</em>. All those who are here are very ignorant, and do not pretend to say when the <em>Jain</em> and <em>Panch Dravada Bráhmans</em> came in upon them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 4. Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Gopi-chitty.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 4.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">4<em>th March.</em>—I went three cosses to <em>Gopi-chitty</em>. For the first part of the journey the road led through a level country, with a few small hills scattered at some distance, and a pretty good soil. It afterwards passed among low hills covered with wood. In many places here, the soil seems good, and the trees are tall; so that pepper might probably be cultivated to advantage. In many other places the hills are barren, producing nothing but bushes, or stunted trees: among them I saw no <em>Teak</em>. <em>Gopi-chitty</em> is a village containing eight houses. Owing to the disturbed state of the country, it had for twenty years been entirely deserted; but the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-210" data-page="210" data-folio="186" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n209_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 186">[p. 186]</button> confidence of enjoying security under Major Monro’s authority, has induced the present inhabitants to settle in the place, and they have already cleared a considerable extent of the rice ground, which consequently belongs entirely to the government. The lower part of the valley, toward the great river, has been destroyed by the breaking down of the dykes that kept out the tide. To repair these, would cost <em>25 Pagodas</em>, which is more than the tenants can afford or choose to advance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">History of the part of <em>Kankana</em> subject to the British.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This part of <em>Kankana</em>, on the fall of the <em>Sultans</em> of <em>Vijaya-pura</em>, became subject to the <em>Rájás</em> of <em>Sudha</em>, which we call <em>Soonda</em>. One of these, named <em>Sedásiva Row</em>, built the fort at the mouth of the river, and called it after his own name. The dialect of <em>Kankana</em> is used by the natives of this place in their own houses; but, from having been long subject to <em>Vijaya-pura</em>, almost all of them can speak the <em>Marattah</em> language, which has a very strong affinity with the <em>Hinduy</em> that is spoken on the banks of the <em>Ganges</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 5. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">5th <em>March.</em>—I went four cosses to <em>Caderi</em>, and did not see a house the whole way; but the heads of some cultivated vallies approach near the road, and extend from thence toward the river. I passed through many places that formerly have been cultivated, but are now waste, and through some places where the soil seems fit for cultivation, but which probably have never been cleared. The trees in some places are of a good size, but none of them are very valuable. The people whom I took with me for the purpose gave me the following account of such as I observed by the way.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Forests.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The most common is the prickly <em>Bamboo</em>, called <em>Colaki</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Cussum</em>, or the <em>Shaguda</em> of my MSS. March 5.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The forests are the property of the gods of the villages in which they are situated, and the trees ought not to be cut without having <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-212" data-page="212" data-folio="188" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n211_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 188">[p. 188]</button> obtained leave from the <em>Gauda</em>, or head man of the village, whose office is hereditary, and who here also is priest (<em>Pújári</em>) to the temple of the village god. The idol receives nothing for granting this permission; but the neglect of the ceremony of asking his leave brings his vengeance on the guilty person. This seems, therefore, merely a contrivance to prevent the government from claiming the property. Each village has a different god, some male, some female, but by the <em>Bráhmans</em> they are all called <em>Saktis</em> (powers), as requiring bloody sacrifices to appease their wrath.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No persons here collect honey or wax.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Caderi</em>. Unhealthy nature of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">River of <em>Sedásiva-ghur</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Caderi</em> at present contains only two houses, with one man and a lad, besides women. It was formerly a place of note; but for several years a great sickness has prevailed, and has swept off nearly all the inhabitants. This is attributed to the vengeance of some enraged <em>Buta</em>, or devil; but may be accounted for from the neighbouring country having been laid waste, and being over-run with forests. On the banks of the river at <em>Caderi</em> there was a fort, which was destroyed by <em>Hyder</em>, and the garrison sent to occupy the fortified island at the mouth of the river. General Mathews, the natives say, took possession of the ruins, erected some works, and left a garrison, which held out until the peace of <em>Mangalore</em>. Most of the cultivators lived on the opposite side of the river. Those who resided near the fort were chiefly traders; and there is still a weekly fair at the place, to which many people resort. This seems to be the reason why the few remaining inhabitants continue in such a situation. They are <em>Bráhmans</em>; and from those who frequent the fair they receive considerable contributions. <em>Patemars</em>, or large craft, can ascend almost to the fort, and canoes can go two miles above it. The water is quite fresh. The encouraging of a market (<em>Bazar</em>) here seems to be an object of importance, and a mean likely to bring back a great trade to this river, which by nature has many advantages.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-213"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="213" data-folio="189" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n212_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 189">[p. 189]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 6. Robbers suppressed.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">6th <em>March.</em>—I went four cosses to <em>Avila-gotna</em>, without having seen the smallest trace of cultivation, or of inhabitants. The country is not, however, entirely a desert. Small villages are scattered through the forests, and hidden in its recesses. Formerly the inhabitants of these lived in a constant defiance of the rest of mankind, robbing whoever unfortunately came within their power, and continually on the alarm to defend themselves from their neighbours. This manner of living has however been entirely stopped. Major Monro, by taking advantage of the terror inspired by the fall of <em>Seringapatam</em>, and by an instant punishment of the first transgressor, has made every thing quiet; and there is reason to think that a defenceless man may now traverse these forests without danger from his fellow-creatures. Tigers are said to be very numerous; and, to lessen the danger to be apprehended from them, the traders who frequent the road have cleared many places where they may encamp, and these are prevented from being overgrown by annually burning the long grass. On one of these clear places I halted, having at no great distance a village of thieves.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The country through which I passed to-day was in general level, with hills near the road toward the left, and a ridge to the right at about four or five miles distance. This ridge is that which runs out into the sea to form the southern boundary of the bay of <em>Sedásiva</em>-<em>ghur</em>. The trees are in general high, with many <em>Bamboos</em> intermixed. The soil is apparently good, and a large proportion of it is sufficiently level for the plough. Near <em>Avila-gotna</em> I crossed the river, which here assumes a very singular appearance. Its channel is about half a mile wide, and consists of a confused mass of rocks, gravel, and sand, intersected by small limpid streams, and overgrown with various trees and shrubs which delight in such situations. In the rainy season, it swells into tremendous torrents, but never fills the channel from bank to bank. It is then, however, quite impassable. At present its clear streams, with the fresh <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-214" data-page="214" data-folio="190" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n213_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 190">[p. 190]</button> verdure of the plants growing near them, are very pleasant, after having come through the forest, whose leaves at this season drop; for all the juices of the trees are dried up by the arid heat of this climate, in the same manner as they are by the cold of an European winter. The nights, however, are at present cool, but the days are burning hot. Near the sea a more equable temperature prevails.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">March 7.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">7th <em>March.</em>—Although before leaving <em>Sedásiva-ghur</em>, I had collected the persons who were said to be best informed concerning the road, and had procured from them a list of stages said to be distant from each other three or four cosses, that is, about ten or thirteen miles; yet to-day I came to my stage at <em>Déva-kara</em>, after less than an hour’s journey.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The road passes along the south side of the river; and toward the east the valley becomes narrower, and more uneven; but still much of it is fit for the plough. From the stunted appearance of the trees, I conclude that the soil is worse than that on yesterday’s route. At <em>Déva-kara</em> there is a good deal of ground cleared, and formed into rice fields; but the people of eight houses, which form the village, are not able to cultivate the whole. The ground that is cleared is by no means equal either in soil or levelness, to much of what I saw waste on the two last days’ journey; but it is finely watered by a stream that even now affords a great supply. The river at <em>Déva-kara</em> is a rapid stream full of small islands; but not so much broken as at <em>Avila-gotna</em>, and of course narrower. In the rainy season it is quite impassable; and then, although very rapid, swells at least ten feet above its present level.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Unhealthy nature of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At the commencement of the last rainy season, this village contained twelve houses; but, twenty persons having died, four of the houses are now deserted. It is looked upon as certain death, for any stranger to attempt to settle in this place.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Robbers.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER XVI. March 7.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Here was the residence of a very notorious robber, who died in consequence of the wounds that he received from the party which <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-215" data-page="215" data-folio="191" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n214_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 191">[p. 191]</button> Major Monro sent to apprehend him. March 7.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">“All sandal trees,” says Mr. Read, “growing upon private lands are considered as the property of the government; but it would be ridiculous to suppose, that they will always be considered as such by the occupiers of estates, who undoubtedly commit frequent depredations upon them. The whole might be easily collected at <em>Onore</em> (<em>Honawera</em>), and taken up by one of the Indiamen passing from <em>Bombay</em> to <em>China</em>.” Mr. Read was probably not aware, that last year all the ripe sandal in <em>Mysore</em> had been cut, and a great danger has consequently been incurred of glutting the market; while some years hence it will probably be greatly enhanced in value. I have already mentioned, that some measure should be adopted for regulating the cutting of the sandal wood; so that a certain supply should annually be brought to market, and no more permitted to grow than can be disposed of to advantage; for it must be considered as a mere superfluous luxury, the only proper use of which is to become a source of as <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-217" data-page="217" data-folio="193" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra03hami_2/page/n216_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 193">[p. 193]</button> much revenue as possible. As the Company and the <em>Mysore Rájá</em> are in the sole possession of the countries which produce it, the arrangement might be readily made on somewhat like the following plan. An estimate of the quantity annually saleable, and of the whole produce that grows in both territories, having been formed, an agreement might be made, that each party should furnish the annual supply for a number of years, in proportion to the whole quantity that grows in his country. For instance, the <em>Mysore Rájá</em> might furnish the supply for nineteen years, and the Company for one, which I imagine is somewhat about the relative proportion of what the two territories produce. The parties, of course, would be tied down to sell no more than a certain weight each year. They might improve its quality, as much as they could; and public sales, such as the Company use in <em>Bengal</em> for opium and salt, I am persuaded would be found by far the most advantageous manner of disposing of this article.
