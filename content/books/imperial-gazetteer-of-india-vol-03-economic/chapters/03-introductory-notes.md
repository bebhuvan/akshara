<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-11"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="11" data-folio="v" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette03hunt/page/n10_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page v">[p. v]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">INTRODUCTORY NOTES</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Notes on Transliteration</span></h3><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Vowel-Sounds</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">a has the sound of <em>a</em> in ‘woman.’ ā has the sound of <em>a</em> in ‘father.’ e has the vowel-sound in ‘grey.’ i has the sound of <em>i</em> in ‘pin.’ ī has the sound of <em>i</em> in ‘police.’ o has the sound of <em>o</em> in ‘bone.’ u has the sound of <em>u</em> in ‘bull.’ ū has the sound of <em>u</em> in ‘flute.’ ai has the vowel-sound in ‘mine.’ au has the vowel-sound in ‘house.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It should be stated that no attempt has been made to distinguish between the long and short sounds of <em>e</em> and <em>o</em> in the Dravidian languages, which possess the vowel-sounds in ‘bet’ and ‘hot’ in addition to those given above. Nor has it been thought necessary to mark vowels as long in cases where mistakes in pronunciation were not likely to be made.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Consonants</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Most Indian languages have different forms for a number of consonants, such as <em>d</em>, <em>t</em>, <em>r</em>, &amp;c., marked in scientific works by the use of dots or italics. As the European ear distinguishes these with difficulty in ordinary pronunciation, it has been considered undesirable to embarrass the reader with them; and only two notes are required. In the first place, the Arabic <em>k</em>, a strong guttural, has been represented by <em>k</em> instead of <em>q</em>, which is often used. Secondly, it should be remarked that aspirated consonants are common; and, in particular, <em>dh</em> and <em>th</em> (except in Burma) never have the sound of <em>th</em> in ‘this’ or ‘thin,’ but should be pronounced as in ‘woodhouse’ and ‘boathook.’</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-12"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="12" data-folio="vi" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette03hunt/page/n11_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page vi">[p. vi]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Burmese Words</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Burmese and some of the languages on the frontier of China have the following special sounds :— aw has the vowel-sound in ‘law.’ ö and ü are pronounced as in German. gy is pronounced almost like <em>j</em> in ‘jewel.’ ky is pronounced almost like <em>ch</em> in ‘church.’ th is pronounced in some cases as in ‘this,’ in some cases as in ‘thin.’ w after a consonant has the force of <em>uw</em>. Thus, <em>ywa</em> and <em>pwe</em> are disyllables, pronounced as if written <em>yuwa</em> and <em>puwe</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It should also be noted that, whereas in Indian words the accent or stress is distributed almost equally on each syllable, in Burmese there is a tendency to throw special stress on the last syllable.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>General</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The names of some places—e. g. Calcutta, Bombay, Lucknow, Cawnpore—have obtained a popular fixity of spelling, while special forms have been officially prescribed for others. Names of persons are often spelt and pronounced differently in different parts of India ; but the variations have been made as few as possible by assimilating forms almost alike, especially where a particular spelling has been generally adopted in English books.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Notes on Money, Prices, Weights and Measures</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As the currency of India is based upon the rupee, all statements with regard to money throughout the <em>Gazetteer</em> have necessarily been expressed in rupees, nor has it been found possible to add generally a conversion into sterling. Down to about 1873 the gold value of the rupee (containing 165 grains of pure silver) was approximately equal to 2<em>s</em>., or one-tenth of a £; and for that period it is easy to convert rupees into sterling by striking off the final cipher (Rs. 1,000 = £100). But after 1873, owing to the depreciation of silver as compared with gold throughout the world, there came a serious and progressive fall in the exchange, until at one time the gold value of the rupee dropped as low as 1<em>s</em>. In order to provide a remedy for the heavy loss caused to the Government of India in respect of its gold payments to be made in England, and also to relieve foreign trade and finance from the inconvenience due to constant and unforeseen fluctuations in exchange, it was resolved in 1893 to close the mints to the free coinage of silver, and thus force up the value of the rupee by restricting the circulation. The intention was to raise <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-13" data-page="13" data-folio="vii" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette03hunt/page/n12_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page vii">[p. vii]</button> the exchange value of the rupee to 1<em>s.</em> 4<em>d.</em>, and then introduce a gold standard (though not necessarily a gold currency) at the rate of Rs. 15 = £1. This policy has been completely successful. From 1899 onwards the value of the rupee has been maintained, with insignificant fluctuations, at the proposed rate of 1<em>s.</em> 4<em>d.</em>; and consequently since that date three rupees have been equivalent to two rupees before 1873. For the intermediate period, between 1873 and 1899, it is manifestly impossible to adopt any fixed sterling value for a constantly changing rupee. But since 1899, if it is desired to convert rupees into sterling, not only must the final cipher be struck off (as before 1873), but also one-third must be subtracted from the result. Thus Rs. 1,000 = £100 - ⅓ = (about) £67.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Another matter in connexion with the expression of money statements in terms of rupees requires to be explained. The method of numerical notation in India differs from that which prevails throughout Europe. Large numbers are not punctuated in hundreds of thousands and millions, but in lakhs and crores. A lakh is one hundred thousand (written out as 1,00,000), and a crore is one hundred lakhs or ten millions (written out as 1,00,00,000). Consequently, according to the exchange value of the rupee, a lakh of rupees (Rs. 1,00,000) may be read as the equivalent of £10,000 before 1873, and as the equivalent of (about) £6,667 after 1899; while a crore of rupees (Rs. 1,00,00,000) may similarly be read as the equivalent of £1,000,000 before 1873, and as the equivalent of (about) £666,667 after 1899.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Finally, it should be mentioned that the rupee is divided into 16 annas, a fraction commonly used for many purposes by both natives and Europeans. The anna was formerly reckoned as 1½<em>d.</em>; it may now be considered as exactly corresponding to 1<em>d.</em> The anna is again subdivided into 12 pies.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The various systems of weights used in India combine uniformity of scale with immense variations in the weight of units. The scale used generally throughout Northern India, and less commonly in Madras and Bombay, may be thus expressed : one maund = 40 seers ; one seer = 16 chittaks or 80 tolas. The actual weight of a seer varies greatly from District to District, and even from village to village ; but in the standard system the tola is 180 grains Troy (the exact weight of the rupee), and the seer thus weighs 2·057 lb., and the maund 82·28 lb. This standard is used in official reports and throughout the <em>Gazetteer</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For calculating retail prices, the universal custom in India is to express them in terms of seers to the rupee. Thus, when prices change, what varies is not the amount of money to be paid for the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-14" data-page="14" data-folio="viii" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette03hunt/page/n13_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page viii">[p. viii]</button> same quantity, but the quantity to be obtained for the same amount of money. In other words, prices in India are quantity prices, not money prices. When the figure of quantity goes up, this of course means that the price has gone down, which is at first sight perplexing to an English reader. It may, however, be mentioned that quantity prices are not altogether unknown in England, especially at small shops, where pennyworths of many groceries can be bought. Eggs, likewise, are commonly sold at a varying number for the shilling. If it be desired to convert quantity prices from Indian into English denominations without having recourse to money prices (which would often be misleading), the following scale may be adopted—based upon the assumptions that a seer is exactly 2 lb., and that the value of the rupee remains constant at 1<em>s</em>. 4<em>d</em>. : 1 seer per rupee = (about) 3 lb. for 2<em>s</em>. ; 2 seers per rupee = (about) 6 lb. for 2<em>s</em>. ; and so on.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The name of the unit for square measurement in India generally is the <em>bīgha</em>, which varies greatly in different parts of the country. But areas have always been expressed throughout the <em>Gazetteer</em> either in square miles or in acres.</p></div>
