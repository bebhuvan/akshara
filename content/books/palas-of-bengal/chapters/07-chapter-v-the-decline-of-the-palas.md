Rebellion in Northern Bengal.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">War in Northern Bengal. Death of Mahīpāla II.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Accession of Śūrapāla II, ignored by Sandhyākara Nandī but recorded in the Manahali grant of Madanapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After the death of Vigrahapāla III, his eldest son Mahīpāla II ascended the throne of his ancestors. According to the author of Rāmacarita, untoward things began to happen in this reign.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-001" href="#sec-07-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> He did not act according to the advice of his ministers and was not well disposed towards his remaining brothers Śūrapāla and Rāmapāla. He was told by the people that Rāmapāla was an able Prince, as well as a popular and vigorous administrator, and that he would kill him and take away his kingdom. So, by low cunning, he tried to kill him, and at last succeeded in confining him in a prison.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-002" href="#sec-07-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> It appears that Mahīpāla's younger brother Śūrapāla was sent to prison at the same time as his youngest brother Rāmapāla :—<em>Apareṇa bhrāttrā Śūrapālena saha kaṣṭāgāraṁ kārāgṛhaṁ mahattavanaṁ rakṣaṇam yatra</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-003" href="#sec-07-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> This Śūrapāla was older than Rāmapāla, because the author of the Rāmacarita states, that Rāmapāla's son succeeded to the throne, though Śūrapāla was Rāmapāla's elder.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-004" href="#sec-07-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The brothers were reduced to very great straits while in prison.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-005" href="#sec-07-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> The author adds in another place that both brothers were sent to prison because Mahīpāla had apprehensions of being dethroned by them.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-006" href="#sec-07-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> About this time Divvoka, a former servant, by cunning, took away a part of Rāmapāla's paternal kingdom Varendrī.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-007" href="#sec-07-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup> Mahīpāla went to fight against the confederate rebels with the small force at his command and fell in battle. This happened while Rāmapāla was in prison. ? <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-008" href="#sec-07-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">8</a></sup> Elsewhere it is specified that the Kaivartta King killed Mahīpāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-009" href="#sec-07-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">9</a></sup> After Mahīpāla's death Rāmapāla seems to have been set free, but driven out of the country, as the author of Rāmacarita states, that Rāmapāla became careless of his body and mind, because he was kept out of his kingdom.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-010" href="#sec-07-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">10</a></sup> Nothing is known about the period following the death of Mahīpāla II up to the accession of Rāmapāla. Śūrapāla II seems to have been recognized by the adherents of the Pāla Princes as the successor of Mahīpāla II, as he is mentioned by name in the Manahali grant of Madanapāladeva. The importance of this grant lies in the fact that it does not ignore a single king of the Pāla dynasty from Gopāla I to Madanapāla. Thus it might have omitted the names of Gopāla III and Kumārapāla, because these two Princes are not ascendants of Madanapāla and such names are usually omitted in the genealogical part of a copperplate grant. If Śūrapāla II had not actually reigned his name would have surely been omitted from this grant. For a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-55" data-page="55" data-folio="85" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n54_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 85">[p. 85]</button> similar reason we do not find the name of Rājyapāla, the eldest son of Rāmapāla , who could not have reigned as he died in his father’s lifetime:—</p></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-005"><img src="/figures/palas-of-bengal/fig-005_p0055.webp" alt="Genealogical tree of Vigrahapāla III, showing sons Mahīpāla II, Śūrapāla, and Rāmapāla; with Rājyapāla (died in his father&#x27;s lifetime) under Rāmapāla, Kumārapāla and Madanapāla, and Gopāla III under Kumārapāla." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Genealogical tree of Vigrahapāla III, showing sons Mahīpāla II, Śūrapāla, and Rāmapāla; with Rājyapāla (died in his father's lifetime) under Rāmapāla, Kumārapāla and Madanapāla, and Gopāla III under Kumārapāla.</figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rāmapāla is urged by his son and his friends to take the field against Bhīma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The mention of Śūrapāla’s accession to the throne or the recognition of his chiefship in the Pāla dominions, may have been omitted by Sandhyākaranandi, either through carelessness, or as not being relevant to his subject. It may also be possible that Śūrapāla was Rāmapāla’s rival for the throne, and though he had succeeded temporarily he was overthrown in the long run and perhaps murdered at the instigation of his younger brother. Nothing is known about the extent of Śūrapāla’s reign or his death. But it is quite certain that he was succeeded by his younger brother Rāmapāla. In the course of time Divvoka had died and was succeeded by his brother Rudoka. Rudoka was succeeded by his son Bhīma, who on his succession, began to harass the people, living in the tract of land, which was still left in the possession of the Pālas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-011" href="#sec-07-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> At that time Rāmapāla was in great straits and thought himself to be without friends.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-014" href="#sec-07-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> But his son and his advisors sought him, and urged him to take the necessary steps, and he regained courage.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-017" href="#sec-07-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The author of the Rāmacarita states in another place that he became very anxious to fight with Bhīma.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-012" href="#sec-07-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> His first step was to travel round the country to propitiate the feudatories and</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">He obtains aid from the feudatories and his cousin Śivarāja crosses the Ganges and enters the enemy’s country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">subordinate kings of his father’s kingdom, and he succeeded in gaining over the forest feudatories.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-015" href="#sec-07-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> During his travels he became convinced that all feudatories were well disposed towards him.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-018" href="#sec-07-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> By giving away lands along river banks and immense wealth, Rāmapāla succeeded in obtaining horse and foot soldiers and elephants from the feudatories. Śivarāja, the son of his maternal uncle and a Mahāpratīhāra, crossed the Ganges with foot, horse and elephants and entered the enemy’s country.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-013" href="#sec-07-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup> This expedition was undertaken either to reconnoitre the enemy’s position or as a sort of counter raid. Śivarāja so impetuously attacked Varendrī that the viṣayas and grāmas in Bhīma’s country became</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">distressed. Śivarāja began to enquire about the ownership of the lands so that the properties of the gods and the Brāhmaṇas might be protected.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-016" href="#sec-07-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">8</a></sup> He succeeded in driving away Bhīma’s followers from Vārendrī proper<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-019" href="#sec-07-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">9</a></sup>, and then came back to <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-56" data-page="56" data-folio="86" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n55_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 86">[p. 86]</button> report to Rāmapāla that his paternal kingdom was free of intruders.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-020" href="#sec-07-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Sandhyākara Nandi is silent about the events which followed this raid into Vārendrī. It appears from the second chapter of his work that the effect of Śivarāja's success in Northern Bengal was only temporary, because it became necessary for Rāmapāla to lead another and much bigger army into Northern Bengal, accompanied by his principal feudatories. One particular incident in the life of Rāmapāla has been totally left out by his biographer, which is his enmity and wars with Devarakṣita of Pīṭhī. In the commentary of the 8th verse of the second chapter of his work Sandhyākaraṇandī hints that Mahaṇa, the maternal uncle of Rāmapāla, recovered the kingdom, as the Boar incarnation had recovered the earth in former days. There is no reference to the enmity which Devarakṣita, the Lord of Pīṭhī and of Sindhu, bore towards Rāmapāla, which has become known to us from the Sārnāth inscription of Kumāradevī discovered by Messrs. Marshall and Konow in 1906-7.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-021" href="#sec-07-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> It is stated there, that Mahaṇa, the King of Aṅga, the venerable maternal uncle of the Kings, conquered Devarakṣita in war, and maintained the glory of Rāmapāla, which rose in splendour, because the obstruction caused by his force was removed:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Taṁ jitvā yudhi Devarakṣitam-adhāt Śrī Rāmapālasya.</em><br>
<em>Yo lakṣmīṁ nirjita-vairi-rodhanatayā dedīpyamānodayām.</em><br>
verse 7.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-022" href="#sec-07-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Devarakṣita of Pīṭhī and Mathanadeva of Magadha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The defeat of Devarakṣita and Mahaṇa is also mentioned in the Rāmacarita, where it is said that Mathana or Mahaṇa defeated the King of Pīṭhī from the back of the elephant Vindhyamāṇikya.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-023" href="#sec-07-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The relationship between Mathanadeva and Rāmapāla has been explicitly mentioned in the commentary on verse 8, Chapter II of the Rāmacarita, so the references about Mathanadeva in the Sarnath inscription of Kumāradevī are quite clear. He is called the maternal uncle of the King because he was the maternal uncle of Rāmapāla, and perhaps also of Śūrapāla and Mahīpāla II also. Besides these, the sons of his other sisters might have been reigning in other parts of the country also. The mention of the defeat of Devarakṣita by Mathana or Mahaṇa is significant. The Sarnath inscription of Kumāradevī leaves no doubt about the fact that Mathana relieved Rāmapāla by defeating Devarakṣita. Evidently Devarakṣita of Pīṭhī had taken the part of one of Rāmapāla's rival claimants to the throne or invaded the Pāla dominions at a time when the Pāla kings were weakened by the defection of Northern Bengal, and so he expected to have an easy victory. The materials at our disposal are quite insufficient for the narration of details, but the Sarnath inscription of Kumāradevī proves, that though Mathana had humbled Devarakṣita at first, he had subsequently, owing to some unknown reason, given his daughter Śaṅkaradevī in marriage to him.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-024" href="#sec-07-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> The probable reason is that either Devarakṣita succeeded in defeating Mathana and a peace was concluded after the marriage, or that Śaṅkaradevī was given to Devarakṣita in order to draw him to the party of Mathana and Rāmapāla. Whatever may be the fact of the case, we are sure that</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-57"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="57" data-folio="87" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n56_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 87">[p. 87]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Devarakṣita did not continue to be the ruler of Pīṭhī for a long time, as we find another king in that country when Rāmapāla led his expedition into Northern Bengal. The relationship between the Pālas, the Gāhaḍavālas, the Rāṣṭrakūṭas of Magadha and the rulers of Pīṭhī are shown in the table on following page.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Pīṭhī has been identified by Dr. Sten Konow with the modern Piṭhapuram in the Madras Presidency.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-025" href="#sec-07-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> But this is perhaps wide of the mark. It is mentioned as a separate principality, the ruler of which makes war upon the Pāla Kings of Bengal, and later on during the war between the Pālas and the Kaivartta King of Bengal, another prince of Pīṭhī is mentioned as a feudatory or as an ally of the Pāla King.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Position of Pīṭhī.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It was hardly possible for the Pāla Kings after Nayapāla and Vigrahapāla III to wage war with the princes of Piṭhapuram or to demand an acknowledgment of suzerainty from them for any length of time. On the other hand, Pīṭhī should be somewhere near Magadha or a province with a boundary contiguous to the possession of the Pālas. A place named Pīṭhaghaṭṭā is mentioned in an ancient geographical work called Deśāvalī,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-026" href="#sec-07-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> a copy of which is in the manuscript collection of the Asiatic Society of Bengal. The addition of the word <em>ghaṭṭā</em> probably means that his place was situated on the Ganges. This Piṭha or Pīṭhī was most probably on the western or northern boundary of Magadha and is perhaps represented by the trans-Son districts or Tirhut in the modern days. Some coins bearing the name Paṭha (most probably Pīṭhī) are preserved in the Cabinet of the Indian Museum,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-027" href="#sec-07-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> but no records are available to prove their find-spots.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Campaign in Varendrī.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">allies and feudatories.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Allies and feudatories.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The great event of Rāmapāla's reign was his campaign in Northern Bengal, against the descendants of the rebel Divvoka, in which he was assisted and accompanied by a large number of A long list of these princes is given, at the beginning of the second Chapter of Sandhyākaranandī's Rāmacarita.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bhīmaysas of Pīṭhī and Magadha</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Unfortunately very few of the localities mentioned in this list can be identified at present. At the head of the list is the name of Bhīmayaśas, Prince of Pīṭhī and Magadha. This prince is apparently the sucessor of Devarakṣita, as in one of the following verses the commentary describes the defeat of Devarakṣita by Mahaṇa as an already accomplished fact.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-029" href="#sec-07-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> It may be that Devarakṣita had placed his son Bhīmayaśas on the throne after his defeat by Mahaṇa. The commentary distinctly states, that Bhīmayaśas was Lord of Pīṭhī and Magadha,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-028" href="#sec-07-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> but in the commentary on the Rāmacarita Mahaṇa is called Lord of Magadha, and Devarakṣita, King of Sindhu and Pīṭhī. Mahaṇa may have been divested of the possession of Magadha by Bhīmayaśas of Pīṭhī, after his defeat of Devarakṣita and the marriage of Mahaṇa's daughter with him. It also appears that though Devarakṣita was the Lord of Pīṭhī and Sindhu, his successor Bhīmayaśas was not. The position of Sindhu is doubtful. Bhīmayaśas is said to have</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-58"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="58" data-folio="88" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n57_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 88">[p. 88]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-006"><img src="/figures/palas-of-bengal/fig-006_p0058.webp" alt="Genealogical chart showing the synchronisms and matrimonial alliances of the Gāhaḍavalas of Kanauj, Cikkoras of Pīṭhī, Rāṣṭrakūṭas of Magadha (?), Pālas, Kalacuri-Cedis of Ḍāhala, and Yādvas of East Bengal." loading="lazy"><figcaption>Genealogical chart showing the synchronisms and matrimonial alliances of the Gāhaḍavalas of Kanauj, Cikkoras of Pīṭhī, Rāṣṭrakūṭas of Magadha (?), Pālas, Kalacuri-Cedis of Ḍāhala, and Yādvas of East Bengal.</figcaption></figure><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-59"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="59" data-folio="89" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n58_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 89">[p. 89]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vīraguṇa of the South.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">defeated the troops of a king of Kānyakubja, whose name has not been discovered as yet. The position of Pīṭhī is also indicated by this reference. It seems to have been a buffer state between those of Kānyakubja and Gauḍa. The Pratīhāra dynasty was falling, and the kingdom of the proud Gāhaḍavāla was rising on its ruins. It is quite possible that Bhīmayaśas of Pīṭhī assisted Candradeva, the Gāhaḍavāla, to obtain the city of Kānyakubja and to overthrow the last Gurjara-Pratīhāra King. The next prince in the order adopted by Sandhyākara-nandī is Vīraguṇa of the forest of Koṭā, who is also styled "the over-lord of the Southern thrones." But nothing is known about this king.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Jaya Siṁha of Daṇḍabhūkti.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vikrama-Keśarī of Devagrāma in Vāla-valabhī.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The position of Vāla-valabhī.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lakṣmīśūra of Mandāra.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Śūrapāla of Kujabaṭī.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rudraśikhara of Tailakampa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Dr. Kielhorn's lists of Northern and South Indian Inscriptions do not contain any record which mentions this king of the South. Jaya Siṁha, the Lord of Daṇḍabhukti, seems to have been a man of great importance. The position of Daṇḍabhūkti has already been indicated.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-030" href="#sec-07-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> It is represented at the present day by the District of Midnapur. So Jaya Siṁha was the march-lord of the South. The commentary very appropriately mentions the defeat of the King Karṇa-keśarī of Utkala by this prince. It is more natural for the King of Orissa to fight with a prince, whose land lay on his border, than with one, whose possessions were separated from his by a belt of mountains and forests. The position of Devagrāma in Vāla-valabhī, the king of which, Vikrama Keśarī, comes next in order, is far less certain. The commentary adds: "Devagrāma-prativaddha-vasudhā-cakravālavālavalabhī-taraṅga-vahala-galahasta-praśasta hastavikramo.''<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-031" href="#sec-07-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The explanation of this is not quite certain and nothing can be made out beyond what has already been stated by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Sāstrī. Vikrama Keśarī was the King of Devagrāma and the surrounding country which was washed by the rivers of Vāla-valabhī. Vāla-valabhī has been identified by Pandit Hara Prasāda Sāstrī with Bāgaḍī, one of the five divisions into which Bengal proper was divided before the Muhammadan conquest, but no reliable authority whatsoever can be cited in support of it. The name Vāla-valabhī itself was unknown in Bengal before the discovery of the Bhuvaneśvara prasasti of Bhavadevabhaṭṭa<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-032" href="#sec-07-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> and has not been found anywhere else except the Rāmacarita. There are hundreds of villages in Bengal bearing the name of Devagrāma, and I do not find any reason to confine it to one of them. Even in the Nadiāh district itself there are several Devagrāmas, and so the attempt to identify it with the materials at present at our command is premature. Lakṣmīśūra is said to be the Madhusūdana of another Mandāra and is described as the head of all Forest feudatories "<em>Samast-āṭavika-sāmanta-cakracuḍāmaṇiḥ</em>.''<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-033" href="#sec-07-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The Mandāra mentioned here seems to be the hill of that name at present in the Bhagalpur district of Bihar. Mandāra hill commands the surrounding hilly and forest country to a great distance, and it may be that its king <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-60" data-page="60" data-folio="90" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n59_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 90">[p. 90]</button> lorded over the Saontals of the Forest. The name of the next prince is suggestive. Śūrapāla is mentioned as being the chief of Kujabaṭī. The Tirumalai inscriptions mention a feudatory of Mahīpāla I, named Dharmmapāla, who ruled over Daṇḍabhūkti.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-034" href="#sec-07-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Perhaps these princes belonged to the minor branches of the Imperial Pāla Dynasty. Rudraśikhara of Tailakampa is mentioned as a great warrior. Perhaps Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī's identification<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-035" href="#sec-07-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> of Tailakampa with the modern Telkupi<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-036" href="#sec-07-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> in the Manbhum district is correct, but there is no proof in support of it beyond the resemblance in place names.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mayagala-siṁha of Ucchāla.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pratāpa-siṁha of Ḍekkariya.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mayagala-siṁha of Ucchāla was the king of a country which was partly surrounded by the sea. The commentary mentions "<em>Aparalohitārṇava</em>" which means another Red Sea, but it is quite possible that the poet intends to mention the river Brahmaputra which is also known as the <em>Lauhitya</em>. Pratāpa-siṁha of Ḍekkariya is also extolled in the commentary as a great warrior. There is nothing in the commentary to indicate the position of Ḍekkariya, but it has been identified by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstri with the modern village of Ḍhekura or Ḍhekuri in the northern part of the Burdwan District, on the ground of similarity of names.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-037" href="#sec-07-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The commentary on the next verse mentions five princes, and among the names of places over which they ruled only two can be identified :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(1) Narasiṁhārjjuna, the king of the Kayaṅgala <em>maṇḍala</em>;</li><li>(2) Candārjjuna of Śaṅkaṭagrāma;</li><li>(3) Vijayarāja of Nidrāvala;</li><li>(4) Dorapavarddhana of Kauśāmbī; and</li><li>(5) Soma of Paduvanvā.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Dorapavarddhana of Kauśāmbi seems to have been a landlord of Varendrī. Kauṣāmbī seems to be the ancient name of the modern Pargana of Kusumba in the Rajshahi District of Bengal. I am indebted to Prof. Jadunath Sarkar of the Patna College for this suggestion. Paduvanvā has been identified by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Sāstrī with the modern Pabna on the ground of similarity of names.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rāmapāla's son and cousins.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At the bottom of the list of feudatories we find mention of Rāmapāla's cousins on his mother's side, viz. the princes of the Rāṣṭrakūṭa family, and his eldest son Rājyapāla, who died in his lifetime. Rāmapāla's maternal relations are specified in the next verse, his eldest maternal uncle Mathanadeva, whom we have already met, his brother Suvarṇadeva and their sons, the Mahāmāṇḍalika Kāhṇuradeva, and the Mahāpratīhāra Śivarājadeva.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-038" href="#sec-07-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The campaign, a bridge of boats on the Ganges.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rāmapāla, with his allies and feudatories, crossed the Ganges either on boats or by a bridge of boats. The commentary on the next verse states that the great army <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-61" data-page="61" data-folio="91" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n60_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 91">[p. 91]</button> crossed the Ganges by a ‘Naukā-melaka’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-039" href="#sec-07-fn-039" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-039" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, which has been interpreted by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstri as ‘‘a bridge of boats.’’ The commentary on the work</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The battle. Capture of Bhīma on the back of an elephant.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">does not specify the place where the battle took place, but it is quite certain that the contending armies met somewhere in the south-western part of the modern District of Rājshāhī, or the southern part of the Maldah District. According to the commentary on verse 16, Bhīma was captured alive during the battle, and the soldiers of Rāmapāla received a fresh impetus from the news.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-040" href="#sec-07-fn-040" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-040" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The commentary on another verse</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The sack of Ḍamara, the enemy's capital.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">states that Bhīma was captured on the back of an elephant.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-041" href="#sec-07-fn-041" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-041" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Bhīma's army most probably dispersed on the capture of their leader, and Rāmapāla seems to have obtained an easy victory, which was followed by the sack of the town of Ḍamara, the capital of Bhīma.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-042" href="#sec-07-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The commentary on another verse states that Rāmapāla destroyed Ḍamara, a small town. The adjective <em>Upapura</em> is no doubt applied slightingly because it happened to be the capital of the enemy.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rebellion of Hari, defeat, capture and execution with Bhīma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Bhīma remained a captive and was placed in charge of a certain Vittapāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-043" href="#sec-07-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> The scattered forces of Bhīma were rallied by one of his friends named Hari. In the ensuing battle Rāmapāla's son contested every inch of ground and at last succeeded in defeating the <em>Kaivarttas</em>. Hari was, at last, deprived of his forces, captured and executed with Bhīma.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Foundation of a capital— Rāmāvatī.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Ḍamara seems to have continued its existence after its sack by Rāmapāla, and even to this day a village named Ḍamara-nagara exists close to Rāmapāla's capital. In another verse, Rāmapāla is said to have taken into employ the soldiers of Bhīma.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-044" href="#sec-07-fn-044" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-044" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> Rāmapāla founded a city named Rāmāvatī at the confluence of the Karatoyā and the Ganges.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-045" href="#sec-07-fn-045" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-045" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The site seems to have been selected for Rāmapāla by a chief named Caṇḍeśvara of Śri-hetu (not Śri-haṭṭa) and one Kṣemeśvara.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-046" href="#sec-07-fn-046" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-046" role="doc-noteref">8</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The city was beautified within a very short time, and the author has devoted the best part of a chapter to its praise. The only feature, worth mentioning, is a Buddhist Vihāra named Jagaddala-Mahāvihāra, which was built by Rāmapāla in the new city. It is interesting to note that there is a village named Jagaddala close to the ruins of Rāmāvatī. Rāmāvatī continued to be the capital of the Pālas for some time, and Madanapāla's Manahali grant was issued from this place.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-047" href="#sec-07-fn-047" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-047" role="doc-noteref">9</a></sup> It continued to be a place of importance for several centuries. In the sixteenth century it gave its name to a fiscal division, and one of the circles in the Sirkar of Lakhnauti was named Ramauti<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-048" href="#sec-07-fn-048" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-048" role="doc-noteref">10</a></sup> in Akbar's time. Ramauti is an exact transliteration of <em>Rāmāvatī</em> as Lakhnauti is of <em>Lakṣmaṇāvatī</em>, and the identity of Ramauti with Rāmāvatī has been made certain by the discoveries of Babu Haridās Pālit in the Maldah District. This gentleman has industriously searched the environments of Rāmāvatī and has traced <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-62" data-page="62" data-folio="92" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n61_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 92">[p. 92]</button> the following villages bearing ancient names: <em>Amrauti</em> or <em>Ramrauti</em> (<em>Rāmāvatī</em>), <em>Jagadalā</em> (<em>Jagaddala</em>), <em>Ḍāmrol</em> (<em>Ḍamara</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Later wars of Rāmapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After the foundation of Rāmāvatī, Rāmapāla engaged in wars with his neighbours. He attacked Utkala and ruled the country up to Kaliṅga,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-049" href="#sec-07-fn-049" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-049" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and returned the kingdom of Utkala to the Nāgavaṁśa. His feudatory chief Māyana conquered Kāmarūpa which seems to have been becoming weaker and weaker at this time, as several invasions into that country were led by successive kings of Bengal or their generals, e.g., Māyana sent by Rāmapāla, Vaidyadeva sent by Kumārapāla, Vijayasena and Lakṣmaṇasena. A king of Eastern Bengal sought the protection of Rāmapāla in order to save himself by surrendering to him his best elephants, his coach of state and his armour.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-050" href="#sec-07-fn-050" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-050" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Svaparitrāṇanimittaṁ patyā yaḥ prāg-diśīyena</em> .<br>
<em>Vara-vāraṇena ca nija-syandana-dānena varmmaṇārādhe</em> ‖.<br>
Rāmacarita III. 44.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This king seems to be one of the Yādavas of Eastern Bengal. Two different powers may have caused him to throw himself under the protection of Rāmapāla: first is an invasion by Pāla forces, and second an invasion of his territories by a new power. Sāmantasena was most probably getting very powerful at this time, and it was he who seems to have caused the Yādava prince to seek the shelter afforded by Rāmapāla.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Death of Mathanadeva and Rāmapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In his later years Rāmapāla returned to Rāmāvatī, leaving the cares of the management of the state to his eldest son Rājyapāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-051" href="#sec-07-fn-051" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-051" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> About this time Mathanadeva, the king's maternal uncle, died. The king was residing at Mudgiri (<em>Mudgagiri</em> or <em>Mungir</em>) at this time,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-052" href="#sec-07-fn-052" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-052" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> and on hearing of his benefactor's death distributed much wealth to the Brāhmaṇas and entered the sacred river Ganges. Mathanadeva must have become a centenarian at the time of his death, and Rāmapāla himself had become a very old man at the time of his death after forty-six years of reign.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Length of reign. Successors.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Ministers.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Tārānātha states that Rāmapāla reigned for forty-six years.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-053" href="#sec-07-fn-053" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-053" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> This is not impossible as the Caṇḍīmau image was dedicated in the 42nd year of the king. We know the names of three of the sons of Rāmapāla, two of whom succeeded him on the throne. His eldest son, Rājyapāla, was an able man, and assisted his father in gaining the throne, in the wars in Vārendrī, and finally in administering the kingdom in his father's old age. He seems to have died during the lifetime of his father, as we find that Kumārapāla succeeded after Rāmapāla's death. His third son, Madanapāla, ascended the throne after the death or dethronement of his brother Kumārapāla's son Gopāla III. We know nothing about the other relations of Rāmapāla except his <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-63" data-page="63" data-folio="93" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n62_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 93">[p. 93]</button> maternal uncles, Mathanadeva or Mahāṇadeva and Suvarṇadeva and their sons Kāhṇuradeva and Śivarājadeva. Sandhyākaranandi's father, Prajāpatinandi, was the principal minister of peace<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-054" href="#sec-07-fn-054" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-054" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and war (<em>Mahāsāndhivigrahika</em>), but his principal adviser was Bodhideva, son of Yogadeva, the minister of his father Vigrahapāla III.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-055" href="#sec-07-fn-055" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-055" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">King's reign. Inscriptions and MS. Records.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The earliest record of Rāmapāla is the Tetrawan inscription recording the erection of an image of Tārā by a certain Bhaṭṭa Īcchara, in the 2nd year of the King's reign. This image was discovered by the late Mr. A. M. Broadley, who read the king's name as Rāmapati.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-056" href="#sec-07-fn-056" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-056" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Cunningham published it in one of his reports.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-057" href="#sec-07-fn-057" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-057" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The inscription was finally published by Babu Nilmani Chackravartti with a good ink impression in 1908.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-058" href="#sec-07-fn-058" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-058" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> It consists of two lines partly damaged, and is at present in the Indian Museum.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The next record in order is a manuscript of the Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā written at Nālandā in the Magadha <em>viṣaya</em>. The manuscript was purchased by Dr. A. F R. Hoernle from Nepal, during his stay in India,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-059" href="#sec-07-fn-059" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-059" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> and afterwards acquired by the Bodleian Library.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-059-2" href="#sec-07-fn-059" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-059" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup> It was written in the 15th year of the king and its final colophon runs as follows:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>1.—ranuttara <em>jñānāvāptaya iti, Mahārājādhirāja Parameśvara-Paramabhaṭṭāraka- Paramasaugata Śrīmad=Rāmapāladeva-pravarddhamāna-vijayarājye pañcadaśame samvatsare abhilikhyamāne yatrāṅkenāpi samvat</em> 15, <em>Vaiśākṣadine kṛṣṇasaptamyām</em> 7 <em>Asti Magadhaviṣaye, Śrī Nālandāvasthita lekhaka Grahaṇakuṇḍena Bhaṭṭārikāpra-</em> 2. —<em>jñāpāramitā likhitā iti.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The date of the Chandimau Image.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Late in the king's reign an image of the Bodhisatva Padmapāṇi was dedicated, at or near the modern village of Chaṇḍimau in the Bihar Sub-division of the Patna District by an inhabitant of Rājagṛiha. This inscribed image was discovered by Cunningham in 1877 or 1878,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-061" href="#sec-07-fn-061" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-061" role="doc-noteref">8</a></sup> but he did not attempt to read it. No attempt has hitherto been made by anybody else to read this inscription. The image was found lying among the number of broken ones in the outskirt of the village of Chaṇḍimau in August 1911 and was removed to Indian Museum. A part of the inscription was broken owing to careless packing, but fortunately two inked impressions of the inscription were taken while <em>in situ</em>, otherwise it would have become quite impossible to read the date, as the part bearing the numerals for the year have disappeared. This is the most interesting part of the epigraph as it proves conclusively that Rāmapāladeva reigned for a considerable length of time, at least 42 years, which made Tārānātha's statement about his length of reign acceptable. Cunningham read the date as 12, but it certainly was 42. The inscription itself runs as follows :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>(1). <em>Ye dharmmā hetu prabhavā hetu (ṁ) teṣām hy = avadat (t) eṣām (ṁ) yo niroddho <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-64" data-page="64" data-folio="94" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n63_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 94">[p. 94]</button> evaṁ vādī mahāśramaṇaḥ</em> । Ś<em>rī-mad</em> = Rā<em>jagṛha vinirggataḥ Etrahāgrāmāvasthitaḥ</em> || <em>Paramopāsaka paramamahājen (ānuy) āvinaḥ</em> । <em>Vaṇika Sādhu</em>. (2) <em>Saharaṇasya Sādhu Bhādulvasutasya vadatra puṇyaḥ</em> || <em>Tad-bhavatv-ācaryopā- dhyāya-mātā-pita purvvaṅgama (ṁ) kṛtvā sakala (satva) rāser-ajñāna phalavāptaya iti</em> । । <em>Paramabhaṭṭāraka Parameśvara Paramas.u (?)</em> (3) ............... <em>ta</em> । <em>Mahārajadhirāja Śrī-mad</em> = <em>Rāmapāladevapāda pravarddhamāna-kalyāṇa-vijayarājye samvat</em> 42 <em>Āṣāḍha dine</em> 30.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The text of the Rāmacarita.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Commentary.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Author.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The date is given in the decimal notation so that there remains no doubt about its reading. The first numeral is certainly 4 and not 1. We find it in a contemporary inscription—the Bodh-Gaya inscription of the 74th year of the Lakṣmaṇasena era.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-062" href="#sec-07-fn-062" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-062" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The donor, Sādhu Saharaṇa, was most probably Vaiṣya by caste and a merchant by profession. Nothing was known about Rāmapāla and his times twenty years ago. When Mr. Venis was editing the Kamauli grant of Vaidyadeva, he was faced with great difficulties for want of materials.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-064" href="#sec-07-fn-064" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-064" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The date of Vaidyaveva's grant was fixed by him on conjecture. Recent discoveries have proved beyond doubt, that the grant must be placed half a century earlier. Rāmapāla's date was fixed and the events of his reign made known by the discovery of the Rāmacarita of Sandhyākaranandi.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-063" href="#sec-07-fn-063" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-063" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Nothing has been stated about, and the place of discovery of, this unique manuscript, by the discoverer himself, in the introduction to his edition of the Rāmacarita, but I have since its publication learnt on enquiry from him that the manuscript was purchased in Nepal in 1897 The manuscript itself consists of two different parts:—(1) The text, which is complete, and (2) the commentary, which is incomplete but older than the text. It runs up to the thirty-fifth verse of the second chapter of the text. The text of the work is written in Bengali characters of the 12th or 13th centuries on strips of palmleaf. It is, Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstri observes, written in imitation of the Rāghava-Pāṇḍavīya, in <em>double entendre</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-065" href="#sec-07-fn-065" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-065" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The difficulty of understanding such a work is apparent, and had it been discovered without its commentary, it would have been of no use to historians or antiquarians. The principal value of the discovery lies in the commentary. The commentary is a mine of historical information, and supplies the details of the events of Rāmapāla's reign. The style of the composition of the commentary is highly ornamental prose, which makes it very difficult for one to get at the truth. The text does not end after the death of Rāmapāla but continues to describe the events of the reigns of his successors, Kumārapala, Gopāla III, and Madanapāla. If the second part of the commentary is ever recovered, then an abundance of detail will be available, about the events of the time of the three princes mentioned above. There is very little doubt about the fact, that the author of the poem was obliged to write the commentary on it himself. The masses of details which are called up by the use of single works, would have <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-65" data-page="65" data-folio="95" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n64_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 95">[p. 95]</button> had no meaning to other persons. The author had great facilities for the collection of information as his father was Rāmapāla's Sāndhivigrahika. The comparison of Rāmapāla with Rāma, the hero of the Rāmāyaṇa, seems to have been habitual with the courtiers of the 11th century <span class="sc">A.D.</span> A verse of the Kamauli grant of Vaidyadeva mentions the conquest of Mithilā and a king named Bhīma, and at the same time compares Rāmapāla with Rama:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tena yena jagat=traye janaka-bhū-lābhād-yathāvad=yaśaḥ.</em><br>
<em>Kṣauṇī-nāyaka-Bhīma Rāvaṇa-vadhād-yuddhārṇṇav=ollamghanāt</em> ||.<br>

22. <em>Likhit-āsau Śrī Padmapāṇiṇā</em> ‖</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The characters of this inscription cannot be said to belong to the 12th century <span class="sc">a.d.</span>, as the form of the test letters are much earlier than those of <span class="sc">Rāmapāla</span>. Most probably Yakṣapāla assumed independence during the troublesome times of the reigns of Vigrahapāla III, Mahīpāla II and Śūrapāla II. Perhaps he was contemporary of Rāmapāla during the earlier part of the latter's reign. He is not mentioned in the Rāmacarita, and most probably he was subdued by Rāmapāla's maternal uncle, Mathanadeva, who is styled <em>Magadhādhipa</em> in the commentary. He might have continued to reign either as a subordinate prince, or as an independent one, because no evidence is forthcoming to prove that any of the Pāla Emperors after Rāmapāla held any part of Western Magadha or South-Western Behar. Rāmapāla's inscriptions have been discovered in Eastern Magadha, but no Pāla record after the time of Vigrahapāla III have been found in the Gayā or Shahabad District, except the two inscriptions of Govindapāladeva. Another powerful dynasty of kings, who founded their monarchy on the ruins of the second Pāla Empire, was that of the Varmans of Eastern Bengal.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Four records of this dynasty have come to light as yet :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(1) Bhuvaneśvara inscription of the time of Bhaṭṭa Bhavadeva.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-075" href="#sec-07-fn-075" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-075" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></li><li>(2) The unpublished copper-plate grant of Harivarmmadeva, which has been noticed by Babu Nagendra Natha Basu in <em>Vaṅgera Jātīya Itihāsa</em>, '' Vol. II, p. 215 and plate. This copperplate grant was seen and examined by the author several years ago. It was obtained from the late Mr. Hari Nath De, and was photographed with the permission of the owner. Very little can be made out of the grant at present.</li><li>(3) A manuscript of the “ <em>Aṣṭa-sāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā</em> ” written in the 19th year of Harivarmmadeva, recently acquired by me.</li><li>(4) The Belabo grant of Bhojavarman. According to the genealogy given in the inscription, the Varmans were descended from the race of Yadu. In that race were a lines of princes, who ruled at Siṁhapura, which was in the Punjab, as we know from the Lakkha-mandal-praśasti of the Princess Īśvarā. Vajravarman of that dynasty was the founder of a kingdom in Eastern Bengal. His son, Jātavar-man, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-68" data-page="68" data-folio="98" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n67_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 98">[p. 98]</button> was, as we have seen, the contemporary of Vigrahapāla III, who defeated the Cedi King Karṇṇa in Aṅga, and obtained the hand of his daughter Vīra-Śrī, conquered Kāmarūpa and Govarddhana, and acquired paramount power. His son was Sāmalavarman, about whom we do not know much. The accounts of Sāmalavarman as found in the genealogical works are wholly imaginary. He was succeeded by his son Bhojavarman. The characters of the new grant show that Harivarman and his father Jyotirvarman cannot either be placed before Vajravarman or taken to be his descendants, because most probably Sāmantasena made an end of the Yādava kingdom of Eastern Bengal shortly afterwards. So it appears probable that the two dynasties were to some extent contemporaneous.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The first inscription has indeed been published by the late Dr. Kielhorn, but no facsimile was published at that time. A complete analysis of the characters of these three records or an attempt to fix the date of Harivarman would be out of place here. I intend very shortly to publish another paper on the inscription and the chronology of the Varman kings. It might suffice here to say that the copperplate of Harivarmmadeva, though in a very bad state of preservation, gives us the name of the king and his father. The last line of the first side and the first line of the second contained the following sentences:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Mahārājādhirāja-Śrīmad-Jyotirvarmmadeva-pādānudhyāta Paramavaiṣṇava-Parameśvara-Paramabhaṭṭāraka-Mahārājādhirāja Śrīmad-Harivarmmadeva kuśalī.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The grant itself was issued from the victorious camp of Vikramapura, and from it we learn that part at least of Eastern Bengal belonged to Harivarmmadeva and that he was preceded by his father Jyotirvarmmadeva on the throne. The characters of the records of those dynasties show that Harivarman cannot be placed in the 12th century <span class="sc">a.d.</span> Consequently it must be admitted that his father Jyotirvarmman has to be placed in the earlier decades of the 11th century. The dynasty seems to have continued for three or four generations. We learn from Bhuvanēśvara inscription that Bhavadeva I received the village of Hastinībhiṭṭa from the King of Gauḍa. His son was Rathāṅga, whose son was Atyaṅga, and from him was descended Ādideva, who was the minister of peace and war (<em>Sāndhivigrahika</em>) of the king of Vaṅga. It is stated in verse 3 that the family settled in the village of Siddhala in Rāḍhā. Ādideva's son was Govarddhana who was renowned as a warrior and most probably served under Jyotirvarmmadeva. His son Bhavadeva II was the minister of Harivarmmadeva and of his son also.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The newly discovered Belabo plate records the grant of 9 <em>droṇas</em> of land in the village of Upyalikā, in the sub-division of Kauśāmvi-Aṣṭagaccha in the <em>Maṇḍala</em> or District of Adhahpattana, in the Division or <em>Bhukti</em> of Pauṇḍravarddhana, to a Brāhmaṇa of the Yajur-deva, named Rāmadevaśarman, son of Viśvarūpadevaśarman, grandson of Jagannāthadevaśarman and great-grandson of Pitāmbaradevaśarman, who was an inhabitant of the village of Siddhala, in Northern Rāḍhā, and had emigrated from Madhyadeśa or Kanauj.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The last line of the Bhuvaneswar inscription of Bhavadeva contained his surname—</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-69"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="69" data-folio="99" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n68_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 99">[p. 99]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Vālavalabhī-bhujanga</em>.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Vālavalabhī is mentioned as the name of a country in the Rāmacarita of Sandhyākaranandi. Vikramarāja of Devagrāma in Vālavalabhī had fought with Rāmapāla, in the war, in Varendra.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-076" href="#sec-07-fn-076" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-076" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Mahāmahopādhyāya Haraprasād Sastri had identified Vālavalabhī with <em>Bāgḍī</em>. He translates the passage of the commentary as follows :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“Vikramarāja, the Rājā of Devagrāma and the surrounding country, washed by the waves of the rivers of Vāla-Valabhī or Bāgḍī, one of the five provinces into which Bengal was divided.” The identification stands without any support. From the description given in the commentary on the Ramacarita it appears that Vālavalabhī was pre-eminently a land of rivers, and must be identified either with Eastern or Southern Bengal. The mention of Vikramapura in the copperplate grant of Harivarman does not help us in fixing the chronology of the Varmans. It may be that both dynasties occupied different parts of East Bengal at the same time and may have laid claim to the ownership of the city of Vikramapur. So far we have no positive evidence to prove that Jyotirvarman and Harivarman were descended from the Yādava Vajravarman, and we can only assume that they belong to co-lateral branches of the same family.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The invasion of the great southern conqueror Rājendra Coḷa I seems to have left some permanent marks in Bengal. We learn from the Sītāhāṭi grant of Vallālasena, that the ancestors of Sāmantasena, the grandfather of Vijayasena, lived in the country of Rāḍhā.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-077" href="#sec-07-fn-077" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-077" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> All Sena inscriptions agree in stating that the Sena kings were descended from a family of Karṇāṭa Kṣatriyas, i.e. from a family which originally came from the Kanarese-speaking districts of Southern India. Though the Cālukya King, Vikramāditya VI of Kalyāṇa, is said to have invaded Bengal during the lifetime of his father Somesvara I,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-078" href="#sec-07-fn-078" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-078" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> it cannot be said that the Cālukya Kings effected any permanent conquest in Eastern India. But, on the other hand, Vilhaṇadeva's remarks should be taken with great reservation, as none of the records of the Cedis of Tripurī or Ratnapura mention any Cālukya invasion of Northern India in the middle of the 11th century <span class="sc">a.d.</span> On the other hand, Rājendra Coḷa I defeated the Cālukya King, Jayasiṁha II, at Muyaṅgi or Musangi, and though Cālukyan poets state that the Cālukyas defeated the Coḷas, the definite terms of the Melpāḍi inscription leave no doubt about the fact that the defeat of the Cālukya Kings was decisive, and Rājendra Coḷa I obtained a large amount of treasure from him.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-079" href="#sec-07-fn-079" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-079" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Some obscure Karṇāṭa Chief seems to have followed Rājendra Coḷa I and settled in Western Bengal after the defeat of his Chief on the banks of the Ganges. From him was descended Sāmantasena, who is generally taken to be the founder of the Sena Dynasty. He seems to have succeeded in carving out a small principality for himself in Western Bengal. In the Deopara <em>praśasti</em> of his grandson, Vijayasena, it is stated that he, Sāmantasena, defeated his enemies after being surrounded by them.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-080" href="#sec-07-fn-080" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-080" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-70"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="70" data-folio="100" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n69_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 100">[p. 100]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">None of the Sena Princes are mentioned in the list of Rāmapāla's feudatories, and most probably their relations with the Imperial Pālas were not cordial. Sāmantasena, probably, came to power during the disturbances, in the earlier part of the reign of Vigrahapāla III. We know nothing about his son, Hemantasena, who was most probably a very tame vassal of the Emperor Rāmapāladeva.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Officers.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rāmapāla's minister was Bodhideva, the son of Yogadeva, who was the prime minister of his father Vigrahapāla III.
