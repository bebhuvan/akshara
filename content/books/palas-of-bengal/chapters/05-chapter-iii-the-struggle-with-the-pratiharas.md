<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-25"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="25" data-folio="55" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n24_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 55">[p. 55]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER III.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">THE STRUGGLE WITH THE PRATĪHĀRAS.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For a long time after the Northern Indian campaign of Govinda III, the Rāṣṭrakūṭa, Bengal enjoyed immunity from Gurjara invasions. The Rāṣṭrakūṭas had barred the Gurjaras so effectively in their desert country, that for the next two or three generations, the Gurjara kings were obliged to remain content with their former boundaries. It was not till the reign of the Gurjara Emperor Bhoja I, Mihira or Ādivarāha, that we hear of a Gurjara invasion of Bengal. After his succession to the throne, Devapāla was engaged in several lengthy campaigns, and pushed his conquests as far as the Himalayas in the North and the Vindhya Hills in the South :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Bhrāmyadbhir=vijaya-krameṇa karibhiḥ svām=eva Vindhy-āṭavīm=uddāma-plavamāna-vāṣpa-payaso dṛṣṭāḥ punar=bāndhavāḥ. Kambojeṣu cā yasya vājiyuvabhir=dhvast-ānyarāj-aujaso heṣā-miśrita hari-heṣita-ravāḥ kāntāś-ciram</em><br>
<em>vīkṣitāḥ.—ll.—19-20.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-001" href="#sec-05-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He met with considerable success in his wars, and we find a corroboration of this statement in an inscription incised at the request of the grandson of his minister, Darbhapāṇi Miśra. The Badal pillar inscription records that “ By his (Darbhapāṇi's) policy the illustrious prince Devapāla made tributary the earth as far as Revā's parent, whose pile of rocks are moist with the rutting juice of elephants, as far as Gauri's father, the mountain which is whitened by the rays of Īśvara's moon, and as far as the two oceans, whose waters are red with the rising and the setting of the sun ”:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Ā Revā-janakān=mataṅgaja-madastimyac-chila-saṅghater=ā-gaurī-pitur=īśvar</em><br>
<em>endu-kiraṇaiḥ puṣyat=sitimno gireḥ,</em><br>
<em>Mārttaṇḍās-tamay-oday-āruṇa-jalād-ā-vārirāśi-dvayān=nītyā yasya bhuvaṁ cakāra</em><br>
<em>karadāṁ Śrī-Devapālo nṛpaḥ.—verse 5.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-002" href="#sec-05-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the very same inscription another verse refers to the campaigns of the same king and mentions the names of his antagonists in detail. This verse has been assigned to Vigrahapāla I by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-003" href="#sec-05-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> but in my humble opinion it refers to the king Devapāla, for the simple reason that the verse referring to Śūrapāla, the next king after Devapāla, according to the Badal pillar inscription, is placed after it. According to this inscription both Darbhapāṇi and his grandson Kedāramiśra were the contemporaries of Devapāla. Someśvara, the son of Darbhapāṇi and the father of Kedāramiśra, was <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-26" data-page="26" data-folio="56" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n25_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 56">[p. 56]</button> most probably a general.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-004" href="#sec-05-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Kedāramiśra continued as minister under Śūrapāla I or Vigrahapāla I, and his son Guravamiśra was the minister of Nārāyaṇpāla. The second verse in the Badal pillar inscription about the campaigns of Devapāla runs as follows:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Utkīlit-otkala-kulaṁ hṛta-hūṇa-garvvaṁ kharvvīkṛta-draviḍa-gurjjaranātha-dar-ppaṁ,</em><br>
<em>Bhū-pīṭham=abdhi-raśan=ābharaṇamvubhoja Gauḍeśvaraś=ciram=upāsya dhiyaṁ yadīyām—verse</em> 13.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>"Attending to his (Kedāramiśra's) wise counsel the lord of Gauḍa long ruled the sea-girt earth, having eradicated the race of the Utkalas, humbled the pride of the Hūṇas, and scattered the conceit of the rulers of Draviḍa and Gurjjara."</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Devapāla, his wars.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The invasion of Utkala is a new point, but the fight with the Hūṇas perhaps is the same as that with the Kambojas referred to in the Monghyr grant. We know from an independent source that there was a war with the Drāviḍas, i.e. the Rāṣṭrakūṭas. In the Nilgund inscription Amoghavarṣa I, it is stated that he was "worshipped by the lords of Vaṅga, Aṅga, Magadha, Mālava and Veṅgī":—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Ari-nṛpati-makuṭa-ghaṭṭita-caraṇas=sakala-bhuvana-vandita-śauryyaḥ,</em><br>
<em>Vaṁg-āṁga-Magadha-Mālava-Veṁgīśair=arccito=tiśayadhavaḷaḥ.</em> 7-8.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-005" href="#sec-05-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Amoghavarṣa I seems to have been the contemporary of Devapāla as we know that his father Govinda III was of Dharmmapāla. The first three names: Vaṅga, Aṅga and Magadha, must refer to one and the same kingdom as we know from inscriptions that Vaṅga, Aṅga and Magadha were under Devapāla, viz. the Monghyr grant and the Ghosrawan inscription. The Rāṣṭrakūṭa invasion was most probably over within a very short time, like those under the predecessors of Amoghavarṣa I, and at its close, in spite of the reverses, Devapāla was left master of Northern India. The war with the Gurjaras was either followed by the invasion of Amoghavarṣa I, or itself followed that. In either case it is quite clear that the Gurjara king Rāmabhadra suffered this reverse at the hands of this king of Bengal, for neither in the Gurjara copper-plate grants nor in their stone inscriptions are any victories assigned to him.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Inscriptions and Mss. records.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the reign of Devapāla, a Brāhmaṇa named Vīradeva, an inhabitant of Nagarahāra, came on a pilgrimage to the Mahābodhi and paid a visit to the Yaśovarmmapura Vihāra. During his stay in Magadha, Devapāla heard of him and he was made the principal abbot of Nālandā.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-006" href="#sec-05-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The Monghyr grant was issued to record the grant of a village named Meṣika, in the Krimilā <em>Viṣaya</em>, and the Śrī-nagara <em>bhūkti</em>, to a brāhmaṇa named Vihekarāta, of the Aupamanyava gotra and the Āśvalāyana <em>Śākhā</em>, in the 33rd year of the king. The <em>dūtaka</em> of the grant was the king's son Rājyapāla. There is a vast mass of MSS. literature <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-27" data-page="27" data-folio="57" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n26_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 57">[p. 57]</button> in Bengal recording the descent of Brāhmaṇas. For the most part these MSS. are carelessly copied and hardly reliable, save for the names and descent of Brāhmaṇas. These records are said to contain historical allusions. Devapāla is mentioned in one of these genealogical works, the old Kārikā of Hari-Miśra-Ghaṭaka.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-007" href="#sec-05-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> But it is quite possible that the name is a modern interpolation, added to prove the authenticity of the work. The śloka itself as quoted by Babu Nagendra Nātha Vasu runs as follows :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Kṣmāpāla-pratibhūr-bhuvaḥ patir-abhūd Gauḍe ca rāṣṭre tataḥ</em>,<br>
<em>Rājā-bhūt pravaḷaḥ sadaiva śaraṇaḥ Śrī-Devapālas=tataḥ</em>.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His successor and relations. Length of reign.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vigrahapāla I or Śūrapāla I.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Prince Rājyapāla was made a Yuvarāja before the grant of the copper-plate in the 33rd regnal year, but he must have died during the lifetime of his father as we find that the king Devapāla was succeeded by Vigrahapāla I, the son of his cousin Jayapāla and the grandson of Vakpāla, the younger brother of Dharmmapāla. In the Badal pillar inscription, the next king after Devapāla is named Śūrapāla. But these two names belong to one and the same person as we shall have to see later on. In the Monghyr grant the date is given as the 33rd regnal year, but according to Tārānātha, Devapāla is said to have reigned forty-eight years. This is most probably incorrect, though we find that both Darbhapāṇi and his grandson Kedāramiśra were his ministers and contemporaries. Devapāla's successor was Vigrahapāla I or Śūrapāla I, whose father Jayapāla had led the expedition against the king of Utkala or Orissa at the request of his cousin and conquered Prāgjyotiṣa for him.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-008" href="#sec-05-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Vigrahapāla I is no doubt the same as the Śūrapāla mentioned in the Badal pillar inscription because it is the only name mentioned between Devapāla and Nārāyaṇapāla, and again in the Bhagalpur grant, Vigrahapāla's name is the only one mentioned between Devapāla and Nārāyaṇapāla. Moreover in the Manahali grant of Madanapāla we do not find the name of Śūrapāla before or close to the name of Nārāyaṇapāla. Had there been a different prince of the name of Śūrapāla, his name would surely have been mentioned in it as that inscription contains almost all the names of the Pāla dynasty. Only two small inscriptions of Śūrapāla I have been discovered as yet. Both of them are dated in the second year of this king and record the erection of images at the Vihāra in Uddaṇḍapura by an old Buddhist monk named Pūrṇadāsa. These two inscriptions have been assigned to Śūrapāla II on palæographical grounds by Prof. Nilmoni Chakravartti but that is hardly tenable, as inscriptions of Mahīpāla I and Rāmapāla are written in Proto-Bengali character and it is hardly possible that the inscriptions of Rāmapāla's brother should be written in the acute-angled form of Nagari characters. The name of the Vihāra was read by Prof. Chakravartti as Uddaṇḍacuṛa,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-009" href="#sec-05-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> but in reality it is Uddaṇḍapura.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-010" href="#sec-05-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Uddaṇḍapura is the ancient name of the modern town of Bihar. It <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-28" data-page="28" data-folio="58" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n27_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 58">[p. 58]</button> is mentioned as Adwand Bihar in the Ṭabaqāt-i-Nāṣirī of Minhāj-ud-dīn<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-011" href="#sec-05-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and as Uddaṇḍapura in another mediæval inscription in the town of the Gaya in Bengal.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-012" href="#sec-05-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The Tibetan historian Lama Tārānatha mentions it as Otantapura,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-013" href="#sec-05-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> which is the nearest approach to the Sanskrit Uddaṇḍapura. Śūrapāla I or Vigrahapāla I married Lajjādevi, the daughter of the Haihaya king of Tripurī.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-014" href="#sec-05-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Vigrahapāla's father, Jayapāla, was a Hindu by inclination, as after his father Vak-pāla's death he is said to have performed the funeral ceremony according to Hindu rites. Umāpati, a learned Brahmana of Kāñjivilvī, is said to have got the Mahādāna on that occasion. The fact is recorded in a commentary on the <em>Chandogapariśiṣṭa</em> named <em>Pariśiṣṭaprakāśa</em> by Umāpati's grandson, Nārāyaṇa :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Kṣmāpālāj=Jayapālataḥ sa hi Mahāśrāddhaṁ prabhutam.</em><br>
<em>Mahādānaṁ c-arthi-gaṇ=ārhaṇ=ārdra-hṛdayaḥ praty-agrahīt puṇyavān.—verse</em> 8.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-015" href="#sec-05-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The name of the village where Umāpati lived is given as Kāñjivindā in the 2nd verse, but it is clearly a mistake for Kanjivilva, the name of a well-known town. Nothing is known about the other relatives of this king, besides his son Nārāyaṇapāla who succeeded him. The votive inscriptions mentioned above were incised in the 2nd year of the king and most probably Vigrahapāla I or Śūrapāla I had a very short reign.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bhoja I and his war with the Palas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Nārāyaṇapāla succeeded to the throne in very troublesome times. The Gurjaras, after their long confinement in the desert, were issuing again for the conquest of Northern India, and this time they were destined to succeed and to make Mahodaya or Kānyakubja their capital. Bhoja I succeeded his father Rāmabhadra and at the beginning his kingdom seems to have consisted of the ancestral lands of the Gurjara-Pratihāras. Step by step Bhoja advanced towards the North. Kanauj or Mahodaya became his capital as several of his grants were issued from that place. It is not known from whom the Gurjara king wrested Kanauj, and it may be that it was taken either from the Pālas or one of their contemporaries. Nothing is known about the state of Uttarāpatha or Northern India about this time, but it is certain that the Pālas lost much of their territorial possessions during this period. Bhoja I invaded Bengal and defeated the king disastrously. The war with Bengal is mentioned in his Gwalior inscription :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Yasya vairi bṛhad=baṅgān=dahataḥ kopa-vahṇinā.</em><br>
<em>Pratāpād=arṇṇasāṃ rāśīn=pātur=vvaitṛṣṇam=āvabhau.—verse</em> 21.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-016" href="#sec-05-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This invasion must have taken place late in the reign of Bhoja I, as it must have taken him some time to be seated on the throne, advance towards Kanauj, conquer it and then invade Magadha and Vaṅga. The invasion is recorded in another Pratihāra inscription found at Māndor in Jodhpur. Kakkuka, whose brother Bauka's <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-29" data-page="29" data-folio="59" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n28_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 59">[p. 59]</button> inscription is dated Vikrama Saṁvat 918=861 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, states that his father Kakka gained fame in a fight with the Gauḍas at Mudgagiri :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tato'pi Śrīyutaḥ Kakkaḥ puttro jāto mahāmatiḥ.</em><br>
<em>Yaśo Mudgagirau labdhaṁ, yena Gauḍaiḥ samaṁ raṇe.</em>—verse 24.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-017" href="#sec-05-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Kakka seems to have accompanied Bhoja in his expedition against Bengal. As his son Bauka was alive in 861 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-018" href="#sec-05-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Bhoja I and Kakka must have invaded Bengal a few years earlier, and this invasion must have taken place during the earlier years of Nārāyaṇapāla.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The statements of the Gwalior inscription of Bhoja I and the Mandor inscription of the Pratīhāra Kakkuka lead one to believe that there was a great war between the first Pratīhāra Emperor Bhoja I and the Pāla Emperor Vigrahapāla I or Nārāyaṇapāla of Gauḍa and Vaṅga. This fact coupled with the discovery at least of three inscriptions mentioning the reign of the Emperor Mahendrapāla, the son of Bhoja I, in Magadha of Southern Bihar and one copper-plate in Tirhut, proves that the Province of Magadha was for a time added to the vast Empire of the Pratīhāras, either during the war of Bhoja I or after it.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We have positive evidence of the fact that the city of Gayā was in the possession of Nārāyaṇapāla up to the seventh year of his reign, because in that year a man named Bhāṇḍadeva erected a monastery for ascetics in that city. Up to the seventeenth year of Nārāyaṇapāla, Mudgagiri was in his possession as his grant was issued from that place in that year. From this grant we learn that at least a part of Tīrabhūkti or Mithilā continued to be in the possession of Nārāyaṇapāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-019" href="#sec-05-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The Pratīhāra Kakka most probably gained renown during the siege of the famous fort of Mudgagiri or Mungir.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Invasion of Amoghavarṣa I.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It appears that during the long reigns of Amoghavarṣa I and Bhoja I,—and they were to some extent contemporaries,—the Gurjaras had not come into collision with the Rāṣṭrakūṭas. In the Sirur and Nilgund inscriptions of Amoghavarṣa I, that monarch claims to have been worshipped by the kings of Vaṅga, Aṅga, Magadha, Mālava and Veṅgī :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Ari-nṛpati-makuṭa-ghaṭṭita-caraṇas=sakala bhuvana bandita śauryyaḥ.</em><br>
<em>Vaṅg-Āṅga-Magadha-Mālava-Veṁgīśair=arccito=tiśayadhavalaḥ.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-020" href="#sec-05-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup><br>
-<em>verse 6 Nilgund inscription and verse 5 Sirur inscription.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-021" href="#sec-05-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The kings of Vaṅga, Aṅga and Magadha were most probably one and the same person, one of the Pālas, either Vigrahapāla I or Nārāyaṇapāla. Amoghavarṣa I must have invaded Magadha and Vaṅga through Orissa, or otherwise he must have come into conflict with the Gurjaras who were then occupying most of Northern India, but of this no record has been discovered up to date.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But as we have seen above, the Gurjaras succeeded in annexing Magadha and most probably Tīrabhūkti or Tirhut permanently to their dominions and succeeded in keeping them till the rise of the Cedis under Karṇṇadeva, when Mahīpāla I <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-30" data-page="30" data-folio="60" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n29_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 60">[p. 60]</button> annexed Magadha to his territories. The Rāṣṭrakūṭa invasion was not a lasting one, like the previous ones, and, at the close of the war, the Gurjjara-Pratīhāras reoccupied Magadha.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Inscriptions of Nārāyaṇapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">According to the Bhagalpur grant, up to the seventeenth year of the king Mudgagiri was in his occupation. After that nothing is known about him. He was succeeded by his son Rājyapāladeva. The names of the other relations of the king are not known. The earliest record of Nārāyaṇapāla is the Gayā inscription of the seventh year. This is at present in the courtyard of the Viṣṇupāda temple in Gaya</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">City. Its discovery was announced by the late Sir Alexander Cunningham in his reports, with a drawing.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-022" href="#sec-05-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> As this inscription has never before been properly edited, I edit it from the original :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">1. <em>Om namo Puruṣottamāya namaḥ</em> || <em>Oṁ jayati jagati nāthaḥ prasphurac=cārumūrttir</em><br>

These Mongolians are named Kāmbojas in a Sanskrit inscription. In the Mungir grant of Devapāla and the Badal pillar inscription, Devapāla is said to have fought the Kāmbojas, but this may refer to the Western Kāmbojas. For a long time scholars have been at a loss to assign a place to a king named Mahendrapāla, several of whose inscriptions have been found in the Gayā District. All along he has been considered to be one of the Pālas of Bengal. The late Dr. F. Kielhorn also thought that he was one of the Pālas, and mentions him in a footnote in his list of the Pāla kings of Bengal.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-025" href="#sec-05-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Mr. V. Major Kittoe spoke of a third inscription of this king, but of that we shall have to speak later on. Acute-angled characters of the ninth century <span class="sc">a.d.</span> had been used in it, and on no account can it be placed later than the tenth century. One of these records the erection, most probably, of an image by a Buddhist monk named Kusuma in the ninth year of Mahendrapāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-030" href="#sec-05-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The nature of the contents of the other inscription is not known, but it is dated in the second year of Mahendrapāladeva. As for the reading of the date, there need not be any difficulty about that, as Kittoe’s readings are invariably faulty. So we have definite proof that in the eight and ninth years of the king Mahendrapāla, Magadha formed an integral part of the Gurjara-Pratīhāra Empire, which at that time extended from the Arabian Sea to the Bay of Bengal.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-031" href="#sec-05-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-35"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="35" data-folio="65" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n34_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 65">[p. 65]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No inscriptions of Rājyapāla have been discovered as yet, and so nothing definite can be stated about the length or the events of his reign. The first of these inscriptions was discovered by the late Sir Alexander Cunningham, at Bargaon in the Patna District, the ancient Nālandā.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-035" href="#sec-05-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> It records the fact that an image of the goddess Vāgīśvarī, at Nālandā, was covered with gold leaf by some unnamed personage in the first year of Gopāladeva.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-033" href="#sec-05-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The second inscription was discovered amidst the ruins of the Mahābodhi temple at Bodh-Gayā and records the erection of image of Buddha by a person named Śakrasena during the reign of Gopāladeva, no year being mentioned. These inscriptions prove that some time during the reign of Gopāla II, South Bihār or Magadha was temporarily occupied by the Pālas. The reason of this sudden enterprise is not far to seek. During the long reign of Amoghavarṣa I, whose certain dates extend from 817 to 877 A.D., there was no war between the Gurjaras and the Rāṣṭrakūṭas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-036" href="#sec-05-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> In fact the only war between Bhoja I and the Rāṣṭrakūṭas was his war with the Rāṣṭrakūtas Dhruvarāja II of Gujarat some time before 867 A.D., in which Bhoja I himself was worsted.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-034" href="#sec-05-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> After Bhoja I, Mahendrapāla succeeded to an empire which had reached its greatest extent at that time, from Punjab to the borders of Bengal, and from the foot of the Himalayas to Saurāṣṭra.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-039" href="#sec-05-fn-039" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-039" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> Mahendrapāla's reign was a very short one, as his certain dates range from 893 to 907 A.D. He had two wives and was succeeded by Bhoja II, his son by Dehanāgā.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-037" href="#sec-05-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup> Most probably there was some dispute about his succession, which may have been contested by his half-brother Mahīpāla II. Bhoja II was assisted to the throne by the Cedi Emperor Kokkalla I, which is referred to in the Bilhari inscription:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Jitvā kṛtsnāṁ yena pṛthvīm = apūrvvaṅ-kīrtti-stambha-dvandvam = āropyate sma,</em><br>
<em>Kaumbhod-bhavyāndiśyasau Kṛṣṇarājaḥ Kaurveyāñ = ca Śrī-nidhir-Bhojadevaḥ—</em><br>
<em>verse 17.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-040" href="#sec-05-fn-040" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-040" role="doc-noteref">8</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We find a corroboration of this statement in the Benares grant of the Cedi Emperor Karṇadeva:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Bhoje Vallabharāje Chitrakūṭa-bhūpāle,</em><br>
<em>Śaṅkaragaṇe ca rājani yasy = āsīd = abhayadaḥ pāṇiḥ—verse 7.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-038" href="#sec-05-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">9</a></sup></div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-36"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="36" data-folio="66" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n35_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 66">[p. 66]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">So the Cedi Emperor also set up the Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Kṛṣṇa II, whose surname was Vallabharāja, on the throne of his father Amoghavarṣa I. Kṛṣṇa II defeated the Gurjaras and at the same time led an invasion into Bengal:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tasy=ottarjjiṭa-Gurjaro hṛta-haṭa-llāṭ-odbhaṭa-śrīmado</em><br>
<em>Gauḍānāṁ vinaya-vratārppaṇa-gurus=Sāmudrā=nidrāharaḥ,</em><br>
<em>Dvārasth=Āṁga-Kaliṁga-Gāṁga-Magadhair=abhyarccit=ājñaś=ciraṁ sūnus=</em><br>
<em>sūnṛtavāg=bhuvaḥ parivṛtaḥ Śrī Kṛṣṇarājo-bhavat.</em><br>
-verse 13, Deoli plates of Kṛṣṇa III, and verse 15, Karhad plates of the<br>
same.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-041" href="#sec-05-fn-041" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-041" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Gurjara king defeated by Kṛṣṇarāja II seems to be Bhoja II. He was succeeded very shortly by his half-brother Mahīpāla, under whom the area of the Gurjāra-Pratīhāra Empire became very circumscribed. The Rāṣṭrakūṭa king Kṛṣṇa II also had a very short reign and was succeeded by his grandson Indra III. His certain years range from 902 to 911, and as those of Bhoja II are almost the same, it is almost certain that he was the king who was defeated by Kṛṣṇa II. After the accession of Mahīpāla, whose certain dates range from 914 to 917, Indra III invaded the Gurjara Empire, crossed the Yamunā, occupied Kānyakubja, and most probably destroyed the city.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-042" href="#sec-05-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Mr. D. R. Bhandarkar has already proved that at that time Kṣitipāla or Mahīpāla was the reigning sovereign at Kanauj. In this campaign, Narasiṁha, a feudatory of Indra III, pursued the Gurjara king Mahīpāla as far as the confluence of the Ganges. Narasiṁha, according to the Karnnāṭaka-Śabdānuśāsana by Bhaṭṭa Kalaṅkadeva, "snatched from the Gurjara king's arms the goddess of victory, whom, though desirous of keeping, he had held too loosely. Mahīpāla fled as if struck by thunder-bolts, staying neither to eat nor rest, nor pick himself up, while Narasiṁha pursuing, bathed his horse at the junction of the Ganges and established his fame.''<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-043" href="#sec-05-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The mention of the confluence of the Ganges as the extremity of Narasiṁha's pursuit of Mahīpāla, without any mention of the Gauḍa king, most probably indicates that the Eastern frontier of the Gurjara-Pratīhāra Empire at that time extended up to the junction of the Ganges with the Sea. This is not to be wondered at as it is now certain that Magadha formed an integral part of the dominions of Mahīpāla's father Mahendrapāla. During this war Gopāla II of Bengal may have taken the opportunity of recovering some of the traditional possessions of his family and pushed the Western frontier as far as the eastern banks of the Sone This re-occupation of the Magadha may have been temporary, and Mahīpāla may have recovered the possession of his Eastern Provinces, with the help of the Candella Yaśovarmman<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-044" href="#sec-05-fn-044" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-044" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> As no inscriptions of Gopāla's successor Vigrahapala II have been discovered, we are not in a position to say definitely whether Magadha continued to be a province of the Pāla Empire or was re-annexed by the Gurjaras. A MS. of the Pañcarakṣā written in the 26th year of Vigrahapāladeva II is preserved in the British Museum collection: the latter part of its colophon runs thus:—</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-37"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="37" data-folio="67" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n36_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 67">[p. 67]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Parameśvara-Paramabhaṭṭāraka-Paramasaugata Mahārājādhirāja-Śrīmad-Vigraha- pāladevasya pravardhamāna-vijayarājye-[about 15 indistinct akṣaras] Samvat 26 Āṣāḍha dina 24</em>॥<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-045" href="#sec-05-fn-045" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-045" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Before closing this chapter it should be noted that about this time an independent kingdom was established in Eastern Bengal. The existence of this kingdom was made known by the discovery of two copper-plate grants of Devakhaḍga, the last king of this dynasty. From these copper-plate grants it is now known that the dynasty reigned for three generations :—</p></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-004"><img src="/figures/palas-of-bengal/fig-004_p0037.webp" alt="Genealogical chart showing succession from Khaḍgodyama to Jātakhadga to Devakhaḍga" loading="lazy"></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Nothing is known about their dates save and except that the grants were issued in the thirteenth year of Devakhaḍga.
