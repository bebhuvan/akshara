Kumārapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rāmapāladeva was succeeded by his second son Kumārapāla about the year 1097 A.D. Immediately after Kumārapāla's accession, rebellions broke out throughout the kingdom. In Assam, which had been conquered for Rāmapāla by the feudatory chief Māyana, Tingyadeva raised the standard of rebellion. Southern Bengal and Western Bengal were overrun by the King of Orissa, Anantavarmman Coḍagaṅga. Sandhyākaranandi dismisses Kumārapāla with a single verse:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Atha rakṣatā (?) Kumārodita pṛthu-paripanthi-pārthiva-pramadaḥ</em> । <em>Rājyam-upa-</em><br>
<em>bhujya bharasya sūnur-agamad=divaṁ tanu-tyāgāt</em>. ‖—v. 11.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-001" href="#sec-08-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This most probably indicates that Kumārapāla reigned for a very short time. But during this short reign, he succeeded in obtaining decisive victories in Assam and in Southern Bengal with the aid of his Minister Vaidyadeva, who was the son of Yogadeva, the Prime Minister of Rāmapāla. We learn from the Kamauli Grant of Vaidyadeva that Kumārapāla having learnt of the disaffection of the feudatory chief of Kāmārūpa, named Tiṅgyadeva, deputed Vaidyadeva to overcome him. Vaidyadeva having received a promise of obtaining the kingdom after its conquest, reached Assam by forced marches and defeated Tiṅgyadeva :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Etādṛśe hari-harid-bhuvi satkṛtasya Śrī-Tiṅgyadeva nṛpater-vvikṛtiṁ niśamya</em><br>
<em>Gauḍeśvareṇa bhuvi tasya nareśvaratve Śrī-Vaidyadeva urukīrttir-iyāṁ niyuk-</em><br>
<em>taḥ</em>. ‖—v. 13-14.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-003" href="#sec-08-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">About this time Anantavarmman Coḍagaṅga invaded Western Bengal and overran the country up to the banks of the Ganges:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Gṛhṇāti-sma karaṁ bhūmer-gaṅgā-gotama-gaṅgayoḥ</em> । <em>Madhye paśyatsu vīreṣu</em><br>
<em>prauḍhaḥ prauḍha-striyā iva</em>. v. 22.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-002" href="#sec-08-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">War with the King of Orissa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rāmapāladeva conquered Utkala and Kaliṅga during the reign of either Rāja-Rāja I or his son Anantavarmman. It appears that Anantavarmman invaded the territories of the Pāla Kings immediately after the death of Rāmapāla. It may be mentioned in this connection that Vaidyadeva is said to have obtained a naval victory in Southern Bengal, and it is quite possible that this victory was obtained over the naval forces of Anantavarmman :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Yasy-ānuttara-vaṅga-saṅgara-jaye nauvāta-hīhīrava-trastair-ddik-karibhiś-ca yan-na</em><br>
<em>calitaṁ cen-nāsti tad-gamya-bhūḥ Kiñ-c-otpātukake-nipāta-patana-protsarpitaiḥ</em><br>
<em>śīkarair-ākāśe sthiratā kṛtā yadi bhavet-syān-niṣkalaṅkaḥ śaśī</em>. v. 11.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-004" href="#sec-08-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-72"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="72" data-folio="102" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n71_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 102">[p. 102]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Nothing is known about the extent of Kumārapāla's reign or the date of his death. But he does not appear to have reigned more than two or three years. He was succeeded by his infant son Gopāladeva (Gopāla III) III :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Pratyarthi-pramadā-kadamvaka-śiraḥ-sindūra-lopakrama-krīḍā-paṭala-pāṇir-eṣa su-</em><br>
<em>ṣuve Gopālam-ūrvvi-bhujaṁ | Dhātrī-pālana-jṛmbhamāna-mahimā-karpūra-pāṁ-</em><br>
<em>ś-ūtkarair-devaḥ kīrttim-a-yonijaṁ vitanute yaḥ śaiśave krīḍitam.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-005" href="#sec-08-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> v. 17.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The infant king seems to have been murdered very soon after his accession :— <em>Api śatrughnopāyād-gopālaḥ svar-jagāma tatsūnuḥ | Hantu (ḥ) kumbhīnasyās-tana- yasy-aitasya sāmayikam-etat.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-006" href="#sec-08-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> v. 12.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A posthumous record of this king has been discovered by Babu Akṣaya Kumāra Maitreya at Manda in the District of Rajshahi and presented to the Indian Museum. The palaeography points to the later part of the 11th and the earlier part of the 12th century as its date. The record is full of mistakes and is untranslatable :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">1. <em>Oṁ sura-sarid-uru-vīcīḥ sīkarau kunda-gauraur-vviracita parabhāgo vāla ca-</em><br>
2. <em>ndr-āvataṁsaḥ diśatu sivamajansraṁ sambhu-koṭīra-bhāra kalama-kaṇisa roci-</em><br>
3. <em>rmmaṁjarī piṁjarīsu || Śrī-mad-Gopāladevas-tridīva muṣarātaḥ svepva-</em><br>
4. <em>-yā tyakta kāsas-tasy-āhaṁ pāda-dhūli-prathita iti nijaṁ nāḥ. Vuddhām-asthīta-</em><br>
<em>pre-</em><br>
5. <em>-trajñā-pratijño nisita-sarasavai Purasenasakṛsāṣṭau nisyajā-dallirā</em><br>
6. <em>jā tṛdaśapuram-agād-Aiḍadeva kṛtajñaḥ || Svataṁ tvato vadhū ya saṁgarāt</em><br>
<em>prāpya</em><br>
7. <em>Candra-kiraṇ-āmalaṁ yasaḥ krīḍati tṛdaśasundarī Dṛso deva-eva Śūbhadeva</em><br>
<em>nanda-</em><br>
8. <em>-naḥ || Artha tadanuga-gīta-vilāsaḥ dharmmadhvara-masthara-galavāsaḥ Dāma-</em><br>
<em>śūra sasa-</em><br>
9. <em>-maṁ vāhitaveśaḥ sa yayate Śrī-sāmbhāvakadāsaḥ dagdhā yatra madadbhūtāḥ</em><br>
<em>śara-śa-</em><br>
10. <em>-ndhāna-pūritā yatra Bhāvakadāsena Kṛtā kīrṇṇā virājateḥ || Rātokeṇa le-</em><br>
11. <em>khitavya.</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We can recognize only a number of names :— (1) Gopāladeva, (2) Dāmaśūra, (3) Aiḍadeva, (4) Subhadeva, (5) Purasena, (6) <em>Sāmbhāvakadāsa</em>, (7) <em>Bhāvakadāsa</em> and (8) <em>Rātoka</em>, the scribe.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His murder by Uadana-pāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The murder of the infant king and the subsequent accession of his uncle Madanapāla seems to point to a parallel of the murder of the infant King Edward V by his uncle Richard III. About this time some dispute seems to have arisen about the succession, and ultimately Madanapāla's party seems to have triumphed. Vaidyadeva ignores Madanapāla completely in the Kamauli copper-plate grant issued in the fourth year of his reign, and so it must be admitted that Vaidyadeva declared his independence after the murder of Gopāla III. Till the reign of Madanapāla the Pāla Kingdom consisted of Eastern Magadha and Northern Bengal.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-73"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="73" data-folio="103" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n72_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 103">[p. 103]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Southern Varendra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Taking advantage of the internal dissensions in the Pāla Kingdom, Vijayasena seems to have strengthened himself in Eastern and Western Bengal, and when the weakness of the Pālas under Madanapāla became apparent, he invaded Northern Bengal and succeeded in wresting the southern part of Varendra. Madanapāladeva seems to have continued to hold the northern part of Varendra, as his Manahali Grant was issued in the 8th year of his reign from the royal city of Rāmāvatī.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-007" href="#sec-08-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We learn from the Deopārā inscription that Vijayasena attacked the King of Gauḍa with great force:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tvaṁ Nānya-Vīra-vijay-īti giraḥ kavīnāṁ śrutvās-nyathā-manana-rūḍha-nigūḍha</em><br>
<em>roṣaḥ | Gauḍendram-adravad-apākṛta Kāmarūpabhūpaṁ Kaliṅgam-api yas-tarasā</em><br>
<em>jigāya.</em> v. 20.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-008" href="#sec-08-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Northern Varendra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Most probably Madanapāla is the Gauḍendra mentioned in the verse quoted above. It is stated in the Deopārā inscription that Vijayasena defeated and imprisoned the King of Mithilā named Nānyadeva, so most probably Vijayasena conquered the remaining portion of Varendra before he turned his attention towards the neighbouring district of Mithilā.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vijayasena invades Magadha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Madanapāla's dominions were confined to the limits of Magadha after this. Vijayasena did not remain content with Varendra. He despatched a flotilla of armed boats for the conquest of the Western regions.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Pāścātya-cakra-jayakeliṣu yasya yāvad-Gaṁgā-pravāham-anudhāvati nauvitāne</em><br>
<em>Bharggasya mauli-sarid-ambhasi bhasma-paṅka-lagn-ojjhit-eva tarir-indu kalā</em><br>
<em>cakāsti.</em> v. 22.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-009" href="#sec-08-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But the expedition does not seem to have been very successful, as otherwise the incident would surely have been mentioned in Sena inscriptions. Madanapāladeva was greatly assisted in his war against Vijayasena by Candradeva, the founder of the Gahadavālā Dynasty of Kanauj. The author of the Rāmacarita has gratefully acknowledged this in his work :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Siṁhī-suta-vikrānten-arjjuna-dhāmnā bhuvaḥ pradīpena</em> | <em>Kamalā-vikāśa-veṣaja</em><br>
<em>viṣajā Candreṇa vandhunopetām</em> || 20. <em>Caṇḍī-caraṇa-saroja-prasāda-sampanna-</em><br>
<em>vigraha-Śrīkaṁ | Na khalu Madanaṁ s-āṅgeśam-īśāṁ agād jagad-vijayalakṣmīḥ</em><br>
v. 21.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-010" href="#sec-08-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Madanapāla seems to have been defeated by Vijayasena some time after the year 1108 A.D., which is the probable date of his Manahali inscription, when Candradeva was dead. Candradeva must have died before 1104 A.D., as in that year the Basāhi Plates of his grandson Govinda-Candra were issued, and that prince is styled "Mahārājaputra", so it must be admitted that Candradeva had ceased to reign at that time, and his son Madanpāla sat on the throne.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-011" href="#sec-08-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-74"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="74" data-folio="104" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n73_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 104">[p. 104]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Nothing is known about Madanapāla save that he continued to reign for about eleven years longer. Nothing is known about his sons or successors, their extent of reigns or dominion. About fifty years after Madanapāla's death, another prince of the Pāla Dynasty seems to have reigned in Magadha, but about this we shall have to speak later on.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Inscriptions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Four inscriptions of Madanapāla have been discovered up to date, of which three are votive inscriptions, incised on the pedestals of images, while the remaining one is on a copper-plate. The earliest inscription is the "Manahali" Grant, which records the grant of a village, perhaps named Kāṣṭhagiri, in the <em>Koṭīvarṣa-Viṣaya</em> of the <em>Pauṇḍravardhana Bhukti</em>, to a Brāhmaṇa named Vaṭeśvarasvāmi-śarmman, an inhabitant of Campāhiṭṭī, as dakṣiṇā for having read the Mahābhārata to the great queen (<em>Paṭṭa-Mahādevī</em>) Citramatikā, on the 15th day of the month of Caitra, in the 8th year of the King's reign. The order confirming the grant was issued from the city of Rāmāvatī, which had been founded by Rāmapāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-012" href="#sec-08-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> An image of <em>Ṣaṣṭhī</em> dedicated in the same year was discovered by Cunningham on Bihar Hill, but it cannot be traced at present.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-013" href="#sec-08-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Another image dedicated on the 30th Āśvina of the 19th year of the King was also discovered by Cunningham at Jayanagar, near Lakhisarai, in the Monghyr District ;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-014" href="#sec-08-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> but this image also is missing at present.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vijayasena.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After conquering Varendra, Vijayasena founded a new capital on the northern bank of the Ganges and named it after himself. The new capital was situated close to Rāmāvatī and its ruins have recently been discovered by the Varendra Research Society.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-015" href="#sec-08-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> He built a new temple of Siva named "Pradyumneśvara" on the bank of a large tank, the site of which also has been identified by the Varendra Archæological Society. This temple was situated in the village of Devapārā or Deopārā, which is about six miles distant from Vijaypur Milik, the site of Vijayapur, the capital founded by Vijayasena. Vijaypur Milik itself is situated on the banks of the river Ganges, about ten miles due east from the town of Rampur-Boalia.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His conquest.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">His Kingdom.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Length of reign: Successors and relations: Inscriptions.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Inscription.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After defeating the King of Gauḍa, who has been identified with Madanapāla, Vijayasena attacked Mithilā and conquered several Kings, viz., <em>Rāghava</em>, <em>Vardhana</em> and <em>Vīra</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-016" href="#sec-08-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> He led an expedition to Kāmarūpa and most probably succeeded in overthrowing Vaidyadeva or his successor.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-017" href="#sec-08-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> We learn from the Deopara Inscription that he defeated the King of Kaliṅga. Most probably Anantavarmman <em>Coḍagaṅga</em> led another raid into Western Bengal, but was repulsed by Vijayasena. Vijayasena's dominions comprised of Eastern Bengal, Western Bengal and Northern Bengal. An unpublished copper-plate grant of this King was issued from the victorious camp at Vikramapura, and so it must be admitted that Eastern <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-75" data-page="75" data-folio="105" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n74_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 105">[p. 105]</button> Bengal formed a part of his kingdom, and also that the Varmman Dynasty had ceased to reign. The kingdom was bounded on the East by that of Nānyadeva in Mithila and that of Madanapāla in Magadha. Vijayasena must have reigned for at least forty years, as his newly-discovered copper-plate grant was issued in the 37th year of his reign. He was succeeded by his son Vallālasena, and the name of his wife Vilāsadevī is known to us both from his own copper-plate grant and that of his son. Only two inscriptions of Vijayasena have been discovered up to date. The most important one is the Deopārā praśasti, recording the erection of the temple of Pradyūmneśvara, which must form the basis of all new accounts of the Sena Dynasty, for some years to come. The other inscription is the newly-discovered copper-plate grant, which was brought to me for decipherment by a friend several years ago, but which I am unable to trace at present. This plate records the grant of a village to a Brāhmaṇa of Śāṇḍilya Gotra as the <em>dakṣiṇā</em> of the <em>Tulāpuruṣa ceremony</em> performed by the Queen Vilāsadevī, and was issued from Vīkrampura in the 37th year of the King. Vijayasena's death seems to have taken place about the year 1108 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> Vijayasena was succeeded by his son Vallālasenadeva, who seems to have been an aged man when he came to the throne. His name is well known throughout Bengal as the founder of Kulinism. But as neither his own copper-plates nor those of his son Lakṣmaṇasena contain any references to Kulinism, even when referring to Brāhmaṇas to whom land was granted, the legend about its origin should be accepted with great caution. The whole system may be of much later origin and of no historical importance at all. Vallālasena's dates, as found in some works on Law and Astronomy, the authorship of which are ascribed to him, are misleading. These dates are found in some verses in the Dānasāgara, a work on Law, and in the Adbhūtasāgara, a work on Astronomy. I have pointed out elsewhere that these verses are not to be found in all manuscripts of these two works, and should, therefore be taken as later additions.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-018" href="#sec-08-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> According to these verses, the Dānasāgara was compiled by Vallālasena in <span class="sc">s.</span> 1091 = 1169 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-019" href="#sec-08-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> and the Adbhūtasāgara was begun by him in <span class="sc">s.</span> 1090 = 1168 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-020" href="#sec-08-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Mr. Manomohan Chakravartti has discovered another verse in the Dānasāgara, according to which Vallālasena ascended the throne in S. 1081 = 1159 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-021" href="#sec-08-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup>, but these verses are hardly of an historical importance, as they appear to be later additions. If, on later enquiry, these verses can be found in all the manuscripts discovered, even then they cannot be accepted as basis for the construction of a chronology, so long as they are to be found in modern manuscripts. If they can be found in manuscript records of the 12th and 13th century <span class="sc">a.d.</span>, then only these dates can be accepted as correct. I have tried to show elsewhere that the Bodh-Gayā inscriptions of Aśokacalla<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-022" href="#sec-08-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> prove that Lakṣmaṇasena died before 1070 <span class="sc">a.d.</span>,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-023" href="#sec-08-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> consequently, unless some contemporary <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-76" data-page="76" data-folio="106" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n75_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 106">[p. 106]</button> record can be cited as evidence, it cannot be said, on the basis of the verses in the Dān asāgara and the Adbhūtasāgara, that Vallālasena came to the throne in 1159 A.D. and wrote a book on Law ten years later. Only one inscription of this King has been discovered up to date This is a copper-plate grant discovered in January, 1911, at Sītāhāṭi, near Kātwā, in the Burdwan District of Bengal. It records the grant of the village of Vallahiṭṭa in the Uttara Rāḍhā <em>Maṇḍala</em> of the Varddhamāna <em>bhukti</em> to a Brāhmaṇa named Ovāsudeva-Śarmman as the <em>Dakṣiṇā</em> of the <em>Hemāśva-Mahādāna</em> (the gift of a golden horse), performed by the Queen Vilāsadevi, the King's mother, on the 16th Vaiśākha in the 11th year of his reign.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-024" href="#sec-08-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The Dūtaka of this grant was the King's minister of peace and war, <em>Hari-ghoṣa</em>, who is the only officer of Vallālasena whose name has come down to us. Vallālasena married Rāmadevī of the Calukya family and was succeeded by his son Lakṣmaṇasena. As the initial year of the Lakṣmaṇasena era is 1119-20 A.D., so Lakṣmaṇasena must have ascended the throne in that year, consequently, Vallālasena cannot be taken to have reigned more than 12 or 13 years. He seems to have been a peaceably inclined, weak, old man, studious in his habits, and a patron of Brāhmanism. Both he and his father seem to have belonged to the Śaiva sect, as their inscriptions begin with an invocation to Śiva.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gāhadavāla Conquest of Magadha.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Patna Grant of Govinda-candra, V.S. 1183.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Step by step, the Gāhaḍavāla Kings of Kanauj advanced towards the East. Govindacandra seems to have conquered the whole of Magadha in the earlier part of his reign (1114 = 54 A.D.). In 1127 he was in a position to grant a village in the Patna District to a Brāhmaṇa. An unpublished grant, a photograph of which has been kindly lent to me by Prof. Jadunath Sircar, M.A., of the Patna College, records the donation of the village of Pāḍoli, together with the village of Guṇāve in the Maṇiari <em>Pattalā</em>, to a Brāhmaṇa of the Kāśyapa Gotra named Gaṇeśvara-Śarman, after bathing in the Ganges at Kānyakubja, on Sunday, the 11th of the dark half of Jyaiṣṭha of the Vikrama year 1183 = 1127 A.D. I have been given to understand by Prof. Sircar that this new inscription will shortly be published in the Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal. The invasion of Magadha by the Gāhaḍavāla King seems to have led to hostilities between Govindacandra and Lakṣmaṇasena. In the Madanapāḍa Grant of Viśvarūpasena and Edilpur Grant of Keśavasena, Lakṣmaṇasena is said to have erected pillars of victory at Benares (<em>Vārāṇasī</em>) and at Allahabad (<em>Triveṇī</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Belāyāṁ dakṣiṇavdher-mmūśala-dhara gadāpāṇi saṁvāsavedyām Tīrotsaṅge triveṇ-</em><br>
<em>yāh kamalabhava-makhārambha nirvvyājapute yen-occair-yajña-yūpaiḥ saha</em><br>
<em>samara-jayastambhamālā nyadhāyi. v.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-025" href="#sec-08-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Govindracandra invades Bengal.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Maṇiari <em>Pattalā</em> mentioned in the copper-plate grant of Govindacandra mentioned above has been identified with the modern Muner, a village of considerable importance in the Patna District, which was a well-known place in the 12th century. Bakhtiyar Khilji directed some of his expeditions against this town before the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-77" data-page="77" data-folio="107" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n76_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 107">[p. 107]</button> conquest of Bihar and Bengal.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-026" href="#sec-08-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Govindacandra advanced as far as Monghyr in the year 1146 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> and granted the village of Tatacavāḍa in the Paṇḍalā <em>Pattalā</em>, in Govisā-loka, that belonged to Dudhāli in Saruvāra, to a Brāhmaṇa named Ṭhakkura Śrīdhara, after bathing in the Ganges at Mudgagiri (Monghyr) on the occasion of the Akṣayatṛtiya, on Monday the 3rd of the bright half of the Vaiśākha of the Vikrama year 1202, the 15th April, 1146 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-030" href="#sec-08-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Govindacandra was most probably leading an expedition into Bengal when he bathed in the Ganges at Monghyr, and granted the village mentioned above. The expedition was no doubt unsuccessful, because, otherwise, the event would surely have been mentioned in some Gāhaḍavāla inscription. The use of the era of Lakṣmaṇasena in two inscriptions at Bodh-Gayā<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-027" href="#sec-08-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> prove that in spite of the efforts of the Gāhaḍavāla Kings Eastern Magadha continued to be in the possession of the Senas up to 1193 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> Most probably the river Son was the boundary line of the Gāhaḍavāla and the Sena Kingdoms.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lakṣmaṇasena.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Lakṣmaṇasena, the son of Vallālasena, ascended the throne in 1119 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> He was an energetic and able ruler like his grandfather Vijayasena. In the lifetime of his father he led an expedition into Kaliṅga.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-028" href="#sec-08-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> After his accession to the throne he defeated the King of Benares, i.e., Govindacandra, in battle and conquered Kāmarūpa.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-031" href="#sec-08-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> In the copper-plate grants of his sons, Keśavasena and Viśvarūpasena, he is said to have planted a pillar of victory on the shores of the Southern Ocean, which most probably means that he defeated some Southern King in battle. Nothing is known about his length of reign, but his kingdom consisted of Eastern, Western and Northern Bengal and the eastern part of Magadha. It is also probable that part of Mithilā was included in his kingdom.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Four copper-plate inscriptions and one stone inscription of this king has been discovered up to date. The earliest of these is the Tarpandighi Grant, found in 1874 at Tarpandighi at Gangarampur in the Dinajpur District. It records the grant of the village of Vilvahiṣṭī in the Pauṇḍravardhana <em>bhūkti</em> as the dakṣiṇā of the Golden Horse and Chariot ceremonies (<em>Hemāśva-ratha</em>) to a Brāhmaṇa named Śrī Iśvara Śarmman.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">During the reign of Lakṣmaṇasena the western part of Magadha seems to have passed into the hands of the Gāhaḍavāla Kings of Kanauj. The local rulers practically acquired independence, as an example of which we may cite the name of the Mahānāyaka Pratāpadhavala of Jāpila. The earliest record of this generation is a short rock inscription near the Tutrahi Falls in the Shahabad District, the date of which corresponds to 19th April, 1158 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-029" href="#sec-08-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> According to an unpublished inscription at Rohṭasgaḍh, the King set up some monuments on the 27th March, 1169 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-032" href="#sec-08-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup> In the same district, there is another rock inscription at Tārāchaṇḍī incised in the Vikrama era 1225, corresponding to 1169 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> According to another inscription at Rohtaṣgaḍh, the family to which this dynasty belonged is called Khayāravāla <em>Vaṁsa</em></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-78"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="78" data-folio="108" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n77_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 108">[p. 108]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The relations between this chief and the Gāhaḍavāla Kings of Kanauj has been made clear by the Tārāchaṇḍī inscription of the same prince. This inscription was edited by Dr. Fitz-Edward Hall in 1860.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-033" href="#sec-08-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> According to this inscription, in Samvat 1225 on Wednesday, the 3rd day of the dark half of Jyaiṣṭha, = 16th April, 1169 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, Pratāpadhavala announces that a certain copper-plate recording the grant of the villages of Kalahaṇḍi and Baḍapilā has been obtained by several Brāhmaṇas by bribing one, Deu, the slave of King Vijayacandra of Kānyakubja. The inscription finally adds that the proprietary share of the rent should be collected yearly as before. This inscription shows very clearly that though Pratāpadhavala was semi-independent, he was obliged to recognize the suzerainty of the Gāhaḍavāla King of Kanauj. The villages stated above within his territories could be granted by the King of Kānyakubja to anybody he liked.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">After the death of Lakṣmaṇasena three of his sons seem to have come to the throne :—(1) Mādhavasena, (2) Viśvarūpasena and (3) Keśavasena. Nothing is known about the order of succession of these princes and their dates. Elsewhere I have tried to prove that Mādhavasena precedes the other sons of Lakṣmaṇasena, Viśvarūpa, and he in his turn preceded Keśavasena. One copper-plate inscription of each of these princes have been discovered, viz., those of Viśvarūpa and Keśavasena. A copper-plate of Mādhavasena has been preserved in a monastery in the Tehri State.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-034" href="#sec-08-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Fall of the Senas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Viśvarūpsena is known from his now lost Madanapāḍa Grant<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-035" href="#sec-08-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> of the year 14 of his reign. It records the grant of certain lands in the village Piñjakāṣṭhī in the Vikramapura division (<em>bhāga</em>) of Eastern Bengal (<em>Vaṅga</em>) of the Pauṇḍravarddhana <em>bhukti</em> to a Brāhmaṇa named Viśvarūpadeva-śarmman. His brother Keśavasena is also known from his Edilpur Grant<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-036" href="#sec-08-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> of the year 3, which records the grant of certain lands in the province (<em>pradeśa</em>) of Eastern Bengal, the division (<em>bhāga</em>) of Vikramapura and the <em>bhukti</em> of Pauṇḍravarddhana to Īśvaradeva-Śarmman, a brother of the Viśvarūpadeva Śarmman of the Madanapāḍa Grant. The Sena Dynasty came to an end with the Muhammadan occupation of East Bengal, and the last kings are not known.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Govindapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In 1161 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> we find a king named Govindapāladeva in Magadha. His existence is proved from a stone inscription and six manuscript records. But we are not yet in a position to state clearly whether he belonged to the Imperial Pāla Dynasty or not. Yet the affix Pāla and the Buddhist titles (e.g. <em>Parama-saugata</em>) would lead us to believe that he was descended from them. He seems to have come to the throne in 1161 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-037" href="#sec-08-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> as the Gayā Stone Inscription distinctly mentions that his fourteenth regnal year fell in <span class="sc">v.s.</span> 1232 = 1175 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> A manuscript of the <em>Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā</em> discovered by Hodgson at Nepal, which was copied in the fourth year of the King's reign, mentions the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-79" data-page="79" data-folio="109" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n78_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 109">[p. 109]</button> name of Nālanda (?) as the place of copying.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-038" href="#sec-08-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> So it may be assumed with a tolerable degree of certainty that Govindapāla ruled a portion, probably the eastern one, of Magadha. The MS. was copied in the 38th year of Govindapāla, who is styled Gauṛeśvara, i.e. the year 1198 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-047" href="#sec-08-fn-047" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-047" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-82"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="82" data-folio="112" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n81_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 112">[p. 112]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(8) A MS. of the Prajñāpāramitā of 8000 verses in the collection of the Royal Asiatic Society of Great Britain and Ireland written at Nālanda in the fourth year of the King's reign :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>1. <em>Mātā-pitṛ-pūrvaṁgamaṁ kṛtvā sakala-satva-rāśer-anuttara-jñāna-phal-āvāp- taya iti</em> ∥ <em>Parameśvara Paramabhaṭṭāraka Paramasaugata Mahārājā- dhirāja Śrī-mad-Govindapālasya-vijaya-rājya-saṁvatsare 4 Śūny-odaka- grāma-vāstavya Śrī-man-Nālanda-</em> 2. <em>m-astu sarva-jagatām.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-048" href="#sec-08-fn-048" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-048" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Extent of Kingdom.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It will be observed that out of these eight records only two mention the King as living. In No. 8 we find the usual titles and no peculiarity, consequently it can be admitted that Govindapāla reigned for at least four years. In No. 4 though titles have been omitted yet the absence of such formulæ as “<em>Parameśvaretyādi Rājāvalī- pūrvvavat</em>” at the beginning, and such phrases as “<em>gatarājye</em>,” “<em>atītarājye</em>” and “<em>vinaṣṭa-rājye</em>” make it certain that the King Govindapāla was alive in the 37th year from the date of his consecration, i.e. 1197 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> This being admitted, we find that the phrases <em>gata</em> and <em>atīta</em> are used in other records in a peculiar sense, signifying that the reign of the Prince was at an end, in that particular locality, but that it was still continuing at some other place. Thus in the Gayā Inscription of the Vikrama year 1232 the use of the word <em>gata</em> means that Govindapāla's reign had ceased at Gayā, but was continuing somewhere else. The use of the word <em>Atīta</em> in the MS. discovered by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī, which was copied in 38th year of his reign, signifies that his reign was at an end at the place where the MS. was copied. Only the use of the special word <em>Vinaṣṭa</em> in No. 6 signifies that the remnants of his authority was destroyed in that year, as has been correctly interpreted by Bendall,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-049" href="#sec-08-fn-049" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-049" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> by the Muhammdans under Bakhtyār-Khilji. It appears that Govindapāla ruled lower part of Eastern Magadha close to Nālanda and yet bore the title of <em>Gauḍeśvara</em>. He was recognized as the real King by Buddhists in all parts of the country. He managed to continue his reign till 1199, when Cauhān, Gahaṛwāṛ, Pāla and Sena were all swept away by the whirlwind of Muhammadan invasion.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Postscript.</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">An inscription of the time of Vigrahapāla III was found by me recently on the pedestal of an image of Buddha in the Indian Museum.
