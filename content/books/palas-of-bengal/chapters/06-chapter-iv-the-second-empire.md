<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-38"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="38" data-folio="68" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n37_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 68">[p. 68]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kamboja or Mongolian invasion of North Bengal.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The date in the Dinājpur inscription.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kāmboja kings of Gauḍa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The period which follows is entirely different in character, the principal actors having changed. The great Gurjara-Pratīhāra Empire was rapidly dissolving and the Rāṣṭrakūṭa kings were gradually becoming weaker. Rāṣṭrakūṭa and Gurjara invasions became things of the past. New actors were appearing in the political arena. The invasion of the Great Coḷa Conqueror left a deep impression on north eastern India. It gave Bengal a new dynasty of kings and indirectly hastened the ruin of the Pāla Empire. After the Badal pillar inscription of Nārāyaṇapāla, there is no other inscription which can throw light on the history of Northern Bengal for three generations, i.e. till the time of Mahīpāla I. About this time some Mongolian tribes occupied the whole of the Northern Bengal and either massacred the old inhabitants or gradually forced them back southwards. A monolithic pillar now standing in the grounds of the place of the Mahārājas of Dinājpur bears a record of one of these Mongolian kings, who also claimed to be the lords of Gauḍa (<em>Gauḍeśvara</em>). At present the whole of Northern Bengal is strewn over with pre-Muhammadan ruins and so far the general theory had been, that these temples, monasteries and towns were ruined at the time of the Muhammadan occupation of the country. But recently a plausible theory has been started by Mr. Ramā Prasād Canda, B.A., on the basis of Dinājpur pillar inscription, according to which the ruin of these ancient cities of Northern Bengal should be differently interpreted. The inscription on the Dinājpur pillar was brought to notice in 1871 when it was published with a rude lithograph.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-001" href="#sec-06-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The late Dr. Bloch examined the inscription during one of his tours and hastily gave a reading which I am afraid cannot be supported. Mr. Canda obtained some very clear and beautiful rubbings of this inscription during one of his many visits and submitted a paper on it to the Asiatic Society of Bengal.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-002" href="#sec-06-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> According to Mr. Canda, the Koch, Mech and the Palias of the present day are the descendants of the Mongolians who invaded and settled in North Bengal during the latter half of the ninth and the tenth century <span class="sc">A.D.</span> The inscription on the Dinājpur pillar, which forms the basis of Mr. Canda’s paper, records the erection of a temple of Śiva during the reign of a king of Gauḍa of the Kāmboja race, in the year 888 of some unspecified era. The date is expressed as a chrono-gram: <em>Kuñjara-Ghaṭā-varṣeṇa</em>, which probably means 888. This date cannot be referred to the Vikrama era as in that case it would be equivalent to 831 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, which is too early to suit the characters used in this inscription. Neither can it be referred to the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-39" data-page="39" data-folio="69" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n38_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 69">[p. 69]</button> Gupta era as in that case it would be equal to 1207 A.D., which is certainly too late. The Kalacuri-Cedī era has never been found to have been used in Bengal. The Śaka era suits best though it has but been sparsely used in the North-East. In that case S. 888=966 A.D. falls just after the reign of Devapāla, the Pratīhāra King of Kanauj. At that time the invaders must have settled down so that the invasion itself must have taken place some time earlier. Northern Bengal was in the undisputed possession of Nārāyaṇapāla at the time of the incision of Guravamiśra’s record. So this invasion must have taken place some time between 850-950 A.D. The irruption of these Mongolian hordes must have taken place through the Himalayas, and most probably they were dispossessed of their former homes in the hills by some other invaders. So the Pālas after Nārāyaṇapāla, i.e. Rājyapāla, Gopāla II, and Vigrahapāla II, were having a rather bad time of it with the Gurjara Empire in the West and occasional Rāṣṭrakūṭa raids thrown in, and with Barbarian hordes advancing in untold numbers through the mountain passes of the North. No wonder that Magadha was annexed to the Gurjara-Pratīhāra Empire. At the time of the invasion of Indra III, the Eastern Frontier of the Gurjara-Pratīhāra Empire most probably extended right up to the modern Bhagirathi, and its confluence near Saugor Island. North Bengal must have remained in the possession of the Mongolian kings up to the end of the tenth century A.D. In the beginning of the eleventh century we find that the Pālas have recovered possession of Northern Bengal, and from this time onwards right up to the end of the second Pāla Empire, Northern Bengal continued to be in their possession. At the time of the Dinajpur inscription the Pālas seem to have been deprived of Gauḍa and consequently the Mongolian king became <em>Gauḍeśvara</em>. The name Kāmboja itself is of great interest. Thus far the Kāmbojas or Kamvojas were known to be a northern tribe who lived side by side with the Greeks in Afghanistan and the Western Punjab, as shown by the phrase “<em>Yona-Kāmbojesu</em>” in the XIII Rock Edict of Asoka.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-003" href="#sec-06-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The occurrence of the name in a Bengal inscription does not mean that the Kāmbojas, whole or part, immigrated into Bengal from the Punjab across the whole of Northern India, because that would have been an impossibility in those days, but shows that all Mongolians were called Kāmbojas, and that people with Mongolian features crossed over into Bengal through the Northern Mountains and as Kāmbojas. They may or may not have been a part of the people who became known during the Maurya period as the Kāmbojas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The occupation of Gauḍa by a barbarian tribe, at a time when the whole of Magadha was in the possession of the Gurjaras, shows that the kings of the Pāla dynasty between Nārāyaṇapāla and Vigrahapāla II and Mahīpāla I were kings in name only. Most probably they ruled over an insignificant kingdom surrounded by a large number of petty monarchies. The Tirumalai inscription of Rājendra Coḻa I shows that the ancient Gauḍa and Vaṅga had become divided into a large number of small kingdoms. The exact state and extent of the Pāla dominions under <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-40" data-page="40" data-folio="70" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n39_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 70">[p. 70]</button> Vigrahapāla are not known. Most probably he lost even what had belonged to Gopāla II as his son Mahīpāla I is said to have recovered his paternal kingdom. His only recorded war seems to have been conducted in Eastern Bengal. In the Āmgāchi and Dinājpur grants there is a verse about this:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Deśe prāci pracura-payasi svaccham=āpīya toyaṁ svairaṁ bhrāntvā tad=anu</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>malay-opatyakā-candaneṣu</em>,</span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Kṛtvā sāndrais=taruṣu jaḍatāṁ śīkarair=abhra-tulyāḥ prāley-ādreḥ kaṭakam=</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>abhajan yasya senā-gajendrāḥ.—verse 11 Dinajpur grant of Mahīpāla.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-004" href="#sec-06-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But this is not very certain, as in the Amgāchi grant of Vigrahapāla the verse is attributed to Vigrahapāla III.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-005" href="#sec-06-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">After the death of Vigrahapāla II, Mahīpāla succeeded to what remained of the first Empire of the Pālas. In his Dinājpur grant he is said to have recovered the kingdom of his father:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Hata-sakala-vipakṣaḥ saṁgare vāhu-darp̣pād=anadhikṛta-viluptaṁ rājyam=āsādya</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>pitṛyaṁ</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Nihita-caraṇa-padmo bhūbhṛtāṁ murdhni tasmād=abhavad=avani-pālaḥ Śrī-Mahīpāladevaḥ.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-006" href="#sec-06-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mahīpāla I conquers Magadha</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">and Mithilā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After the recovery of his paternal kingdom, Mahīpāla I must have turned his attention to the West. In his sixth year Nālanda was included in his kingdom as a manuscript copied at that place in that year of the king has been acquired for the Asiatic Society of Bengal by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī. Magadha seems to have continued in the possession of the king for a long time. In the eleventh year of the king an image of Buddha was dedicated in or near the temple of the Mahābodhi at Bodh-Gaya, and in the same year the great temple at Nālanda was restored, as it had been burnt down in a fire. After the conquest of Magadha, Mahīpāla seems to have attacked Tīrabhukti or Mithilā which continued in the possession of the king at least till his 48th year. His kingdom seems to have extended as far as Benares and continued to be included in it till 1020 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> In that year, two persons were deputed by the king, named Sthirapāla and Vasantapāla, to execute some repairs near the Buddhist city. The state of the Gurjara kingdom of Kanauj favoured the occupation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Occupies Benares.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Only a few years before it had been devastated by Sultan Mahmūd of Ghazni, and after his departure, the king Rājyapāladeva had been deposed and murdered by the Indian Princes for having submitted to an alien conqueror. At that time Trilocanapāla was seated on the throne of the Gurjara-Pratīhāras and most probably his power did not extend beyond the confluence of the Yamunā.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Invasion of</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rājendra Coḷa I.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Oḍra and Kośala.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Daṇḍabukti and Southern Rāḍhā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In spite of the victories during the earlier part of his reign, Mahīpāla I suffered some very severe reverses from the time of the rise of the Cedis, under Gāṅgeyadeva <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-41" data-page="41" data-folio="71" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n40_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 71">[p. 71]</button> and Karṇadeva and the invasion of the Coḷa king Rājendra Coḷa I. The invasion of the Coḷa king took place before the 13th year of that prince, i.e. before 1025 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> Rājendra Coḷa earned the <em>viruda</em> of “<em>Gaṅgegoṇḍā</em>” or “Gangā-vijayī” by pushing as far North as the Ganges during this raid. The Tirumalai Rock inscription of the great conqueror records the Northern Campaign in detail. It is said that the king seized the “Oḍḍa-<em>viṣaya</em>” which was difficult to approach. This is clearly the Oḍra <em>Viṣaya</em> of the copper-plate inscriptions of Orissa. Then he entered “Koṣalaināḍu,” i.e. the Kośala of the inscriptions of the Soma-vaṁśī kings of Orissa.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-007" href="#sec-06-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Next in order comes the subjugation of Daṇḍabhukti. The province has been identified by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śastrī with the modern province of Bihar, because the ancient name of the town of Bihar was called Otantapuri by the Tibetans and Adwand Bihār by the Muhammadans. But this identification is scarcely tenable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Uddandapura,</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rāḍhā as an ancient name.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The place is mentioned in the <em>Rāmacarita</em> of Sandhyākaranandi where a person named Jayasiṁha is said to have been its ruler and is said to have aided Rāmapāla in his wars in Northern Bengal. This man is said to have defeated Karṇa-Keśari, the king of Orissa.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-008" href="#sec-06-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Most probably Daṇḍabhukti was the march-land between Orissa and Bengal, corresponding to the modern British districts of Midnapur and Balasore, and the man had defeated the king of Orissa in one of his expeditions against Bengal. It is more probable for a king of the march-lands to come into conflict with the king of Orissa than for the ruler of Magadha. Moreover the order in which the names of the countries are mentioned prevents us from supposing that Bihar is the country mentioned as we shall see later on. From Daṇḍabhukti the king passed on to Bengal, attacking and occupying the province of “Takkaṇa-Lāḍam.” This name has been taken to be the equivalent of “Dakṣiṇa Lāṭa” by the late Dr. Kielhorn, which is the ancient name of Southern Gujarat.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-009" href="#sec-06-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> But Messrs. Hultzsch and Venkayya take it to mean “Dakṣiṇa Virāṭa” or Southern Berar.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-010" href="#sec-06-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Mr. Venkayya is a great authority on Tamil, and he supposes that “the Tamil term “<em>Ilaḍa</em>” does not correspond to Sanskrit <em>Lāṭa</em> (Gujarat) but to <em>Virāṭa</em> (Berar)”. But nowhere did it strike the learned scholars that the order in which the countries are mentioned, prevents us from supposing that either Berar or Gujarat is mentioned. In fact the country mentioned is Southern Rāḍhā. Mr. Venkayya will find, on re-considering the question, that <em>Dakṣiṇa-Rāḍhā</em> is a better equivalent for Tamil <em>Takkaṇa-Lāḍam</em> than <em>Dakṣiṇa-Virāṭa</em>. Immediately after “Takkaṇa-Lāḍam” we have the mention of <em>Vaṅgāla-deśa</em>, which all authorities agree as being equal to Vaṅga or Eastern Bengal. No sane man would turn from Orissa to conquer Southern Gujarat or Berar and then return to the East to conquer East Bengal, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-42" data-page="42" data-folio="72" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n41_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 72">[p. 72]</button> after which he turns back to the West to defeat Mahīpāla in North Bengal and again rushes to North Gujarat or Berar to conquer it. The more natural explanation is that Rājendra Coḷa defeated Raṇaśūra, the ruler of Southern Rāḍhā, and then passed on through that country to invade Vaṅga. From very early times a part of Bengal has been called Rāḍhā. It occurs in a dated inscription of the Indo-Scythian period as Rārā. This inscription is at present in the Indian Museum, in Calcutta, but it was discovered in Mathurā in the United Provinces. The record mentions the erection of a Jaina image in the year 62 of the Kuṣana era = 150 A.D. at the request of a Jaina monk who was an inhabitant of the country of Rārā.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-011" href="#sec-06-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> In comparatively modern times the name has been found on two copper-plate inscriptions:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(1) The newly discovered grant of the Sena king Vallālasena, found at Sitāhāṭi, near Kāṭwā, in the Burdwān district of Bengal, where we find that the village granted, Vāllahiṭṭi, was situated in the North Rāḍhā (<em>Uttara-Rāḍhā-maṇḍale</em>).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-012" href="#sec-06-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The very name Uttara-Rāḍhā occurs in the Tirumalai inscription as we shall see later on. Besides this, the kings of the Sena dynasty seem to have ruled in the Rāḍhā country:—</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Vaṁśe tasy=ābhyudayini sadācāra-caryā-niruḍhi-prauḍhāṁ</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Rāḍhām-akalita-carair=bhūṣayantoऽnubhāvaiḥ,</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Śaśvad=viśv-ābhaya-vitaraṇa-sthūla-lakṣyāvalakṣaiḥ kīrtty-ullolaiḥ snapita-viyato</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>jajñire rājaputrāḥ.—verse 3.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-013" href="#sec-06-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There being a Uttara-Rāḍhā we can say from immediate inference, that there was a Dakṣina-Rāḍhā, which in Tāmil becomes “Takkaṇa-Lāḍam.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(2) Besides this the Kenduāpatna plates of Narasiṁhadeva II of Orissa, dated Śaka 1217 = 1296 A.D., show very clearly that Rāḍhā and Vārendrī were well-known names of divisions of Bengal:—</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Rāḍhā-Vārendra-yavanī-nayan-āñjan-āśru-pūreṇa dūra-viniveśitakālima-śrīḥ,</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Tad-vipralambha-karaṇ-ādbhuta-nistaraṅgā Gaṅgāpi nūnam-amunā Yamun=</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>ādhun=ābhūt.—verse 84.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-014" href="#sec-06-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Eastern Bengal.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Defeat of Mahīpāla and conquest of Northern Rāḍha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At the time of the Coḷa invasion a king named Raṇaśūra was ruling Southern Rāḍhā. In Bengal there is a tradition that a dynasty of kings with the affix Śūra ruled in Bengal before the Pālas. We have no reliable evidence for this. But three kings of this family, at least with the word Śūra affixed to their names, have been mentioned in epigraphs. These are: Raṇaśūra, of the Tirumalai inscription; Lakṣmīśūra, a king of a division of Bengal named Apara-Mandāra, a contemporary of Rāmapāla, who was the headman of all feudatories of Forest lands (<em>samastāṭavika-sāmanta- cakra-cuḍāmaṇiḥ</em>); a man named Damaśūra, who is mentioned in a newly-discovered inscription of the time of Gopāla III, found at Manda in the Rājshāhi district of Bengal. After conquering Southern Rāḍhā, the Coḷa king did not proceed to subdue the northern portion of it, but on the other hand, passed eastwards towards Vaṅga, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-43" data-page="43" data-folio="73" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n42_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 73">[p. 73]</button> which was then ruled by a king named Govinda-Candra. This king has been wrongly identified with a king named Govinda-Candra or Govi-Canda, about whom some songs are current in the State of Kuch-Bihar and the Rungpur district of Bengal. The king of that name mentioned in the Tirumalai inscription is expressly stated to be the King of Eastern Bengal, and so there is very little chance of identifying him with the local hero of Rungpur. After conquering Eastern Bengal, Rājendra Coḷa turned towards the West and faced Mahīpāla, who had been rightly identified by the late Dr. Kielhorn with the Pāla king Mahīpāla I, who was defeated. The inscription is so worded that one at once understands that by defeating Mahīpāla, the king was able to reach “<em>Uttira-Lāḍam</em>” and the Ganges. <em>Uttira-Lāḍam</em> for the same reasons as have been stated above in the case “<em>Takkaṇa-Lāḍam</em>” should be taken to be Northern Rāḍhā, which is actually mentioned as a maṇḍala in the Sitāhāṭi grant of Vallālasena. Moreover there is no evidence to prove that Berar or Virāṭa was divided into two parts at any time. Again from Bengal Rājendra Coḷa reached Uttara-Rāḍha and after that the Ganges. It is a far cry from Berar to the Ganges, but the sacred river which added lustre to the conquest of Rājendra Coḷa I in the eyes of the Southern people actually forms the Northern boundary of Rāḍha. The divisions of Bengal across the great river are known as Mithilā and Vārendra, the latter of which is mentioned in the Rāma-carita,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-015" href="#sec-06-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and at least three copper-plates. So now it is clear that the Ganges formed the Northern boundary of the conquest of Rajendra Coḷa I. Curiously enough he did not attempt to cross the Ganges to the other side. The Tirumalai inscription being a <em>Praśasti</em> does not mention such details. But the desired details are supplied by an ancient manuscript discovered by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī and now in the library of the Asiatic Society of Bengal. In 1893 the Mahāmahopādhyāya published notes on a find of ancient Sanskrit manuscripts among which was a drama named Caṇḍa-Kauśika, by Ārya Kṣemīśvara. This play was enacted before the king by his order, and it contains a verse in which the king Mahīpāla I is compared with Candragupta and a people named Karṇāṭakas, to the Nandas. So this contemporary work gives the credit of defeating the Karṇāṭakas to Mahīpāla I. The Karṇāṭakas seem to be the southerners who invaded Bengal under Rājendra Coḷa I. It appears that though Mahīpāla I was defeated by Rājendra Coḷa when he crossed into Rāḍhā from East Bengal, he prevented him from crossing the Ganges into Varendra or Northern Bengal, and so the Coḷa conqueror had to turn back from the banks of the Ganges. The manuscript on which Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī relies is not a modern one, as it was copied in 1331 <span class="sc">a.d.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-016" href="#sec-06-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The invasion of the Coḷa king did not change the political divisions of the country, but it left one permanent mark in the shape of a body of settlers, who occupied the thrones of Bengal and Mithilā as the Sena and Karṇāta dynasties during the latter days of the Pālas. The Coḷa invasion took place, as has been stated above, before the thirteenth <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-44" data-page="44" data-folio="74" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n43_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 74">[p. 74]</button> year of the king, i.e. 1025 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> The very next year we find that the Wheel of Law at Benares is being repaired, and a new temple (<em>Gandha-kuṭī</em>) built by the brothers Sthirapāla and Vasantapāla under the orders of the king.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The struggle with the Cedi Empire.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Mithilā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The record of these events is found in an inscription discovered amidst the ruins of Sārnāth near Benares more than a hundred years ago.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-017" href="#sec-06-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> It is incised on the pedestal of an image of Buddha, which is at present in the Provincial Museum at Lucknow. This image was dedicated in the Vikrama year 1083=1026 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> Very soon after this Benares was taken away from the Pālas by the Cedi Emperor of Gāṅgeyadeva who invaded North-Eastern India about this time and had occupied it six or seven years ago. Some time before 1881 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, some metal images were found near a village Imādpur in the Muzaffarpur district of Bengal,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-018" href="#sec-06-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> which were pronounced by Dr. Hoernle to have been dedicated in the 48th year of Mahīpāladeva.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-019" href="#sec-06-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> As these images were found in Tirhut or Tīrabhukti, it is natural to conclude that Mithilā was in the possession of the Pālas up to the 48th year of Mahīpāla I. But six years before the erection of the temple of Sārnāth, Mithilā passed out of the hands of the Pālas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the year 1020 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> Gāṅgeyadeva was in possession of Tīrabhukti or Mithilā. A copy of the Rāmāyaṇa copied in that year v. s. 1076 mentions Tīrabhukti as being in the possession of Gāṅgeyadeva:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Saṁvat 1076 āṣāḍha badi 4 mahārājādhirāja puṇyāvaloka-somavaṁśodbhava-Gau-ḍadhvaja-Śrīmad-Gāṅgeyadeva-bhujyamāna Tīrabhuktau kalyāṇavijayarājye.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-020" href="#sec-06-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Very soon after Benares passed into the hands of the Cedīs. Karṇṇadeva, the son of Gāṅgeyadeva, was in possession of Benares in 1042 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> (Kalacuri-Cedī year 793).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-021" href="#sec-06-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> Tīrabhukti or Mithilā was never recovered by the Pālas. The only Pāla records referring to this Province are the Bhāgalpur grant of Nārāyaṇapāla and the Imādpur image inscription of Mahīpāla I.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Successor and length of reign</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mahīpāla I was succeeded by his son Nayapāla, who is called Nyāyapāla on the authority of some unpublished record, by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī. According to Tārānātha, Mahīpāla reigned for fifty-two years, which is most probably correct as the Imādpur images were dedicated in the 48th year of the king. Of the relations of the king we only know the names of the brothers Sthirapāla and Vasantapāla, who were most probably nearly related to him besides his son Nayapāla. The long reign of Mahīpāla I is very fruitful in inscription and manuscript records. The earliest of these is the manuscript of Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā, now in the University Library at Cambridge. The colophon runs thus:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Parameśvara paramabhaṭṭāraka-paramasaugata-mahārājādhirāja Śrīman-Mahīpāladeva pravarddhamāna-vijayarājye saṁvat 5 Āśvine Kṛṣṇe.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-022" href="#sec-06-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-45"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="45" data-folio="75" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n44_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 75">[p. 75]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The date next in order is to be found in a manuscript of the same work, collected by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī, for the Asiatic Society of Bengal. The reading of the colophon as revised by the late Dr. Theodor Bloch runs as follows:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Deyadharmmeyaṁ pravaramahāyānayāyinaḥ Tāḍivāḍi-Mahāvihārīya āvasthi- tena Śākyācārya-sthavira-Sādhuguptasya yad = atra puṇyan = tad = bhavatv = ācāry = opādhyāya-mātā-pitṛ-puraṅgamaṁ kṛtvā sakala-satva-rāśer = anu- ttara-jñāna phal = āvāptaya iti. Paramabhaṭṭāraka-Mahārājādhirāja-Para- meśvara-Paramasaugata Śrīmad-Vigrahapāladeva-pādānudhyāta Parama- bhaṭṭāraka-Mahārājādhirāja-Parameśvara-Paramasaugata Śrīman = Mahīpā- ladeva-pravarddhamāna-Kalyāṇavijayarājye ṣaṣṭha-sambatsare abhilikhyamāne yatrāṅke saṁvat 6 Kārttika-Kṛṣṇa-trayodaśyān = tithau maṅgala- vāreṇa bhaṭṭārikā-niṣpāditam = iti || Śrī Nālandāvasthita-Kalyāṇamitra-Cin- tāmaṇikasya likhita iti.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-023" href="#sec-06-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This colophon proves that in the sixth year of Mahīpāla Nālanda was in his possession, and thus a part at least of Magadha was included in his dominions. Next in order comes an inscription incised on the pedestal of an image of Buddha, in the attitude of touching the earth (<em>Bhūmisparśa mudrā</em>). This image is now being worshipped as one of the five Pāṇḍus, in a small shrine just in front of the entrance of the great temple at Bodh-Gayā. The inscription consists of three lines, in an imperfect state of preservation, the first part of each line having lost a number of letters. It is dated in the eleventh year of the reign of Mahīpāla, presumably the first, as the letters still show signs of acute angles at their lower extremities. It has been referred to by Cunningham.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-024" href="#sec-06-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The text runs:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Deya-dharmmoyaṁ ......... tad-bhavatv = ācāry = opādhāya-mātā-pitṛ-pūrvvaṅgamaṁ kṛtvā sakala-satva-rāśer = anuttara-jñān = āvāptaya-iti .| Mahā-</em></li><li>2. [<em>rājādhirāja-Parameśvara-Pa</em>]<em>ramabhaṭṭāraka-Paramasaugata-Śrī-mān = Mahīpāladeva-pravarddhamāna-vijayarājye ekādaśame saṁvatsare abhilikhya</em>[<em>māne</em>]</li><li>3. ......... <em>pañcamyān = tithau gandha-Kūṭī-dvaya-sahitā ..... karitāv = iti.</em></li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The restoration of the Great Vihāra at Nālandā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The name of the donor of the two temples (<em>Gandha-Kūṭidvaya</em>) and the image is unfortunately lost. As the name of the month in this inscription is illegible it is impossible to state whether it was incised before or after Bālāditya’s Nālandā inscription of the same year which has been placed next in order. This inscription was discovered by Broadley among the ruins of the great Vihāra at Nālandā where it was found on a door-jamb. According to this inscription the great temple at Nālandā was restored after being burnt down by a man named Bālāditya, a Jyāvisa of Telāḍhaka (modern Telara) who had emigrated from Kauśāmbī, in the eleventh year of Mahīpāladeva.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-025" href="#sec-06-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-46"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="46" data-folio="76" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n45_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 76">[p. 76]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The conquest of Northern Bengal must have taken place some years earlier. In his ninth year Mahīpāla granted the village of Kuraṭapallikā, with the exception of Cuṭapallikā, in the Gokalikā <em>maṇḍala</em>, Koṭivarṣa <em>viṣaya</em> of the Pauṇḍravarddhana <em>bhukti</em> to a Brāhmaṇa named Kṛṣṇādityaśarman. It has been proved by another inscription, the Maṇahali grant of Madanapāla, that the Koṭivarṣa <em>viṣaya</em> was situated in Northern Bengal, as both inscriptions have been discovered in the Dinājpur district.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-026" href="#sec-06-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> An inscription incised on the pedestal of a colossal image of Buddha, still in situ, at Tetrawan, an ancient site six miles from Bihār in the Patna District of Bengal, contains the name of Mahīpāla, the rest having become illegible.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-027" href="#sec-06-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Most probably it was dedicated during the reign of Mahīpāladeva. The images discovered at Imādpur in the Muzaffarpur district of Bengal in 1881 were most probably dedicated in the 48th year of Mahīpāla I,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-028" href="#sec-06-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> as Mahīpāla II had a very short reign. The 48th year of Mahīpāla I must have fallen before 1020 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, as in that year the Cedi Emperor Gāṅgeyadeva was in possession of Tīrabhukti or Tirhut. The last inscription of Mahīpāla is the Sarnath inscription of the Vikrama year 1083. This inscription was either posthumous, or incised when the city of Benares had passed from the hands of the Pālas to those of the Cedis. The repair of the Wheel of Law and the building of the temple seem to have begun some time before and the work was completed either after Mahīpāla’s death or in his last year, when he had lost Benares and Tirhut. Mahīpāla was succeeded by his son Nayapāla, called Nyāyapāla by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstri, on some unknown authority. His minister’s name was Vāmaṇabhaṭṭa, who is the Dūtaka of the Bangarh grant of this king called the Dinājpur grant by Dr. Kielhorn.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nayapāla Acc. 1025—30 <span class="sc">A.D.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Nayapāla succeeded the throne of the Pālas some time between 1025—30 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> At that time the extent of the Pāla Empire had been considerably diminished by the loss of Benares and Tīrabhukti. Gāṅgeyadeva was succeeded by his son Karṇa, who with the help of some Southerners overran the whole of Northern India. The Nāgpur praśasti of Udayāditya of Mālava speaks of him as one who, joined by the Karṇāṭakas, had swept over the earth like a mighty ocean:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Tasmin=vāsava-vandhutām=upagate rājye ca kuly=ākule</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Magnasvāmini tasya vandhur=Udayādityo-bhavad-bhūpatiḥ</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Yen=oddhṛtya mahārṇṇav=opama-mīlat=Karṇṇāṭa-Karṇṇa-prabhum=urvvīpālakadarthitām bhuvam=imāṁ Śrīmad-Varāhāyitām—verse</em> 32.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-029" href="#sec-06-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">According to the Bheraghat inscription of Alhaṇadevī, we find:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Pāṇḍyaś=caṇḍimatām=mumoca Muralas=tatyāja garvva-grahaṁ</em></span><br>

In the Karanbel inscription of Jayasiṁhadeva it is stated that Karṇṇa was waited upon by the Coḍa, Kuṅga, Hūṇa, Gauḍa, Gurjara and Kīra princes:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Nīcaiḥ sañcara Coḍa-Kuṅga kim=idaṁ phalgu tvayā valgyate Hūṇ=aivaṁ raṇituṁ</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>na yuktam=iha te tvaṁ Gauḍa garvvan=tyaja</em>,</span><br>
<span class="line"><em>m=aivaṁ Gurjjara garjja Kīra nibhṛto varttasva sevā-gatān=itthaṁ yasya mitho-virodhi-nṛpatīn dvāstho vininye janaḥ.—L.</em> 11—12.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-031" href="#sec-06-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">According to the Cedī inscriptions Karṇṇa subdued or defeated the king of Gauḍa, whoever he might be. Mr. Monmohan Cakravartti first of all pointed out mentions of a war between Nayapāla and the king of Karṇya. The term “king of Karṇya” seems to be a translation of the Sanskrit word “Karṇarāja,” “the king Karṇa.” The form Karṇya seems to be a mistake.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-032" href="#sec-06-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> In his article on the Kṛṣṇadvārikā temple inscription of Nayapāla Mr. Cakravartti has pointed out that Atīśa mediated between Nayapāla and the king of Karṇya about the year 1035 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> So the Cedi Emperor Karṇadeva, who is in reality the same person as the king of Karṇya of Tibetan literature, must have invaded Magadha some time before 1035 <span class="sc">A.D.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-033" href="#sec-06-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The incidents of the campaign are mentioned in Rai Śarat Candra Dās Bahadur’s article on the Life of Atīśa:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Karṇadeva, the Cedī, invades Magadha.</aside></div><blockquote><p>“During Atīśa’s residence at Vajrasena a dispute having risen between the two, Nayapāla, king of Magadha, and the Tirthika, king of Karṇya of the West, the latter made</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>war upon Magadha. As the inscription itself was found in the temple of Narasiṁha which is only few paces behind that of Gadādhara at Gayā it seems certain that the ancient materials which have been profusely used in the modern temple of Gadādhara are the remains of the temple built by Viśvarūpa in the fifteenth year of the reign of Nayapāladeva.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Kṛṣṇa-Dvārikā temple inscription referred to above also records the erection of temple of Viṣṇu in the fifteenth year of Nayapāla:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Saptāmvu-rāśi-visarat (ac·ch) ślatha mekhalāyā asyā bhūvaḥ kati na bhūmi-bhujo-vabhūvuḥ,</em></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Siddhim na kasyacid=agād=yad=analpa-kalpais=ten=ātra Kīrttanam=akāri</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Janārdanasya,—verse</em> 17.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-042" href="#sec-06-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The modern temple of Kṛṣṇa-dvārikā is built almost entirely of ancient materials and it is quite possible that these materials are the only remnants of Viśvāditya or Viśvarūpa’s temple. The only other existing record of Nayapāla is in the colophon of a manuscript of Pañca-rakṣā in the collection of the Cambridge University:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Deyadharmosyam = pravara-mahāyāna-yāyinyāḥ Paramopāsikā-Rājñī-Uddākāyā yad=atra puṇyan=tad=bhavatv=ācāry=opādhyāya-matā-pitṛ (pūrvvaṅgama) ṅ-kṛtvā sakala-satva-rāśer-anuttara-jñān=āvāptaya iti || Paramasaugata-Mahārājādhirāja-Parameśvara Śrī-man=Nayapāladeva-pravarddhamāna-vijayarājye saṁvat</em> 14 <em>Caitra dine</em> 27 <em>likhit-eyaṁ bhaṭṭārikā iti.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-043" href="#sec-06-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Nothing else is known about Nayapāla and his relations. He was succeeded by his son Vigrahapāla III. Nayapāla’s reign most probably did not extend beyond the date of the Kṛṣṇa-dvārikā and Gadādhara temple inscriptions and seems to have come to an end some time between 1045 and 1050 <span class="sc">a.d.</span> It is said in a commentary on Cakradatta that Cakrapāṇi Datta was the kitchen superintendent of king Nayapāla.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-044" href="#sec-06-fn-044" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-044" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vigrahapāla III: his war.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Marriage with Princess Yauvanaśrī, the daughter of Karṇa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At the beginning of his reign Vigrahapāla came into conflict with his father’s antagonist, the Cedi Emperor Karṇa. Karṇa’s power at that time was at its lowest ebb. He was being constantly defeated by the neighbouring princes. He had a very long reign, his own with that of his son having covered a century. In the height of his power he had overrun the whole of Northern India but in his old age he suffered many reverses. He was defeated by the Candella Kīrttivarman,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-045" href="#sec-06-fn-045" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-045" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> by Udayāditya of Mālava,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-046" href="#sec-06-fn-046" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-046" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> by Bhīmadeva I of Anahilvād, who is eulogised by the grammarian Hema-candra for having defeated Karṇa in battle,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-047" href="#sec-06-fn-047" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-047" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> and by the Western Cālukya Someśvara I, which is recorded by the poet <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-50" data-page="50" data-folio="80" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924096224476/page/n49_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 80">[p. 80]</button> Bilhaṇa in his <em>Vikramāṅka-deva Carita</em>, where Karṇa is mentioned as the god of death to the Lord of the Kalañjara mountains, e.g. the Caṇḍellas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-048" href="#sec-06-fn-048" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-048" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> In his last war with the Pālas, Karṇa was defeated and sued for peace. Vigrahapāla III married the aged king’s daughter Yauvanaśrī. Karṇa’s war with Vigrahapāla and his subsequent relationship was made known to us by Mahāmahopādhyāya Hara Prasāda Śāstrī’s unique discovery, “The Rāmacarita of Sandhyākara-nandi”:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line"><em>Anyatra | yo Vigrahapālo Yauvanaśriyā Karṇasya rājñaḥ sutayā saha Kṣauṇīmudūḍhavān | Sahasā valen = āvito-rakṣito raṇajitaḥ saṅgrāmajitaḥ Karṇo Dāhal</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>= ādhipatir = yena | Raṇajita eva parantu rakṣito na unmūlitaḥ—Commentary</em></span><br>
<span class="line"><em>on verse</em> 9.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-049" href="#sec-06-fn-049" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-049" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is evident from the commentary the Karṇa suffered a severe reverse at the hands of his future son-in-law and that though defeated he was not “uprooted,” i.e. deprived of his kingdom. It may be that the proud Cedī gave his daughter to Vigrahapāla to avert a calamity. Vigrahapāla III probably had a very short reign, not exceeding thirteen years. Had he lived longer the Pāla Empire may have lasted for some time. His sons were continually quarrelling among themselves and reigned for very short periods. The subordinate princes eagerly availed themselves of the opportunity of throwing off the yoke and the Pāla princes never gained the opportunity of subjugating the territories lost at this time.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Three inscriptions of Vigrahapāla III have been discovered as yet, of which one is on a copper-plate and the other two on stone. The copper-plate is the well-known one from Āmgachi in the Dinājpur District.
