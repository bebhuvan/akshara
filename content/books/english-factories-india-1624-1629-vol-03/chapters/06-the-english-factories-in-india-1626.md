<span id="page-170" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Khwāja Murād Beg, Governor of Dābhol, to the [President and Council at Surat?], January</span> 1, 1626 (<em>O.C.</em> 1215).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Thanks them for their letter; also the Governor of Surat, to whom he will send an answer shortly. In reply to their request for a pass to establish a factory at his port, he is quite willing to further such a scheme. This letter will serve instead of a pass. Should any provisions, wood, or water be desired, he will furnish them gladly. Desires that they will in return secure from capture ships frequenting his port, and will aid them if met with at sea. (<em>Translation. 1 p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Robert Fox’s Account of the Voyage of the</span> <em>James</em> <span class="sc">from Gombroon to Surat</span> (<em>Marine Records</em>, vol. xxxix).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1626, <em>January</em> 1. The <em>James, Jonas, Anne, Falcon, Spy,</em> and six Dutch ships sailed from Gombroon. <em>January</em> 24. Saw the coast to the west of Diu. <em>January</em> 29. Reached Swally, where they found four Dutch ships from Batavia. (3 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Richard Monk’s Account of the Same Voyage</span> (<em>Ibid.</em>, vol. xl).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1626, <em>January</em> 1. The fleet sailed. <em>January</em> 24. Saw the Indian coast near Diu. <em>January</em> 29. Anchored off Swally, where they found four Dutch ships. <em>January</em> 30. The fleet went into the Hole, except the <em>Anne,</em> which followed next day. (1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-171"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="171" data-folio="117" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n170_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 117">[p. 117]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Francis Pinder's Account of the Same Voyage</span> (<em>Ibid.</em>, vol. xlii).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1626, <em>January</em> 1. The two fleets sailed. <em>January</em> 5. They were opposite Jask. <em>January</em> 25. In sight of land. <em>January</em> 29. Anchored 'without the swatch of Swally'. Found there three Dutch ships<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-001" href="#sec-06-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and a pinnace with a prize. 'These ships towld us thay had past the straights to the southerd of Magelayn and in the South Sea thay had burned near 100 vessels and maney townes. Also that the Portingall had 9 sayle of gallions and an armye of frigots at a port some 30 or 40 legues to the southerd of us, which with both our forces we might essily vanquesh; so their was pretence of exsecution of it, but as smoke it vanisht, thoughe of Englishe and Dutch we wear 14 ships and pineses.' (1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Consultation held at Masulipatam by Thomas Mills, Edward Trafford, and Thomas Johnson, January</span> 26, 1626 (<em>Factory Records, Miscellaneous</em>, vol. ix. p. 63).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-002" href="#sec-06-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A letter having lately been received from the President and Council at Batavia, ordering search to be made for some convenient place on the Coast for procuring 'paintings and woven cloths', it is decided to make trial of Armagon, a place about 12 leagues from Pulicat, 'whereof myself, Thomas Mille, have had some experience the time of my residence in Pallicatt, and since my coming hither to Musulapatam have received letters, both from the Naico or lord of that country and merchants there inhabiting, for that negotiation, earnestly soliciting our plantation, fairly promising such accommodation and entertainment to our contentment.' No English vessel is available, but as they have with them thirteen or fourteen of the crew of the <em>Rose</em>, a country boat has been hired and it is decided that Mills and Johnson, accompanied by John Beverley, shall proceed to Armagon to establish a factory, with 8,500 rials of eight for capital. Johnson and Beverley are to be left at the new settlement, with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-172" data-page="172" data-folio="118" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n171_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 118">[p. 118]</button> a man or two to assist. Three murtherers and a number of muskets provided for the boat. Trafford is to remain in charge at Masulipatam and to look after matters at Petapoli. (<em>6 pp.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">President Hawley and Council at Batavia to the Company, February 6, 1626</span> (<em>O.C.</em> 1217).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">While at Lagundy they resolved to refit the <em>Bull</em> for a voyage to Masulipatam, but afterwards, finding her so much decayed, determined to dispatch the <em>Reformation</em> instead. In this they were prevented by the death of so many men. On March 15 they received by the Dutch letters from Masulipatam, Achin, and Surat; ‘by those from Achin and Musulpatam (the copies now sent) you shall perceive the janglings and distractions there, and by those from Surratt their progresse in busines and proceedings.’ They abandoned their settlement at Lagundy on May 29, and the next day sighted the pinnace <em>Spy</em> from Surat. Landed at Batavia on June 1. The <em>Simon and Jude</em> came in on the 4th, and the <em>Royal James</em> arrived six days later under Captain Weddell. She brought George Muschamp, whom the Company had nominated for employment at Masulipatam, but, there being much need of experienced merchants at Batavia, he was appointed a Member of Council here instead. They were unable to lend Weddell any assistance in shipping, but gave him two long field-pieces and some powder. He sailed on August 6 with the <em>James</em> and <em>Spy</em> and three Dutch ships [<em>see</em> p. 91] to encounter the Portuguese at Ormus. He carried a cargo of upwards of 100,000 rials in money and cloves. Arrival of the <em>London</em> from England on August 23. On the 25th they dispatched the <em>Rose</em> to Masulipatam, with a crew of 21 Englishmen and 12 blacks, and a cargo of money, cloves, sandal-wood, alum, and coral. Next day the Dutch signified their intention to send ‘three of the South Sea shipps now arrived’ [<em>see</em> p. 117] to strengthen the fleet intended for Ormus, and demanded whether the English would ‘prepare the like forces’. In reply they pleaded inability for want of men, but expressed a hope that the English at Surat would be able to ‘parralell’ the Dutch fleet. Hearing by accident that Herman van Speult was to be the general of the ships for Surat, they remonstrated with the Council, on the ground of the accusations against him in the Amboyna <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-173" data-page="173" data-folio="119" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n172_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 119">[p. 119]</button> business; but their protest was disregarded and he was sent off in grand style on September 4 [<em>see</em> p. 97]. Letters were received on August 29 through the Dutch from Masulipatam, ‘importing manie manie differences in that factory among themselves;’ replied by the same conveyance on September 14. The <em>Eagle</em> arrived from Surat and Achin on October 31. Have already advised that a Danish ship lately settled a poor factory at Macassar. In returning to Tranquebar, she drove ashore (last September) in the Bay of Bengal, where her goods were all lost and only seven men saved. These made their way to Masulipatam, where a small Danish ship from Tranquebar had previously arrived to settle a factory. ‘They have intercepted our intended factory at Podisera [Pondicherri] for paintings, which will somewhat distract us for those sorts of cloth; but wee have written to Masulpatam for their advice how best wee maie be supplied, and when from you wee maie be releeved with men it shall be one of our first works (God willing) to plant ourselves for the furnishing of such commodities in some one place or other, which of necessity must be effected, for the paintings of those parts and Tanjour are of more use then the cloth of Musulpatam. As for Musulpatam itself, our trade lieth there also on bleeding, cheefly by wicked devices of Governours, tolerated by their weake spirited King, and partly animated by the disagreements between us and the Dutch, as by our letter from thence dated in November (arrived here in a Dutch shipp the 20th December last) amongst the copies will appeare; concerning which busines of Musulpatam it is also our meaning at our next meeting with the Dutch to moove them that some good order between us may be taken for the remooving of these inconveniences by those Governours of Musulpatam.’ (<em>Extracts only. 3½ pp.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Consultation held in Surat by President Kerridge and Messrs. Wylde, Hoare, and Page, February</span> 20, 1626 (<em>Factory Records, Surat</em>, vol. i. p. 117).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">John Leachland having for some years past privately kept a woman of this country and refusing to put her away, in spite of all persuasions, it is debated whether to dismiss him from the Company’s service; but as this would only lead to his marrying her and forsaking his country and friends, it is resolved not to adopt <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-174" data-page="174" data-folio="120" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n173_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 120">[p. 120]</button> this extreme course, in the hope that time will reclaim him, 'being otherwise a man of fayre demeanor, sufficient abillities, and cleare of accounts with the Honorable Company in India.'<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-003" href="#sec-06-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> (½ <em>p.</em>)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Consultation held at Masulipatam by Edward Trafford, Thomas Dale, John Lawrence, and Philip Bearden</span>, <span class="sc">February</span> 23, 1626 (<em>Factory Records, Miscellaneous</em>, vol. ix. p. 153).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The intended journey of Mr. Trafford to Petapoli has been delayed 'by reason of the Kings death<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-004" href="#sec-06-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> and other casualties'. It is now decided that he shall depart forthwith in a native boat, accompanied by John Lawrence and Edward Powell, and shall take with him the goods allotted to that factory by Mr. Mills, viz. some sandal-wood and cloves, besides 2,500 rials of eight and four bags of 'plate tegalls'.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-005" href="#sec-06-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> (<em>Extract made in 1788. 2 pp.</em>)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Narrative by Thomas Mills and Thomas Johnson of their Settlement of a Factory at Armagon, March</span> 1, 1626 (<em>Ibid.</em>, p. 73).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-006" href="#sec-06-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Started from Masulipatam on February 1 and reached Armagon eleven days later. 'We found a most friendly welcome by all the inhabitants, who seemed overjoyed at our coming, especially those merchants of that place, who by their sundry letters unto us soliciting our settling a residence with them, and for our better encouragement procured a coule and letter of favour from the Naico or lord of those parts, which was sent us; who likewise importuned our plantation within his authority and we should find such entertainment as we desired.' The Governor sent notice of their arrival to the Nāyak, who was then, with at least 24,000 soldiers, besieging a fort<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-007" href="#sec-06-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> about twenty miles from 'his [? this] <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-175" data-page="175" data-folio="121" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n174_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 121">[p. 121]</button> place’; and the Nāyak at once dispatched a captain and 200 soldiers to conduct the English to his camp. The Dutch, hearing of their arrival, in all haste sent their ‘scrivan’ [writer: Port. <em>escrivão</em>], a Brahman, ‘with his pallankine, kimdeleroes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-008" href="#sec-06-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, and attendants compleat,’ to carry a present to the Nāyak and prevent the success of the English. The Brahman arrived a day before the latter, and, in addition to bribing thirty or forty of the principal natives, offered 4,000 pagodas to the Nāyak himself if he would refuse the English permission to settle in his territories; but he, ‘although young, of the age of 19 years, yet, as it seems, though a heathen, will maintain the reputation of his father, who [was?] by general report reputed very constant of his word and promise.’ ‘We were received with all friendly respect and lodged in a tent near him. And so the day following we had audience, to deliver him the intent of our coming and our present, which he entertained with all friendly acceptance, seeming to yield consent to our demands; and so, after some few compliments of courtesy, were dismissed of further parley, in regard of his other important affairs then in handling. Now the Dutch ambassador, or rather prating Bramine, employed his wits yet how he might circumvent us; and ceased not day or night to instigate those great ones against us, supplying them with feeling courtesies, in part of larger sums promised if his request took effect; which they, lawyerlike, for the covet of gain accordingly pleaded for in his behalf, as we had daily notice of what passed in their general assemblies, besides this man having private conference with the great man, by means of his scrivans of his own cast, importunately again and again urging the power of the Dutch, and how beneficial their abiding would be unto him, by reason of their great investments they meant to make yearly, of no less moment than fifty or sixty thousand pagodas, by which their great employments he might expect the benefit of four or five thousand pagodas per annum to come freely into his purse; besides, they would build a fort for the better guard and security of his port. And for us, the English, he reported to know our estate as well as ourselves; which was so <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-176" data-page="176" data-folio="122" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n175_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 122">[p. 122]</button> poor and bare that we were not able to perform what we pretended, and that we had not the means to employ above four or five thousand rials a year; and further that we meant not to keep here any settled residence, but would leave a sailor or two to manage that small business; and withal reported that we would have remained longer in Palicat but for want of means to pay our charges. And with such and worse terms did this Dutch ambassador disable and abuse us and our nation, and would have proceeded further with his forged matter but that the great man interrupted him, demanding the reason wherefore the Dutch, in all this time of their long residence so near his country, would not know him or make the least motion for what they now so importunately desired upon the coming of the English, and not before; to which the Bramine made reply that it was long since minded, but ignorantly omitted and deferred through their many employments; which frivolous answer was easily apprehended and their intent censured by the great man accordingly; and, notwithstanding all his forged projects and large proffers, for that time was dismissed and referred to their further consideration; all which was presently noted unto us’. On the English soliciting a decision, one way or the other, ‘the great man, assembling those of his council about him, sent for us; where we being come, they began to reiterate the large proffers; to which we made this answer, in fair and beseeming terms, without the least reproaching language, that we had not to do with them, nor to pattern of their actions or proceedings; though howsoever their rude tongues were guided with false reports of us, yet our discretion should moderate all their spleen with no other than friendly report of them; and for their large proffered offers, they concerned us not at all; but if he pleased to entertain our residence in his country, according to our motion made and his own promise performed in a letter sent us to Musulapatam, for which purpose we are now come, and because we would not further omit his so free a proffer we thus rawly undertook this journey in a boat; and if his pleasure now so minded to give us entertainment, we were readily prepared to embrace with all respect; if not, attended his answer to the contrary, that we might depart as freely as we came; which speech of ours was so well favoured by the great man that he presently granted our request, notwithstanding the main opposition of a many of those great ones <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-177" data-page="177" data-folio="123" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n176_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 123">[p. 123]</button> about him, which pleaded for the Dutch. And in the midst of our parley it was rumoured amongst them that the Braminy had further promised (which is very certain) two pieces of ordnance, with five candy of powder and as much lead; which bred a great dispute amongst them. Whereupon, being pressed unto it, we consented to give him a piece of ordnance, and so our request was in a great part confirmed under hand, though not so ample and freely as [we?] might expect, had not the Dutch so insolently opposed us; yet not to say but that we have procured sufficient for our quiet and free trading, without any allowance for the entrance of the Dutch to hinder us. And seeing their pretences could not prevail, they pretend revenge by seizing or forcing those junckes yearly bound to that port, with provisions of rice and such like, to go for Pallicat; which may prove a great hinderance to our trade, if not remedied’. Complain of the unreasonableness of the Dutch in thus seeking to exclude the English, though there is ample room for both in the country. A sloop has twice been sent from Pulicat to this place ‘to pry into our actions’. (16 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Commission from President Kerridge and Messrs. Wylde, Hoare, and Martin to Captains Weddell and Clevenger, March</span> 4, 1626 (<em>O.C.</em> 1218).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Weddell is to command the <em>Royal James</em> and <em>Jonas</em> during their voyage to England, and also the <em>Anne</em>, <em>Falcon</em>, and <em>Spy</em> while they remain in company; but Robert Young and William Hoare, the cape merchants of the <em>James</em> and <em>Jonas</em>, are exempted from his control, ‘in respect of their longe service and repute in the Companies affaires.’ As the Portuguese fleet is understood to be at ‘Bombayee’, great vigilance is enjoined against a possible attack. The <em>Anne</em> and <em>Falcon</em> are to be speeded to Batavia and Masulipatam respectively at the first opportunity. The <em>Spy</em> is to go to St. Augustine’s Bay and the Comoro Islands, to meet the fleet expected from England. After separation from these ships care must be taken to avoid surprise by pirates. The ships should keep together and appoint a rendezvous in case of separation. In the event of Weddell’s death, Clevenger is to take command, and Goodall to be master of the <em>Jonas</em>. Should Clevenger die, his successor is to be appointed by the Council, which is to consist <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-178" data-page="178" data-folio="124" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n177_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 124">[p. 124]</button> of Weddell, Clevenger, Young, Hoare, Goodall, Swanley, Eaton, and Wheatley; Man, Clement, Wills, Hawley, and Phelps to be also members, so long as they are in company. Weddell is to have a casting vote, as usual. (<em>Signed copy.</em> 3 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">INSTRUCTIONS FROM PRESIDENT KERRIDGE AND COUNCIL TO JOHN PHELPS, MASTER OF THE <em>SPY</em>, MARCH 4, 1626 (<em>O.C.</em> 1219).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To sail with Weddell's fleet until licensed to depart, and then to make his way to St. Augustine's Bay in Madagascar. Not finding the expected ships there, he is to leave letters, and proceed to the Comoro Islands to await them. On meeting them he is to put himself under the orders of the commander, and return with him to Surat. Two Dutchmen are sent on his ship with letters for their expected fleet. (<em>Signed copy.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">JOINT NOTIFICATION<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-009" href="#sec-06-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> TO DUTCH AND ENGLISH SHIPS MET BY THE <em>SPY</em> [MARCH, 1626] (<em>O.C.</em> 1220).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It has been agreed that all English and Dutch ships bound for India shall join together, and proceed to a spot about thirty-five leagues from the coast in latitude 18° North, and there ride from the 1st to the 20th October to await the ships expected from Batavia. (<em>Copy.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">COMMISSION FROM PRESIDENT KERRIDGE AND COUNCIL TO JOSEPH WILLS, MASTER OF THE <em>FALCON</em>, MARCH 4, 1626 (<em>O.C.</em> 1221).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">His ship is to proceed to Masulipatam, to embark the goods there provided, 'which by the <em>Roses</em> disaster remaine untransported.' To lose no time by touching anywhere on the way; but he may chase and capture any vessels belonging to Portuguese ports, or to Chaul or Dābhol (if unprovided with English passes). Any goods thus taken may be left at Masulipatam, if required by the factors there, but the prisoners are to be carried to Batavia. On arriving at Masulipatam he is to place himself at the disposal of the Agent; and similarly on reaching Batavia. Mr. Hawley, the merchant of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-179" data-page="179" data-folio="125" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n178_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 125">[p. 125]</button> the ship, is exempted from the master's control. The council is to consist of Wills, Hawley, the chief mate, and the purser. (<em>Signed copy. 1½ pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Commission from President Kerridge and Council to Eustace Man, Master of the</span> <em>Anne</em>, <span class="sc">March</span> 4, 1626 (<em>O.C.</em> 1222).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In similar terms to the foregoing. On arriving at his port [Batavia] the ship is to be refitted, and they advise him to cut two bow ports on the lower orlop to withstand assaults by frigates. The want of such ports was a main cause of the <em>Lion's</em> disaster. In case of Man's death, Darby is to succeed. The council is to consist of Man, Clement, Darby, and Thomas Joyce. (<em>Signed copy. 1½ pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Robert Fox's Account of the Homeward Voyage of the</span> <em>James</em> (<em>Marine Records</em>, vol. xxxix).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1626, <em>February</em> 8. Commenced to lade goods. <em>March</em> 6. Went out into the Road. <em>March</em> 31. The whole fleet (five English and ten Dutch) sailed together. <em>April</em> 8. The pinnace <em>Spy</em> parted company for the Comoro Islands, to meet the fleet from England. <em>April</em> 11. Anchored in the Road of 'Ewall', near 'Mount Dellee' [Mt. Delly, on the Malabar Coast]. <em>April</em> 13. Sailed again. <em>April</em> 16. The <em>Falcon</em> and <em>Anne</em> parted company, bound for Masulipatam and Batavia respectively. <em>April</em> 17. The three Dutch ships (<em>Heusden</em>, <em>Nieuw Bantam</em>, and a Portuguese prize) left for Batavia. <em>April</em> 21. Crossed the Line. <em>July</em> 7. Reached Table Bay, and found there a Dutch ship which had left Masulipatam four months ago<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-010" href="#sec-06-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. <em>July</em> 9. The <em>Exchange</em> and <em>Christopher</em> came in, bound for Batavia and Surat respectively. <em>July</em> 17. Sailed with the <em>Jonas</em> and the Dutch ship. <em>August</em> 6. Passed St. Helena. <em>October</em> 6. Spoke a Dutch man-of-war. (<em>The journal ends at sea on October 17.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-011" href="#sec-06-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> <em>17 pp.</em>)</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-180"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="180" data-folio="126" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n179_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 126">[p. 126]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Richard Monk’s Account of the Same Voyage</span> (<em>Marine Records</em>, vol. xl).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1626, <em>February</em> 8. They landed all the treasure, except one chest of rials reserved as ordered for Persia.
