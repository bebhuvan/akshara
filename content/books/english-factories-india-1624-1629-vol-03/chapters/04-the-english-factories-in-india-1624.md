James, Bangham, Martin, and Hoare, January</span> 7, 1624 (<em>Factory Records, Surat</em>, vol. i. p. 80).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Company having by their last letters empowered the Council to increase the wages of their servants in India, the following decisions are come to, subject to their confirmation at home : (1) Robert Tottle, who has served from 1614 and has received no wages until the last two years, is given 30<em>l.</em> a year for the future. (2) John Bangham, who has hitherto had no wages, is allowed 50<em>l.</em> per annum. (3) John Leachland is to have 60<em>l.</em> per annum. (4) Joseph Hopkinson, in view of his past services and his appointment as accountant, is to receive 100<em>l.</em> per annum in future. Examination having been made of the charges against certain sailors of pilfering from the natives, the question is adjourned until the return of the <em>Blessing</em>. (<em>Copy.</em> 2 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">John Facy, Purser’s Mate of the</span> <em>Reformation</em> [<span class="sc">at Swally</span>], <span class="sc">to the Governor of the Company, January</span> 8, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1140).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">His mate, Thomas Read, is dead, and has left his accounts in great confusion. Has done his best to put them straight, and now transmits a book of wills and inventories of deceased men, with the inventories of some who deserted at Ormus. The provisions brought from England have proved very good. Billings, the late purser of the <em>Whale</em>, has been appointed by the President and Council to be purser of the <em>Reformation</em>. Will write again from Bantam. (1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-56"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="56" data-folio="2" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n55_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 2">[p. 2]</button></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Consultation held in Batavia by President Brockedon and Council, January</span> 8, 1624 (<em>Factory Records, Java</em>, vol. iii. part ii. p. 127).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Whereas John Johnson, ‘late master in one of the Danes shipps,’ has given information regarding an opening for trade in the King of Tanjūr’s country, which may prove very beneficial, they have offered him 6<em>l.</em> per month to proceed in the ship intended thither. Refusing this, he is granted leave to go to England in the next shipping,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-001" href="#sec-04-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and is given 50 rials of eight towards his expenses here. (<em>Extract only.</em> ¼ <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Extract from a Dutch Letter from Ispahān, January</span> <sup>8</sup>/<sub>18</sub>, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1141).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-002" href="#sec-04-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Portuguese came by night with rowing vessels under Ormus Castle and set fire to the <em>Reformation</em> and two native ships. The latter were wholly burnt, but the flames in the <em>Reformation</em> were quenched with the aid of the Hollanders. The assailants were detected by the watch on the Dutch ships, who fired several shots at them. The <em>Reformation</em> was in great peril, having forty sick men on board. The Dutch lent sixteen men to carry her to Surat; otherwise she could not have sailed. (¾ <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">President Brockedon and Council at Batavia to the Company, January</span> 17, 1624 (<em>Factory Records, Java</em>, vol. iii. part ii. p. 214).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">They intend to send the <em>Hart</em> to the place the Danes have fortified on the Coast of Coromandel, where they understand that good cloths and paintings and store of pepper may be had. The prince of that country has sent a letter inviting them to trade in his dominions; and they are told that ‘the Danes in ten weeks time have laden three or four hundred tonnes of pepper, and that the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-57" data-page="57" data-folio="3" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n56_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 3">[p. 3]</button> Portugalls buy almost all their cloth in that country. Hee is accounted the most warlike prince in all those parts, an observer of justice, and will protect all those that trade in his country.’ The <em>Unity</em> is to go to Masulipatam with a suitable capital, but less than usual, in view of the venture to Tanjūr. (<em>Extract.</em> <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub> <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Henry Hawley at Batavia to Thomas Keightley</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-003" href="#sec-04-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> <span class="sc">in London, January</span> 17, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1145).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">They have received commendations of ‘Tanjoer’, as providing commodities ‘both better cheape and more plenty then at Masulapatan, more convenient for shipping, pepper and other comodities there growinge.’ Having seen samples of the cloth, they have decided to send a ship in the spring to plant a factory there. (<em>Extract.</em> <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub> <em>p. Much damaged.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">President Rastell at Surat to the King of Socotra, February</span> 9, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1148).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Received His Highness’s letters by his servants, ‘Sade Sheckaune’ [Saīd Shaikhān] and ‘Essuf Turcke’ [Yūsuf Turkī], whom Rastell has always assisted with his best furtherance. Thanks the King for his favour to the English. Has sent his letter [<em>see previous volume</em>, p. 343 <em>n.</em>] to King James. As regards his desire of assistance with ships and soldiers against his enemy the King of ‘Share’ [Shehr], the English are willing to do him service, but cannot this year, owing to want of vessels. Besides, so weighty a business requires consideration ‘and conditions on both sides’. Desires that he will not receive into his ships the goods or persons of those enemies of the English, ‘the Dabulliers and Chaulemen.’ (<em>Substance of a letter in Persian.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Pieter Van den Broeck, Dutch Chief at Surat, to the Company, February</span> 28, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1151).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Encloses a copy of a letter he has received from ‘Your Honors servaunts (our good frendes) in this place’. Will comply with the request, and do any other service in his power, consistent with his duty to his own employers. Wishes the writers had related their <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-58" data-page="58" data-folio="4" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n57_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 4">[p. 4]</button> particular grievances, but, as they are kept so close that communication is impossible, patience is the only course. <em>Annexed</em>:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Thomas Rastell, Giles James, and Joseph Hopkinson, at Surat, to P. Van den Broeck, February 28, 1624. As the ships are shortly expected from Persia, and some of the English, in ignorance of the factors' troubles, may land 'and so become lyable to our suffrings', they beg his friendly assistance in warning them, and also in conveying to them the letter enclosed. (<em>Attested copies.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-004" href="#sec-04-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> <em>In all</em> 2 <em>pp. An endorsement states that the original came from the Indies in June</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-005" href="#sec-04-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>, 1625, <em>and was kept back by the Dutch Company, who merely sent the present copies.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Consultation held in Batavia by President Brockedon and Council, March</span> 2, 1624 (<em>Factory Records, Java</em>, vol. iii. part ii. p. 128).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is decided to send the <em>Unity</em> to Masulipatam and the <em>Hart</em> to Tanjūr. Mr. Joseph Cockram (one of the Council) is to go in the latter as 'cheife director in the whole action', with George Brewen (who is to be left as principal in the new factory), John Cartwright, Richard Robinson, and Edward Powell. Edward Trafford and Thomas Johnson sent in the <em>Unity</em> to be second and third at Masulipatam. They are to take thither (besides goods) 20,000 rials in coin and Japan silver. The stock for Tanjūr is fixed at 52,000 rials, of which 40,500 is to be for pepper and the rest for calicoes. (<em>Extracts only.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">President Brockedon and Council at Batavia to the Factors at Masulipatam, March</span> 5, 1624 (<em>Ibid.</em>, p. 243).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Received their letters by the <em>Ruby</em> on November 22 last, and will answer them by the <em>Unity</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-006" href="#sec-04-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Their letters by the <em>Wapen van Rotterdam</em> have also come to hand. Complain that the latter are 'couched with such affectation that in many places wee are forced to guesse at your meaning, and in many places can finde no sence at all'; so beg that in future they will write 'in a more plaine and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-59" data-page="59" data-folio="5" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n58_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 5">[p. 5]</button> marchantlike stile and leave such affectate eloquence to more triviall occasions’. They are sorry for the wretched state of Francis Futter, though it seems to be due to his own folly; it would have been better for him to have made his peace with the Governor, even upon somewhat intolerable conditions, than ‘to have stood to triall before those prowd Moores of Golconda’. However, the factors should now endeavour to clear him if possible. They are likewise sorry to understand the injuries done by the natives to the Dutch Governor [Van Uffelen: <em>see previous volume</em>, pp. 315, 316], occasioned partly by ‘his proud and disdainefull demeanour towards them’. This should be a warning to the English lest they should be treated in like manner. The Dutch here complain, on the authority of letters from Signor Libenaer, that Mills ‘was the principall cause of all their troubles’, and that the Moors would not have dared to attempt such violence but for his instigation. They assert that during their Governor’s imprisonment Mills went divers times to the Dutch house and read certain letters from Golconda, ‘to terrify them and feele their resolution;’ that ‘you had offered 10,000 pagods to the Moores to drive the Dutch from thence, that wee might have the trade alone to ourselves; and finally that you held certaine conventions with the Governour, &amp;c., in our owne house, and gave to the value of 300 pagods to them in presents at the same time’. Cannot believe these accusations; but as they will doubtless be transmitted to Europe, it will be well for Mills to clear himself. They expressly forbid him ‘to use any violent courses or harsh demeanours towards the Dutch in this businesse, well knowing the many inconveniences that may grow by such harsh and uncivill proceedings’. The Dutch General here has promised to warn the English in time should he decide to make reprisals against Masulipatam; but he intends to avoid extreme measures if possible, ‘because it would be exceeding prejudiciall unto their affaires to loose the trade of that place.’ Still, it is necessary for the factors to watch closely the proceedings of the Dutch, in order that they may not suffer in any hostilities that may ensue. On February 22 the Dutch <em>Schoonhoven</em> and another pinnace arrived from Surat, and brought a short letter from President Rastell, announcing the capture of the junks and ‘a good and proffitable agreement to our masters <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-60" data-page="60" data-folio="6" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n59_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 6">[p. 6]</button> benifitt’ in consequence. He did not mention that any subjects of Golconda had been taken in the junks; so probably that is ‘a meere fable’. Three ships are to go home from Surat this year: the <em>Dolphin</em> in January, and the <em>Blessing</em> and <em>William</em> in March. The dispatch of the <em>Reformation</em> to Batavia with a good capital is also promised. Intend to send them by the <em>Unity</em> about 20,000 rials of eight, with some lead and porcelain; but have little or no spices to spare. Steel of the broad sort may be bought at Masulipatam for Batavia; but of ordinary steel there is at present a glut. The prices of other Coast goods have also fallen considerably. ‘The cloth that wee most desire from Musulpatam is long cloth, betillees white and redd of 40 covetts, salempoores and redd percallas.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-007" href="#sec-04-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Intended dispatch of the <em>Hart</em> to Tanjūr. Mr. Johnson affirms that he delivered to Mr. Mills a letter from the ‘Naick’ intended for the President and Council, ‘which wee admire you should keepe back, being it so much importeth our masters businesse, for wee know not what his promises were in the said letter nor what answeare to make him.’ Sent the <em>Exchange</em> and <em>Elizabeth</em> for England on December 15, and the <em>Anne</em> on February 25. (<em>Copy.</em> 3½ <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Captain John Hall, Captain Christopher Brown, Edward Heynes, Richard Lancaster, and Thomas Waller</span>, ABOARD THE <em>WILLIAM</em> AT THE BAR OF SURAT, TO THE DUTCH [AT SURAT?], MARCH 14, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1152).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">They know not why the Dutch protect these India junks, goods, and people, who have so manifestly dishonoured the King of England and wronged the English Company ‘by zeazing violently into their hands their whole estate and imprisoning their servants’. If this course be persisted in, they must protest against the Dutch and hold them responsible for all that happens. They disclaim any intention of infringing the Treaty of Defence or the instructions given by the two Companies to their servants, which (they must remind them) enjoin mutual help when wrongs are received by either from a foreign nation; but they assure them that the junks ‘shall not part from under our commaunds untill wee understand <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-61" data-page="61" data-folio="7" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n60_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 7">[p. 7]</button> better of the estate and condition of our President and Councell in Suratt, which by all circumstances wee may justlie suppose are both betrayed and inthrauled by your instigations and underhand dealing with our enemies these people'. <em>P.S.</em>—Have sent copies of this protest to 'your assistance [<em>sic</em>] and protectors of these juncks' that they may not plead ignorance should cause be found 'to oppose you in our rights'. (<em>Copy.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">President Brockedon and Council at Batavia to Thomas Staverton at Jambi, March</span> 21, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1154).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">. . . (¼ <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">The Same to the 'Nayge of Tanjuer', March</span> 27, 1624 (<em>O.C.</em> 1155).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-008" href="#sec-04-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">They trust that His Highness's letter to their agent in Masulipatam (lost by the latter's negligence) will excuse this abrupt coming to him before they could commend his gracious favours unto their own sovereign; and since they have learned by Mr. Johnson's relations his good inclination towards their nation, they have dispatched the bearer, Mr. Joseph Cockram, with the aforesaid Mr. Johnson, to offer their service in the accommodation of commerce, that 'yt may for ever remayne inviolablie a band of ametie between the howses of Tanjurr and Greate Brittaine'. They <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-62" data-page="62" data-folio="8" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n61_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 8">[p. 8]</button> do not doubt His Majesty's gracious favour for the grant of the necessary privileges, on which point the bearer is empowered to negotiate. (<em>Copy.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Commission from the President and Council at Batavia to William Langford, March 27, 1624</span> (<em>Factory Records, Java,</em> vol. iii. part ii. p. 260).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To proceed to 'Sericall'<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-009" href="#sec-04-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and Masulipatam with his ship the <em>Unity</em>, of 300 tons. At the latter port he is to stand carefully on his guard, and take his instructions from the merchants, in case of hostilities between the Dutch and the natives. Special attention is to be paid to the stowage of goods. He is to endeavour the capture of any Portuguese vessels he may meet. (<em>Copy.</em> 1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Instructions to Joseph Cockram and others for Settling a Factory in Tanjūr, March 27, 1624</span> (<em>Ibid.,</em> p. 265).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-010" href="#sec-04-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On arrival at 'Sericall' a 'caoul' [Arabic <em>qaul</em>, a safe-conduct] is to be procured from the Governor before landing. Mr. Johnson or some other should next be sent to the Nāyak to procure a farmān 'that you may come safely unto him; which being granted, you shall present him with our letters and such presents as wee have sent', and demand the grant of suitable privileges. The <em>Unity</em> is to be sent on to Masulipatam within five or six days of their arrival, with advice to Mr. Mills of the quantities, &amp;c., of cloth likely to be procured at the new settlement. No attempt should be made to build a factory house until they are satisfied that the trade is worth pursuing. 'The cheefest thing wee aime at is the procuring of large quantities of pepper; wherefore, if pepper be extraordinary deare, or but little to be gotten, wee hold it unfitting to remayne there, unlesse the place afford extraordinary good cloth and good cheape, and that there be hopes to vend quantities of our English commodities, which you are especially to inquire after.' To be cautious in landing goods until well assured of the Nāyak's affection; also in giving credit to the natives. 'In Musulpatam it is common to trust Committees [<em>see previous volume,</em> p. 135]; but in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-63" data-page="63" data-folio="9" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n62_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 9">[p. 9]</button> all the time of our trade wee have not had 300 rials bad debts. . . Wee are informed there is a great Commitie in the Naicks country named Malaya, which wee make account wilbe your cheef marchant and will undertake great matters. He shewed himself very honest in his dealings with Mr. Mills at Pallicatt; yet by dealing with inferiour Committies you shall better understand the markett and know how to bargaine with Malaya with more certainty.’ To be especially careful in choosing good colours, as there is much deceit in the reds (the most usual colour); also in seeing that the goods correspond with the samples. Packing should not be carried on in moist weather or while the cloths are still damp. They must look vigilantly to the delivery of the goods at the time appointed, and must have packing materials prepared in time. Should the Nāyak be very earnest for a piece of ordnance, they may spare him one of the ship’s guns, ‘with all his furniture.’ ‘Keepe good correspondence with the Danes and Portugalls, but trust them not; and have as little conversation with the Portugalls as may be, for they are exceeding treacherous and will poison you if you eat with them, as our people by wofull experience in Siam have made triall with the losse of their lives.’ Mr. Johnson may be left at the new factory or not, as may be found expedient; but he is not to be trusted with the Company’s cash. Two Portuguese women and [     ] men sent in this ship to be landed at Negapatam or S. Thomé. An endeavour should be made to procure the liberty of Mrs. Frobisher and her family in return.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-011" href="#sec-04-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> A letter to the Captain of S. Thomé on this subject is sent herewith. If Mrs. Frobisher is not there, the Captain should be urged to write to Goa and Malacca. Should it be decided to leave a factory, Mr. Brewen is to be chief, John Cartwright second, and Richard Robinson third, with four or five other Englishmen. If sufficient pepper be not obtained, the <em>Hart</em> should be dispatched to the west coast of Sumatra for a supply, and so to Batavia. While in port the sailors are to be allowed ‘four fresh meales the weeke, either fish or flesh . . . <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-64" data-page="64" data-folio="10" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n63_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 10">[p. 10]</button> and if butter be deare, let them have salt fish to eat with their rice’. ‘You may entreat the Naicks favour to bring from thence 15 bricklayers that have skill to lay plaister of pallist,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-012" href="#sec-04-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and 15 coulies to labour, which you shall buy, though they cost 20 rials per piece and upward. And lade as much plaister of pallist and tiles abord the <em>Hart</em> as shee can conveniently take in, for wee shall have great occasion to use them.’ Private trade is to be rigorously suppressed. <em>Annexed:</em> (i) List of goods understood to be made in the Nāyak’s country, with the quantities required : ‘serassas’, ‘tape chindes’, ‘dragams’, ‘chelas’, ‘caingouloons’, ‘samadramoores’, ‘rambuttans’, ‘balachus’, &amp;c.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-013" href="#sec-04-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>; (ii) List of goods appointed from Masulipatam, consisting of piece-goods, butter, wheat, and hair. (<em>Copy. 5 pp.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">President Brockedon and Council at Batavia to the Captain of S. Thomé, March</span> 27, 1624 (<em>Factory Records, Java</em>, vol. iii. part ii. p. 272).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Send two gentlewomen, who (with many other Portuguese) were captured at Mozambique in 1622 [<em>see previous volume</em>, p. 155]. They would have been restored earlier, but first the Dutch objected and then no opportunity of passage could be found. Would be glad if he would in return set at liberty any English prisoners in his power. (<em>Copy. ½ p.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Instructions to Captain John Bickley for the Voyage of the <em>Hart</em>, March</span> 27, 1624 (<em>Ibid.</em>, p. 273).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-014" href="#sec-04-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To stop at the Islands of Sunda, to cut wood for the intended factory at ‘Sericall’. He therefore proposes the consideration of the question whether, in case they be not allowed to lade at least one of the ships with indigo, they should both attend the Company’s affairs in other ports, or whether <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-74" data-page="74" data-folio="20" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories03fost/page/n73_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 20">[p. 20]</button> they should endeavour to effect the release of the factors here by seizing the Gujarātī ships on their return. These considerations being debated, it is agreed that although there would be a great probability of torture and even death in such an event, yet the factors are willing to risk it, especially as there will be many prisoners taken in the junks who may be held as hostages. Moreover, some such action is thought to be the only means of effecting the release of themselves and their estates. As regards the Dutch, the natives understand that the two nations are distinct: and in any case the present necessity is too urgent ‘to bee soe tenderlie fearefull of the Dutches distastes’: besides, there is reason to think that the troubles are mainly due to ‘their instigacions and abetting’, as witness their recent defence of the junks by hoisting their colours on them. As for the presumed poverty of the junks, it need not be doubted they will be rich enough to countervail the English goods ashore, (‘beeing per estimate about 26 or 27 thowsand pounds starling at most’), for often one ship from Jiddah is worth more in treasure alone; while as for the Dutch ‘wee are noething interessed’.
