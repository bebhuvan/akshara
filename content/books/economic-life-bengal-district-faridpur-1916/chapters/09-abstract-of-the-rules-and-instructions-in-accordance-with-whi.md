<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-142"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="142" data-folio="138" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n141_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 138">[p. 138]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">I. RULES</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Forms nos.</span> 180 and 181 are the same form with headings suitable for the different classes, no. 180 for the cultivating classes and no. 181 for the non-cultivating classes. Every family inhabiting a homestead in the village and with a separate ‘mess’ will be shown separately in one or other of these forms, but not in both. It is necessary to understand clearly the difference between the two parts. In no. 180 will be included:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(1) All who cultivate land themselves or by their family or servants and whose main dependence is upon the produce of the land.</li><li>(2) All who live by agricultural labour.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In no. 181 will be included all who live by service or industry or on the rents of tenants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Note</span> 1.—The widow of a cultivator, who has leased out her lands on a produce rent during the minority of her children, will be included in no. 180.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Note</span> 2.—A cultivator who is also a village watchman or a messenger of his landlord will be shown in no. 180. A cultivating fisherman or weaver will be included in no. 180 or no. 181 according as the bulk of his income is derived from the land or from fishing and weaving.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Note</span> 3.—A petty landlord of the respectable classes who subsists on the produce rents of his tenants will be shown in no. 181.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-143"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="143" data-folio="139" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n142_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 139">[p. 139]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The following instructions in the filling up of the two forms should be carefully read:</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">No. 180. <em>Cultivating classes.</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">If two families live in the same homestead but in separate ‘mess’ (i.e. taking their meals apart), there should be two separate entries with sub-numbers.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Population</em> (cols. 2–6).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These must be accurate, showing the numbers of the family at home. Members employed elsewhere and not usually living at home should not be included, but temporary absentees should.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Religion and caste</em> (col. 7).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Write ‘Christian’ or Muhammadan, but instead of Hindu write the caste, ‘Brahmin,’ ‘Kayastha,’ &amp;c.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Sale of crops</em> (col. 8).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A cultivator keeps a large amount of his food crops for the consumption of the family during the year and sells the remainder. The amount which he obtains annually by such sales only should be shown here. Where land is held at a produce rent, the landlord’s share of the crop should be excluded. The proceeds of the sale of all crops, rice, jute, sugar-cane, thatching grass, reeds, tobacco, &amp;c., should be totalled and entered here.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Sale of bye-products</em> (col. 9).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Bye-products are chiefly straw and paddy-huskings. The amount obtained by sale of these should be shown here. Local markets should be watched to see if sale of straw and huskings is prevalent.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Sale of dairy produce</em> (col. 10).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Milk, eggs, butter, cheese, &amp;c.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Sale of garden produce</em> (col. 11).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Receipts from all kinds of vegetables together with the produce of fruit-bearing trees as shown in cols. 10–17 of the Homestead Statement and the value of bamboos annually sold should be added together and shown here. It is clear that where there is a large garden, there will usually be a corresponding entry in this column.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-144"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="144" data-folio="140" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n143_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 140">[p. 140]</button></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Sale of live-stock</em> (col. 12).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Calves, foals, fowls, chickens, ducks, pigs, sheep, goats. The average annual amount obtained by sale of these should be shown here.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Rent of under-tenants</em> (col. 13).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">When a cultivator sublets some of his land for a money rent, the amount will be entered here. If he sublets it at a produce rent and actually takes his share of the crop, it will not be entered, as he has either retained the crop for the subsistence of his family or has sold it, when it is included in col. 8. If, however, the crop is jute or a similar crop, the landlord’s share is often sold or valued, and the tenant hands over not the produce but the money equivalent. In such cases the money equivalent will be entered here.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Sale of fish</em> (col. 14).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Members of a cultivator’s family often supplement their earnings by catching fish in a small way. (Fishermen of the fishing castes who live by fishing will be entered in no. 181.)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Carting</em> (col. 15).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Where there are roads, cultivators may earn large sums annually by plying their carts, cattle, or pack-ponies for hire. An adequate allowance for fodder should be deducted and only the profit shown here.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Hire of cattle</em> (col. 16).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Cultivators with cattle to spare let them out to less fortunate men for ploughing, carting, &amp;c.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Agricultural labour</em> (cols. 17 and 18).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Where one or more male adults in the same ‘mess’ labour on hire in the fields of others, their numbers will be entered in col. 17. If they receive a share of the crop as payment for their labour, the word ‘kind’ only will be entered in col. 18; if they receive cash, the annual amount earned by all. If they go to other districts to labour, the name of the district should be noted. Where labour is merely exchanged, there will be no entry. Agricultural labour may be either <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-145" data-page="145" data-folio="141" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n144_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 141">[p. 141]</button> ploughing, sowing, weeding, or reaping, sugar pressing or steeping jute, &amp;c.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Other kinds of unskilled labour</em> (cols. 19 and 20).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This refers to labour on roads, or as a porter (coolie), bricklayer, &amp;c.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Industries and service</em> (cols. 21–3).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Certain small industries, such as paddy-husking or matweaving, are carried on by men and women. In such cases enter ‘paddy-husking’, &amp;c., in col. 21 and the receipts in col. 23. Enter similarly the nature and profits of trading; probably petty moneylending will be the commonest case.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Service is more frequent. Thus the cultivator, his brothers, or his sons may serve as messengers of the landlord or the State, as village watchmen, as watchmen of shopkeepers and moneylenders, or as servants, or their womenkind may serve as maid-servants of the respectable classes. Enter in col. 21 ‘watchman’, ‘servant’, ‘maid-servant’, &amp;c., and the total amount of annual earnings in col. 22 or 23, as the case may be. If they get food as well as money wages enter ‘Rs. 16 and food’, &amp;c.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Contributions of absent earning members</em> (cols. 24 and 25).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These columns deal with servants of the State, the railways, steamers, or private persons, who live away from their homes but maintain the family at home by remitting monthly contributions. Col. 25 will show the total amount sent home annually. If it varies, the amount sent home last year will be shown. If the absentee is in another part of the district, the fact is important and should be shown by noting (D.) in addition.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Purchase of rice</em> (col. 26).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This must be filled up after careful enquiry, as it is an important entry. Cultivators whose land is sown chiefly with jute and sugar-cane very often require to buy paddy (rice). In such cases enter here the annual amount bought in maunds. If it varies from year to year, give the figures of a normal <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-146" data-page="146" data-folio="142" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n145_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 142">[p. 142]</button> year and not the figures of a bumper year or a year of distress.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Village taxation (col. 27).</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Enter here the actual total chokidari tax paid last year. The assessment should not be consulted, but the amount actually paid. Note that quarterly or monthly figures will not be given, but the total of the year.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Debt (col. 28).</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In this column the total debt covered by written bonds will be entered. This will be found out by enquiry.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Classification (col. 29).</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The circle officer will enter in this column against each family S. (starvation), A. S. (above starvation), B. C. (below comfort), or C. (comfort) after consultation with the Kanungo. See instructions to officers. This column should be carefully filled up.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">No. 181. <em>Non-cultivating classes.</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Cols. 1–7. As in no. 180 care should be taken to include all inmates of the homestead, where there is a large Hindu family on the joint-family system.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Produce of homestead (col. 8).</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Very often there is a little land attached to the homesteads of non-cultivators, e.g. weavers, fishermen, and so on, the produce of which should be entered here. Some respectable people cultivate a large amount of land by means of servants.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Rent (cols. 9 and 10).</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">When rent is paid in kind by the tenants it will be shown in col. 9. The party’s statement as to the amount should be accepted. When rent is paid in money it will be shown in col. 10.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In both cols. 8 and 9 paddy (unhusked rice) will be shown in maunds, and all other crops, e.g. jute, in money, i.e. the money equivalent of the landlord’s share.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-147"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="147" data-folio="143" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n146_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 143">[p. 143]</button></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Trading profits</em> (cols. 11 and 12).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The nature of the trade will be entered in col. 11 and the annual profit in col. 12. Where traders refuse to give any information, this column may be filled up by consulting neighbours. This column also includes the important item of moneylending. Officers should be careful to see that the amount of indebtedness shown in col. 29 of no. 180, and col. 22 of no. 181, in every considerable area should agree with the amount of moneylending, although of course there may be no balance in individual villages. The profit of traders may be calculated at 20 per cent. on the annual turnover in the default of any trustworthy accounts, the profit of moneylending at the rates of interest found prevalent in the village.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Earnings in industries</em> (cols. 13 and 14).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">All industries, whether skilled or unskilled, which may be found in the village should be entered here. Thus, weavers, fishermen, sugar millers, carpenters, boat builders, blacksmiths, &amp;c. Labour, whether on earthwork or of any other kind, should be entered in these columns. Professional begging may be regarded as an industry for the purposes of this form. In col. 14 the profit should be arrived at by deducting the annual expenses of machinery and raw material from the total income.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Domestic or menial service</em> (cols. 15 and 16).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In these columns such menial service as that of barbers, washermen, palki bearers will be included together with all guards, messengers, watchmen, and servants who are not cultivators.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Professions and clerical labour</em> (cols. 17 and 18).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These are columns for the respectable classes. Here include all clerks, agents of landlords, &amp;c., lawyers, village touts, doctors, priests, &amp;c. In col. 18 the actual earnings and not the nominal salary should be shown; thus, a Naib’s pay may be three rupees a month, but his earnings over a hundred. An estimate should in all cases be made of the real earnings.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-148"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="148" data-folio="144" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/economiclifeofbe00jackuoft/page/n147_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 144">[p. 144]</button></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Contributions of absent earning members</em> (cols. 19 and 20).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Absent earning members in poor families may be messengers, guards, servants, and the like; and amongst the respectable classes, lawyers, clerks, doctors, &amp;c. Col. 20 should show the amount they sent home as a contribution to the family expenses last year. Here, if the absent earning member be employed in this district, note (D.) in col. 19 in addition.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Cols. 17 to 20 should be filled up with special care in dealing with a large joint-family.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Village taxation</em> (col. 21).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The actual annual chokidari tax paid should be noted. Quarterly or monthly figures will not be given.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Debt</em> (col. 22).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The total debt covered by written bonds will be entered.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Classification</em> (col. 23).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Circle Officer will fill this column after consultation with his Kanungos by entering ‘S.’, ‘A. S.’, ‘B. C.’ or ‘C.’ See instructions to officers.</p></div>
