HINDU PERIOD.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">PART I.</h3><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Indications of Maritime Activity in Indian Literature and Art.</strong></h3><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-53"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="53" data-folio="19" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n52_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 19">[p. 19]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">BOOK I.—PART I.</h2><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER I.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Direct Evidences from Sanskrit and Pali Literature.</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">It</span> has been already pointed out that though Sanskrit and Pali literature abounds in references to the trading voyages of Indians, they unfortunately furnish but few references having a direct bearing on the ships and shipbuilding of India which enabled her to keep up her international connections. I have, however, been able to find one Sanskrit work,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-001" href="#sec-09-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> which is something like a treatise on the art of shipbuilding in ancient India, setting forth many interesting details about the various sizes and kinds of ships, the materials out of which they were built, and the like; and it sums up in a condensed form all the available information and knowledge about that truly ancient industry of India. The book requires a full notice, and its contents have to be explained.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-54"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="54" data-folio="20" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n53_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 20">[p. 20]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The ancient shipbuilders had a good knowledge of the materials as well as the varieties and properties of wood which went to the making of ships. According to the <em>Vṛiksha-Āyurveda</em>, or the Science of Plant Life (Botany), four different kinds of wood<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-002" href="#sec-09-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> are to be distinguished: the first or the Brahman class comprises wood that is light and soft and can be easily joined to any other kind of wood; the second or the Kshatriya class of wood is light and hard but cannot be joined on to other classes; the wood that is soft and heavy belongs to the third or Vaisya class; while the fourth or the Sudra class of wood is characterized by both hardness and heaviness. There may also be distinguished wood of the mixed (Dvijāti) class, in which are blended properties of two separate classes.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">According to Bhoja, an earlier authority on shipbuilding, a ship built of the Kshatriya class of wood brings wealth and happiness.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-003" href="#sec-09-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> It is these ships that are to be used as means of communication where the communication is difficult owing to vast water.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-004" href="#sec-09-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-55" data-page="55" data-folio="21" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n54_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 21">[p. 21]</button> Ships, on the other hand, which are made of timbers of different classes possessing contrary properties are of no good and not at all comfortable. They do not last for a long time, they soon rot in water, and they are liable to split at the slightest shock and to sink down.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-005" href="#sec-09-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Besides pointing out the class of wood which is best for ships, Bhoja also lays down a very important direction for shipbuilders in the nature of a warning which is worth carefully noting.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-006" href="#sec-09-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> He says that care should be taken that no iron is used in holding or joining together the planks of bottoms intended to be sea-going vessels, for the iron will inevitably expose them to the influence of magnetic rocks in the sea, or bring them within a magnetic field and so lead them to risks. Hence the planks of bottoms are to be fitted together or mortised by means of substances other than iron. This rather quaint direction was perhaps necessary in an age when Indian ships plied in deep waters on the main.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Besides Bhoja’s classification of the kinds of wood used in making ships and boats, the <em>Yuktikalpataru</em> gives an elaborate classification of the ships themselves, based on their size. Four kinds of metal are recommended for decorative purposes, viz. gold, silver, copper, and the compound of all three. Four kinds of colours are recommended respectively for four kinds of vessels: a vessel with four masts is to be painted white, that with three masts to be painted red, that with two masts is to be a yellow ship, and the one-masted ship must be painted blue. The prows of ships admit of a great variety of fanciful shapes or forms: these comprise the heads of lion, buffalo, serpent, elephant, tiger, birds such as the duck, peahen or parrot, the frog, and man, thus arguing a great development of the art of the carpenter or the sculptor. Other elements of decoration are pearls and garlands of gold to be attached to and hung from the beautifully shaped prows.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-014" href="#sec-09-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-60"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="60" data-folio="26" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n59_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 26">[p. 26]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are also given interesting details about the cabins of ships. Three classes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-015" href="#sec-09-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> of ships are distinguished according to the length and position of their cabins. There are firstly the Sarbamandirā (सर्व्वमन्दिरा) vessels, which have the largest cabins extending from one end of the ship to the other.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-016" href="#sec-09-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> These ships are used for the transport of royal treasure, horses, and women.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-017" href="#sec-09-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Secondly, there are the Madhyamandirā (मध्यमन्दिरा) vessels,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-018" href="#sec-09-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> which have their cabins just in the middle part. These vessels are used in pleasure trips by kings, and they are also suited for the rainy season. Thirdly, ships may have their cabins towards their prows, in which case they will be called Agramandirā<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-019" href="#sec-09-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> (अग्रमन्दिरा). These ships are used in the dry season after the rains have ceased. They are eminently suited for long voyages and also to be <em>used in naval warfare.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-020" href="#sec-09-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-020" And, further, it was in these ships that the Bengalis once made a stand against the invincible prowess of Raghu as described in Kālidāsa’s <em>Raghuvańsa</em>, who retired after planting the pillars of his victory on the isles of the holy Ganges.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-024" href="#sec-09-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The conclusions as to ancient Indian ships and shipping suggested by these evidences from Sanskrit literature directly bearing on them are also confirmed by similar evidences culled from the Pali literature. The Pali literature, like the Sanskrit, also abounds with allusions to sea voyages and sea-borne trade, and it would appear that the ships employed for these purposes were of quite a large size. Though indeed the Pali texts do not usually give the actual measurements of the different dimensions of ships such as the Sanskrit texts furnish, still they make definite mention of the number of passengers which the ships carried, and thus enable us in another very conclusive way to have a precise idea of their size. Thus, according to the <em>Rājavalliya</em>, the ship in which Prince Vijaya and his followers were sent away by King Sińhaba (Sińhavāhu) of Bengal was so large as to accommodate full seven hundred passengers, all <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-63" data-page="63" data-folio="29" id="ref-sec-09-fn-025" href="#sec-09-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Their wives and children, making up more than seven hundred, were also cast adrift in similar ships.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-026" href="#sec-09-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The ship in which the lion-prince, Sińhala, sailed from some unknown part of Jambudvīpa to Ceylon contained five hundred merchants besides himself.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-027" href="#sec-09-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The ship in which Vijaya’s Pandyan bride was brought over to Ceylon was also of a very large size, for she is said to have carried no less than 800 passengers on board.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-028" href="#sec-09-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The <em>Janaka-Jātaka</em> mentions a ship that was wrecked with all its crew and passengers to the favourite number of seven hundred, in addition to Buddha himself in an earlier incarnation.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-029" href="#sec-09-fn-029"
