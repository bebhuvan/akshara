<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-152"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="152" data-folio="100" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n151_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 100">[p. 100]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER II.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">The Maurya Period.</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">We</span> now reach the age of the Mauryas, which may be taken roughly to begin from the date of Alexander’s Indian campaigns, about 325 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> In the accounts of these campaigns by Greek writers like Arrian, Curtius, and others, interesting light is sometimes thrown on the economic life of the period. Thus it may be stated with certainty that shipbuilding was in those very ancient days (so far back as 325 <span class="sc">b.c.</span>) a very flourishing industry giving employment to many, and the stimulus to its development must have come from the demands of both river and ocean traffic. Alexander’s passage of the Indus was effected by means of boats<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-001" href="#sec-13-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> supplied by native craftsmen. A flotilla of boats was also used in bridging the difficult river of the Hydaspes.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-002" href="#sec-13-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> For purposes of the famous voyage of Nearchus<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-003" href="#sec-13-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> down the rivers and to the Persian Gulf, all available country boats were impressed for the service, and a stupendous fleet was formed, numbering, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-153" data-page="153" data-folio="101" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n152_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 101">[p. 101]</button> according to Arrian,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-004" href="#sec-13-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> about 800 vessels, according to Curtius and Diodorus about 1,000 vessels, but according to the “more reliable estimate of Ptolemy” nearly 2,000 vessels, which between them accommodated 8,000 troops, several thousand horses, and vast quantities of supplies. It was indeed an extraordinarily huge fleet, built entirely of Indian wood by the hands of Indian craftsmen. In this connection the remarks made by the two great authorities on the history of ancient Oriental commerce, namely Dr. Vincent and Dr. Robertson, are of considerable interest. Says Dr. Vincent:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The <em>Ayeen Akbari</em> reckons the Panje-ab as the third province of the Mogul Empire, and mentions 40,000 vessels employed in the commerce of the Indus. It was this commerce that furnished Alexander with the means of seizing, building, hiring, or purchasing the fleet with which he fell down the stream; and when we reflect that his army consisted of 124,000 men, with the whole country at his command, and that a considerable portion of these had been left at the Hydaspes during the interval that the main body advanced to the Hyphasis and returned to the Hydaspes again, we shall have no reason to accuse Arrian of exaggeration when he asserts that the fleet consisted of 800 vessels of which 30 only were ships of war and the rest such as were usually employed in the navigation of the river. . . . Strabo mentions the proximity of Emodus, which afforded plenty of fir, pine, cedar, and other timber; and Arrian informs us that Alexander in the country of the Assaconi, and before he reached the Indus, had already built vessels which he sent down the Koppenes to Taxila. All these circumstances contribute to prove the reality of a fact highly controverted; and even though we were to extend the whole number of the fleet, comprehending tenders and boats, with some authors to 2,000, there is no improbability sufficient to excite astonishment.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-005" href="#sec-13-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-154"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="154" data-folio="102" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n153_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 102">[p. 102]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Dr. Robertson also expresses the same opinion:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>That a fleet so numerous should have been collected in so short a time is apt to appear at first sight incredible. But as the Punjab country is full of navigable rivers, on which all the intercourse among the natives was carried on, it abounded with vessels ready constructed to the conqueror’s hands, so that he might easily collect that number. If we could give credit to the account of the invasion of India by Semiramis, no fewer than 4,000 vessels were assembled in the Indus to oppose her fleet (<em>Diod. Sicul.</em>, lib. ii., cap. 74). It is remarkable that when Mahmud of Ghazni invaded India, a fleet was collected on the Indus to oppose his, consisting of the same number of vessels. We learn from the <em>Ayeen Akbari</em> that the inhabitants of this part of India still continue to carry on all their communication with each other by water; the inhabitants of the Circar of Tatta alone (in Sindh) have not less than 40,000 vessels of various construction.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-006" href="#sec-13-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Further, we have the actual mention made by Arrian of the construction of dockyards, and the supply by the tribe called Xathroi of galleys of thirty oars and transport vessels which were all built by them.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-007" href="#sec-13-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">All this clearly indicates that in the age of the Mauryas shipbuilding in India was a regular and flourishing industry of which the output was quite large. The industry was, however, in the hands of the State and was a Government monopoly; for, as Megasthenes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-008" href="#sec-13-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> informs us, while noticing the existence of a class of shipbuilders among the artisans, they were salaried public servants and were not permitted to work for any private person. These <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-155" data-page="155" data-folio="103" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n154_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 103">[p. 103]</button> ships built in the royal ship-yards were, however, as Strabo<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-009" href="#sec-13-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> informs us, let out on hire both to those who undertook voyages and to professional merchants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A few more interesting details regarding the shipping and navigation of the period are given by Pliny<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-010" href="#sec-13-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> in his description of Taprobane (Ceylon): “The sea between the island of Ceylon and India is full of shallows not more than six paces in depth, but in some channels so deep that no anchors can find the bottom. For this reason ships are built with prows at each end, for turning about in channels of extreme narrowness. In making sea voyages the Taprobane mariners make no observations of the stars, and indeed the Greater Bear is not visible to them, but they take birds out to sea with them which they let loose from time to time and follow the direction of their flight as they make for land.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-011" href="#sec-13-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Pliny also indicates the tonnage of these ancient Indian vessels, which is said to be 3,000 amphorae, the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-156" data-page="156" data-folio="104" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n155_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 104">[p. 104]</button> amphora being regarded as weighing about a fortieth of a ton.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-012" href="#sec-13-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The development of this national shipping made possible and necessary the creation and organization of a Board of Admiralty <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-013" href="#sec-13-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> as one of the six Boards which made up the War Office of Emperor Chandra Gupta (321 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> to 297 <span class="sc">b.c.</span>), “one of the greatest and most successful kings known to history.” Fortunately, for information regarding this Board of Admiralty and the Naval Department we can depend not only on foreign notices like those of Megasthenes and Strabo, but also on the much more elaborate and reliable account given in the invaluable Sanskrit work of the period, the <em>Arthaśāstra</em> of Kautilya, which is undoubtedly one of the most important landmarks not only in the literary history of India but also in the history of Indian civilization itself. The book <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-014" href="#sec-13-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> requires to be thoroughly studied, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-157" data-page="157" data-folio="105" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n156_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 105">[p. 105]</button> being a unique production of its kind in the entire Sanskrit literature, and a most valuable historical document, conveying as it does a perfectly complete picture of the extraordinarily rich and varied civilization that was developed in Maurya India over 2,000 years ago. I have, therefore, no hesitation in drawing largely upon the contents of this remarkable work of Chāṇakya and placing before the reader all such passages as tend to throw any light on the condition of the national shipping, navigation, and sea-borne trade of India in the glorious age of the Mauryas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Naval Department seems to have been very well organized. At its head was placed an officer who was called the नावध्यक्ष or the Superintendent of Ships.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-015" href="#sec-13-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> He was entrusted with the duty of dealing with all matters relating to navigation, including not only <em>navigation of the oceans</em>, but also inland navigation on rivers and lakes, natural or artificial.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-016" href="#sec-13-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The matters relating to navigation were of course manifold. The Superintendent of Ships seems to have been something like a modern Port Commissioner, and his first duty was to see that all the dues of his port were paid, and not one evaded.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-158"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="158" data-folio="106" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n157_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 106">[p. 106]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The kind and degree of the maritime activity of the period will be evident from the various kinds of port-taxes that were levied. Thus villages on seashores or on the banks of rivers and lakes had to pay regularly a fixed amount of tax.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-017" href="#sec-13-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Fishermen had to yield one-sixth of their haul as fees for fishing license.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-018" href="#sec-13-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Merchants also had to pay the customary tax levied in port towns.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-019" href="#sec-13-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Passengers arriving on board the State or the king’s ship had to pay the fixed and requisite amount of sailing fees.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-020" href="#sec-13-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> State boats were also let out to those who wanted to use them for pearl-fishery or for fishing for conch shells, and they had to pay the required amount of hire<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-021" href="#sec-13-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup>; but they were also free to use their own boats for the purpose.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-022" href="#sec-13-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> Besides these taxes payable to the Port Commissioner, there were the various sorts of ferry fees, which are also very interesting and equally indicative of a brisk trade and a throbbing commercial life. A man with a minor quadruped carrying some load had to pay a ferry <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-159" data-page="159" data-folio="107" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n158_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 107">[p. 107]</button> fee of one <em>masha</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-023" href="#sec-13-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>  A load carried on the head, a load carried on the shoulders, a cow, and a horse had each to pay two <em>mashas</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-024" href="#sec-13-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>  Four <em>mashas</em> were demanded for each camel or buffalo that was transported across the river.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-025" href="#sec-13-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>  Five <em>mashas</em> were levied for a small cart, six <em>mashas</em> for a cart of medium size that was drawn by bulls, and seven <em>mashas</em> for a big cart.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-026" href="#sec-13-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup>  Four <em>mashas</em> had to be paid for a load of merchandise whether for sale or not.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-027" href="#sec-13-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup>  Again, for big rivers involving greater risks, double the ferry fees above mentioned were charged.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-028" href="#sec-13-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup>  Thus conveyances and beasts of burden as well as loads of merchandise were subject to ferry fees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But besides seeing to the realization and collection of all proper taxes and dues, the Superintendent of Ships was also entrusted with the duty of enforcing many humane harbour regulations.  Thus whenever any weather-beaten, tempest-tossed ship arrived at his port, his first duty was to lend her the protecting hand of a father.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-029" href="#sec-13-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup>  He was also <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-160" data-page="160" data-folio="108" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n159_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 108">[p. 108]</button> empowered to exempt from toll any ship laden with merchandise that was damaged and spoiled by water, or to charge only half the due toll, and then allow it to sail when the proper time for setting sail approached.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-030" href="#sec-13-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Again, whenever a ship laden with merchandise foundered owing to want of hands or on account of ill-repair, it was the duty of the Superintendent of Ships to make good the loss of merchandise in part or full, as the case might be, because presumably the loss was due not to any fault of the merchants but to defects in the State vessel, and therefore must be made good from State funds.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-031" href="#sec-13-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But besides relieving ships in distress the Superintendent had to adopt many preventive measures to ensure safety. Thus during the period from the 7th day of Ashadha till the month of Kartika, i.e. when rivers are swollen owing to rains, the crossing of rivers by State or licensed ferries was strictly enforced.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-032" href="#sec-13-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Again, in those large rivers which cannot be forded during either the winter or summer seasons the Superintendent of Ships had to see that large and perfectly safe vessels were launched, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-161" data-page="161" data-folio="109" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n160_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 109">[p. 109]</button> manned with all necessary officers and hands, viz. a captain, a steersman, and a number of servants who would hold the oars and the ropes and bale out water.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-033" href="#sec-13-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Small boats were launched only in small rivers that overflowed during the rainy seasons.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-034" href="#sec-13-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To ensure safety there were also in force many strict regulations regarding the fording or crossing of rivers. Fording or crossing of rivers without permission was prohibited in order to ensure that no traitor or enemy could escape.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-035" href="#sec-13-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The time and even the place for fording and crossing rivers were definitely fixed, so that any person fording and crossing outside the proper place and in unusual times was punished with first amercement;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-036" href="#sec-13-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> and the man who forded or crossed a river at the usual place and time but without permission had to pay a fine of 26¾ <em>panas</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-037" href="#sec-13-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> Exceptions to this stringent rule were, however, allowed in the interests of trade and public good. Thus the following<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-038" href="#sec-13-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> were <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-162" data-page="162" data-folio="110" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n161_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 110">[p. 110]</button> freely allowed to cross rivers at any time and place:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(1) Fishermen, whose business would be seriously hampered by the above regulations.</li><li>(2) Carriers of firewood, grass, flowers and fruits; gardeners and vegetable dealers who had to go far and wide to find the things they dealt in.</li><li>(3) Persons pursuing suspected criminals.</li><li>(4) Messengers following other messengers going in advance.</li><li>(5) Servants engaged to carry things (provisions and orders) to the army.</li><li>(6) Persons using their own ferries; and</li><li>(7) Dealers supplying villages of marshy districts with seeds, necessaries of life, commodities, and other accessory things. Again, Brahmans, ascetics, children, the aged and afflicted, royal messengers, and pregnant women had all to be provided by the Superintendent with free passes to cross rivers.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-039" href="#sec-13-fn-039" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-039" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> There was also another regulation permitting foreign merchants who had often been visiting the country, as also those who were well known to local merchants, to land freely in port towns.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-040" href="#sec-13-fn-040" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-040" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Lastly, the Superintendent of Ships was also entrusted with the duty of punishing all violations <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-163" data-page="163" data-folio="111" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n162_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 111">[p. 111]</button> of harbour regulations, and miscreants that were dangerous to public peace. Thus to destruction were doomed the ships of pirates, the ships which were bound for the enemy’s country, and the ships that violated the customs and rules in force in port towns.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-041" href="#sec-13-fn-041" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-041" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The Superintendent had also to arrest persons of the following descriptions<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-042" href="#sec-13-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>: Any person who eloped with the wife or daughter of another; one who carried off the wealth of another; a suspected person; one having a perturbed appearance; one who had no baggage; one who attempted to conceal or evade the cognisance of a valuable load in his hand; one who had just put on a different garb; one who had just turned out an ascetic; one who pretended to be suffering from a disease; one who seemed to be alarmed; a person stealthily carrying valuable things; a person going on a secret mission; a person carrying weapons or explosives or holding poison in his hand; and lastly, one who came from a long distance without a pass. The Superintendent finally was to direct the confiscation of the commodities of those who were found to travel without a pass and of those also who with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-164" data-page="164" data-folio="112" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n163_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 112">[p. 112]</button> a heavy load forded a river at an unusual place and time.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-043" href="#sec-13-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We now have some idea of the organization of the Naval Department, the development of the national shipping, and the abounding commercial life in the India of the Mauryas. All this no doubt was due to the vast extent of the empire founded by Chandra Gupta that extended over the whole of Northern India from sea to sea, including even the provinces of the Paropanisadai, Aria, and Arachosia, beyond the modern frontiers of British India. The alliance of such a powerful emperor was courted even by the potentates of the Hellenistic world of his time. The consequence of this vast and varied realm was no doubt the constant stream of visitors, travellers, and envoys to and from India, and the resulting growth of elaborate regulations for their care and entertainment which were framed by the municipal commission under Chandra Gupta. “All foreigners were closely watched by officials, who provided suitable lodgings, escorts, and, in case of need, medical attendance.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-044" href="#sec-13-fn-044" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-044" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> As Mr. Vincent Smith remarks, “the existence of these elaborate regulations is <em>conclusive proof</em> that the Maurya Empire in the 3rd century <span class="sc">B.C.</span> was in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-165" data-page="165" data-folio="113" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n164_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 113">[p. 113]</button> constant intercourse with foreign states, and that large numbers of strangers visited the capital on business.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-045" href="#sec-13-fn-045" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-045" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> So great was the growth of foreign commerce that the mere taxes on imports formed a good and expanding source of revenue.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the days of Asoka, whose empire embraced a much wider area than that of his grandfather, India was brought into systematic connection with the distant Hellenistic monarchies of Syria, Egypt, Cyrene, Macedonia, and Epirus,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-046" href="#sec-13-fn-046" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-046" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> and she soon became, through the efforts of merchants, and missionaries preaching the gospel of universal brotherhood, at once the commercial and spiritual centre, the very heart, of the Old World. This was possible only through the instrumentality of an efficient national shipping and system of communications. As Mr. V. A. Smith observes: “When we remember Asoka’s relations with Ceylon and even more distant powers, we may credit him with a sea-going fleet as well as an army.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-047" href="#sec-13-fn-047" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-047" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In that monumental work called <em>Bodhisattvāvadāna Kalpalatā</em>, by the Kashmirian poet Kshemendra, of the 10th century <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, is preserved a very interesting story regarding Indian mercantile activity in the Eastern waters, which clearly <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-166" data-page="166" data-folio="114" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n165_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 114">[p. 114]</button> indicates that the progress of the foreign intercourse and naval activity of India during the days of the Emperor Chandra Gupta was continued also in the days of Asoka the Great. The 73rd Pallava or chapter of Kshemendra’s work above referred to relates how the Emperor Asoka, seated on the throne in the city of Pataliputra, while holding his court, was one day approached by some Indian merchants who traded to the distant islands. They informed him of their losses and complete ruin brought about by the depredations of seafaring pirates called Nagas (probably the Chinese, who are worshippers of the Dragon), who destroyed all their ships and plundered their treasure. They said that if the Emperor was disposed to be indifferent to them they would no doubt be forced to take to other ways of earning their livelihood, but the imperial exchequer in that case was liable to be emptied owing to absence of sea voyages (i.e. if there was a slackening of the sea-borne trade and a consequent falling off in the export and import duties). Then the story goes on to relate how Asoka, after bestowing some thought on the seafaring Nagas, was persuaded by a Buddhist priest to issue a sort of edict (which we may call Asoka’s <em>Marine Edict)</em> inscribed on a copper plate, which was, however, contemptuously set at naught by those for whom it was meant. It was only when Asoka became a devout Buddhist that he was able to make the Nagas respect his <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-167" data-page="167" data-folio="115" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n166_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 115">[p. 115]</button> edict and give up all their booty, which was afterwards distributed among the merchants robbed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-048" href="#sec-13-fn-048" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-048" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We have now narrated some of the facts in the sea-borne trade of India from the earliest times recorded to the glorious epoch of the Mauryas, seeking humbly to unroll the ample pages of one of the many forgotten but brilliant chapters in the early history of our country.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-13-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-13-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">V.
