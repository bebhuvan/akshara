<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-66"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="66" data-folio="32" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n65_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 32">[p. 32]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER II.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">DIRECT EVIDENCES FROM INDIAN SCULPTURE, PAINTING, AND COINS.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> conclusions pointed to by these literary evidences seem further to be supported by other kinds of evidence mainly monumental in their character. They are derived from old Indian art—from Indian sculpture and painting—and also from Indian coins. These evidences, though meagre in comparison with the available literary evidences, native and foreign alike, have, however, a compensating directness and freshness, nay, the permanence which Art confers, creating things of beauty that remain a joy for ever. Indeed, the light that is thrown on ancient Indian shipping by old Indian art is not yet extinguished, thanks to the durable character of old Indian monuments, thanks also to the labours of the Archaeological Department for their preservation and maintenance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are several representations of ships and boats in old Indian art. The earliest of them are those to be found among the Sanchi sculptures belonging to an age so far back as the 2nd century <span class="sc">b.c.</span> One of the sculptures on the Eastern Gateway of No. 1 Stupa at Sanchi represents a canoe made up of rough planks rudely sewn together by hemp <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-69" data-page="69" data-folio="33" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n68_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 33">[p. 33]</button> or string. “It represents a river or a sheet of fresh water with a canoe crossing it, and carrying three men in the ascetic priestly costume, two propelling and steering the boat, and the central figure, with hands resting on the gunwale, facing towards four ascetics, who are standing in reverential attitude at the water’s edge below.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-001" href="#sec-10-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> According to Sir A. Cunningham,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-002" href="#sec-10-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> the figures in the boat represent Sakya Buddha and his two principal followers; and Buddha himself has been compared in many Buddhist writings to “a boat and oar in the vast ocean of life and death.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-003" href="#sec-10-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> But General F. C. Maisley is inclined to view this sculpture “as representing merely the departure on some expedition or mission of an ascetic, or priest, of rank amid the reverential farewells of his followers.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-004" href="#sec-10-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> His main reasons for supporting this view are, firstly, that no representations of Buddha in human shape were resorted to until several centuries later than the date of these sculptures; and, secondly, because the representation is that of a common thong-bound canoe and not of a sacred barge suiting the great Buddha. There is another sculpture to be found on the Western Gateway of No. 1 Stupa at Sanchi which “represents a piece of water, with a barge <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-70" data-page="70" data-folio="34" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n69_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 34">[p. 34]</button> floating on it whose prow is formed by a winged gryphon and stern by a fish’s tail. The barge contains a pavilion overshadowing a vacant throne, over which a male attendant holds a <em>chatta</em>, while another man has a <em>chaori</em>; a third man is steering or propelling the vessel with a large paddle. In the water are fresh-water flowers and buds and a large shell; and there are five men floating about, holding on by spars and inflated skins, while a sixth appears to be asking the occupant of the stern of the vessel for help out of the water.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-005" href="#sec-10-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> This sculpture appears simply to represent the royal state barge, which quite anticipates its modern successors used by Indian nobles at the present day, and the scene is that of the king and some of his courtiers disporting themselves in an artificial piece of water; but it is also capable of a symbolical meaning, especially when we consider that the shape of the barge here shown is that of the sacred <em>Makara</em>, the fish avatara or Jataka of the Buddhist, just as the Hindu scriptures make the <em>Matsya</em>, or fish, the first of the avatars of Vishnu, whose latest incarnation was Buddha. According to Lieutenant Massey, however, this sculpture represents the conveyance of relics from India to Ceylon which is intercepted by Nagas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-006" href="#sec-10-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-67"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="67" data-folio="33" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n66_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 33">[p. 33]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-003"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-003_p0067.webp" alt="Engraving of a stone relief sculpture from the Sanchi Stupas depicting a boat on water with figures, a pavilion, and lotus flowers." loading="lazy"></figure><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-004"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-004_p0067.webp" alt="SCULPTURES FROM THE SANCHI STUPAS." loading="lazy"><figcaption>SCULPTURES FROM THE SANCHI STUPAS.</figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In passing it may be noted that the grotesque <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-71" data-page="71" data-folio="35" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n70_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 35">[p. 35]</button> and fanciful shapes given to the prow herein represented are not the invention or innovation of an ingenious sculptor trying his wit in original design; they are strictly traditional, and conform to established standards,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-007" href="#sec-10-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and are therefore identical with one or other of those possible forms of the prow of a ship which have been preserved for us in the slokas of the Sanskrit work <em>Yuktikalpataru</em> quoted and referred to above.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Next to Sanchi sculptures in point of time we may mention the sculptures in the caves of Kanhery in the small island of Salsette near Bombay, belonging, according to the unerring testimony of their inscriptions, to the 2nd century <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, the time of the Andhrabhritya or S’atakarni king Vashishthiputra (<span class="sc">A.D.</span> 133–162) and of Gotamiputra II. (<span class="sc">A.D.</span> 177–196). Among these sculptures there is a representation of a scene of shipwreck on the sea and two persons helplessly praying for rescue to god Padmapani who sends two messengers for the purpose. This is perhaps the oldest representation of a sea voyage in Indian sculpture.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-008" href="#sec-10-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I have come across other representations of ships and boats in Indian sculpture and painting.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-72"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="72" data-folio="36" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n71_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 36">[p. 36]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the course of a journey I made through Orissa and South India I noticed among the sculptures of the Temple of Jagannath at Puri a fine, well-preserved representation of a royal barge shown in relief on stone, of which I got a sketch made. The representation appears on that portion of the great Temple of Jagannath which is said to have been once a part of the Black Pagoda of Kanaraka belonging to the 12th century <span class="sc">A.D.</span> The sculpture shows in splendid relief a stately barge propelled by lusty oarsmen with all their might, and one almost hears the very splash of their oars; the water through which it cuts its way is thrown into ripples and waves indicated by a few simple and yet masterly touches; and the entire scene is one of dash and hurry indicative of the desperate speed of a flight or escape from danger. The beauty of the cabin and the simplicity of its design are particularly noticeable; the rocking-seat within is quite an innovation, probably meant to be effective against sea-sickness, while an equally ingenious idea is that of the rope or chain which hangs from the top and is grasped by the hand by the master of the vessel to steady himself on the rolling waters. It is difficult to ascertain what particular scene from our Shastras is here represented. It is very probably not a mere secular picture meant as an ornament. The interpretation put upon it by one of the many priests of whom I inquired, and which seems most <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-75" data-page="75" data-folio="37" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n74_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 37">[p. 37]</button> likely, being suggested by the surrounding sculptures, was that the scene represented S’rī Krishña being secretly and hurriedly carried away beyond the destructive reach of King Kańsa. It will also be remembered that the vessel herein represented is that of the Madhyamandirā type as defined in the <em>Yuktikalpataru</em>.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-73"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="73" data-folio="37" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n72_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 37">[p. 37]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-005"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-005_p0073.webp" alt="THE ROYAL BARGE ON THE JAGANNATH TEMPLE, PURI." loading="lazy"><figcaption>THE ROYAL BARGE ON THE JAGANNATH TEMPLE, PURI.</figcaption></figure><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-006"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-006_p0073.webp" alt="VAITAL DEUL." loading="lazy"><figcaption>VAITAL DEUL.</figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In Bhubaneshwara there is an old temple on the west side of the tank of Vindusārovara which requires to be noticed in this connection. The temple is called <em>Vaitāl Deul</em> after the peculiar form of its roof resembling a ship or boat capsized, the word <em>vaitāra</em> denoting a ship. The roof is more in the style of some of the Dravidian temples of Southern India, notably the <em>raths</em> of Mahavellipore, than of Orissan architecture.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are a few very fine representations of old Indian ships and boats among the far-famed paintings of the Buddhist cave-temples at Ajantā, whither the devotees of Buddhism, nineteen centuries or more ago, retreated from the distracting cares of the world to give themselves up to contemplation. There for centuries the wild ravine and the basaltic rocks were the scene of an application of labour, skill, perseverance, and endurance that went to the excavation of these painted palaces, standing to this day as monuments of a boldness of conception and a defiance of difficulty as possible, we believe, to the modern as to the ancient Indian <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-76" data-page="76" data-folio="38" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n75_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 38">[p. 38]</button> character. The worth of the achievement will be further evident from the fact that “much of the work has been carried on with the help of artificial light, and no great stretch of imagination is necessary to picture all that this involves in the Indian climate and in situations where thorough ventilation is impossible.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-009" href="#sec-10-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> About the truth and precision of the work, which are no less admirable than its boldness and extent, Mr. Griffiths has the following glowing testimony:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>During my long and careful study of the caves I have not been able to detect a single instance where a mistake has been made by cutting away too much stone; for if once a slip of this kind occurred, it could only have been repaired by the insertion of a piece which would have been a blemish.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-010" href="#sec-10-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">According to the best information, the execution of these works is supposed to have extended from the 2nd century <span class="sc">B.C.</span> to the 7th or the 8th century <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, covering a period of more than a thousand years. The earliest caves, namely the numbers 13, 12, 10, 9, 8, arranged in the order of their age, were made under the Andhrabhrityas or S’ātakarni kings in the 2nd and 1st centuries <span class="sc">B.C.</span>, and the date of the latest ones, namely the numbers 1–5, is placed between 525–650 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> By the time of Hiuen Tsang’s visit their execution was completed. Hiuen Tsang’s is the earliest recorded reference <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-77" data-page="77" data-folio="39" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n76_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 39">[p. 39]</button> we have to these caves. The Chinese pilgrim did not himself visit Ajantā, but he was at the capital of Pulakeshi II., King of Mahārāstra, where he heard that “on the eastern frontier of the country is a great mountain with towering crags and a continuous stretch of piled-up rocks and scarped precipice. In this there is a Sangharam (monastery) constructed in a dark valley. . . . On the four sides of the Vihara, on the stone walls, are painted different scenes in the life of the Tathagata’s preparatory life as a Bodhisattva. . . . These scenes have been cut out with the greatest accuracy and finish.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-011" href="#sec-10-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The representations of ships and boats furnished by Ajantā paintings are mostly in Cave No. 2, of which the date is, as we have seen, placed between 525–650 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> These were the closing years of the age which witnessed the expansion of India and the spread of Indian thought and culture over the greater part of the Asiatic continent. The vitality and individuality of Indian civilization were already fully developed during the spacious times of Gupta imperialism, which about the end of the 7th century even transplanted itself to the farther East, aiding in the civilization of Java, Cambodia, Siam, China, and even Japan. After the passing away of the Gupta Empire, the government of India was in the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-78" data-page="78" data-folio="40" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n77_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 40">[p. 40]</button> opening of the 7th century <span class="sc">a.d.</span> divided between Harshavardhana of Kanauj and Pulakeshi II. of the Deccan, both of whom carried on extensive intercourse with foreign countries. The fame of Pulakeshi spread beyond the limits of India and “reached the ears of Khusru II., King of Persia, who in the thirty-sixth year of his reign, 625–6 <span class="sc">a.d.</span>, even received a complimentary embassy from Pulakeshi. The courtesy was reciprocated by a return embassy sent from Persia, which was received in the Indian court with due honour.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-012" href="#sec-10-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> There is a large fresco painting in the Cave No. 1 at Ajantā which is still easily recognizable as a vivid representation of the ceremonial attending the presentation of their credentials by the Persian envoys.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As might be naturally expected, it was also the golden age of India’s maritime activity which is reflected, though dimly, in the national art of the period. The imperial fleet was thoroughly organized, consisting of hundreds of ships; and a naval invasion of Pulakeshi II. reduced Puri, “which was the mistress of the Western seas.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-013" href="#sec-10-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> About this time, as has been already hinted at, swarms of daring adventurers from Gujarat ports, anticipating the enterprise of the Drakes and Frobishers, or more properly of the Pilgrim Fathers, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-81" data-page="81" data-folio="41" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n80_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 41">[p. 41]</button> sailed in search of plenty till the shores of Java arrested their progress and gave scope to their colonizing ambition.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-79"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="79" data-folio="41" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n78_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 41">[p. 41]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-007"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-007_p0079.webp" alt="A SEA-GOING VESSEL. (From the Ajantā Paintings.) — [To face p. 40." loading="lazy"><figcaption>A SEA-GOING VESSEL. (From the Ajantā Paintings.) — <em>[To face p. 40.</em></figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The representations of ships and boats in the Ajantā paintings are therefore rightly interpreted by Griffiths as only a “vivid testimony to the ancient foreign trade of India.” Of the two representations herein reproduced, the first shows “a sea-going vessel with high stem and stern, with three oblong sails attached to as many upright masts. Each mast is surmounted by a truck, and there is carried a lug-sail. The jib is well filled with wind. A sort of bowsprit, projecting from a kind of gallows on deck, is indicated with the outflying jib, square in form,” like that borne till recent times by European vessels. The ship appears to be decked and has ports. Steering-oars hang in sockets or rowlocks on the quarter, and eyes are painted on the bows. There is also an oar behind; and under the awning are a number of jars, while two small platforms project fore and aft.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-014" href="#sec-10-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The vessel is of the Agramandirā type as defined in the <em>Yuktikalpataru</em>, our Sanskrit treatise on ships.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The second representation is that of the emperor’s pleasure-boat, which is “like the heraldic lymphad, with painted eyes at stem and stern, a pillared canopy amidships, and an umbrella forward, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-82" data-page="82" data-folio="42" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n81_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 42">[p. 42]</button> the steersman being accommodated on a sort of ladder which remotely suggests the steersman’s chair in the modern Burmese row-boats; while a rower is in the bows.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-015" href="#sec-10-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The vessel is of the Madhyamandirā type, and corresponds exactly to the form of those vessels which, according to the <em>Yuktikalpataru</em>, are to be used in pleasure trips by kings.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The third representation from the Ajantā paintings reproduced here is that of the scene of the landing of Vijaya in Ceylon, with his army and fleet, and his installation. The circumstances of Vijaya’s banishment from Bengal with all his followers and their families are fully set forth in the Pali works, <em>Mahāwańso, Rājāvalliya</em>, and the like. The fleet of Vijaya carried no less than 1,500 passengers. After touching at several places which, according to some authorities, lay on the western coast of the Deccan, the fleet reached the shores of Ceylon, approaching the island from the southern side. The date of Vijaya’s landing in Ceylon is said to have been the very day on which another very important event happened in the far-off father-land of Vijaya, for it was the day on which the Buddha attained the Nirvāṇa. Vijaya was next installed as king, and he became the founder of the “Great Dynasty.”</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-83"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="83" data-folio="43" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n82_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 43">[p. 43]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-008"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-008_p0083.webp" alt="THE ROYAL PLEASURE-BOAT. (From the Ajantā Paintings.) — [To face p. 42." loading="lazy"><figcaption>THE ROYAL PLEASURE-BOAT. (From the Ajantā Paintings.) — <em>[To face p. 42.</em></figcaption></figure><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-85"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="85" data-folio="43" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n84_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 43">[p. 43]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The conquest of Ceylon, laying as it did the foundation of a Greater India, was a national achievement that was calculated to stir deeply the popular mind, and was naturally seized by the imagination of the artist as a fit theme for the exercise of his powers. It is thus that we can explain its place in our national gallery at Ajantā as we can explain that of another similar representation suggestive of India’s position in the Asiatic political system of old—I mean the representation of Pulakeshi II. receiving the Persian embassy. Truly, Ajantā unfolds some of the forgotten chapters of Indian history.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The explanation of the complex picture before us can best be given after Mr. Griffiths, than whom no one is more competent to speak on the subject. On the left of the picture, issuing from a gateway, is a chief on his great white elephant, with a bow in his hand; and two minor chiefs, likewise on elephants, each shadowed by an umbrella. They are accompanied by a retinue of foot-soldiers, some of whom bear banners and spears and others swords and shields. The drivers of the elephants, with goads in their hands, are seated, in the usual manner, on the necks of the animals. Sheaves of arrows are attached to the sides of the howdahs. The men are dressed in tightly-fitting short-sleeved jackets, and loin-cloths with long ends hanging behind in folds. Below, four soldiers on horseback with spears are <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-86" data-page="86" data-folio="44" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n85_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 44">[p. 44]</button> in a boat, and to the right are represented again the group on their elephants, also in boats, engaged in battle, as the principal figures have just discharged their bows. The elephants sway their trunks about, as is their wont when excited. The near one is shown in the act of trumpeting, and the swing of his bell indicates motion. “These may be thought open to the criticism on Raphael’s cartoon of the Draught of Fishes, viz. that his boat is too small to carry his figures. The Indian artist has used Raphael’s treatment for Raphael’s reason; preferring, by reduced and conventional indication of the inanimate and merely accessory vessels, to find space for expression, intelligible to his public, of the elephants and horses and their riders necessary to his story.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Vijaya Sińha, according to legend, went (<span class="sc">B.C.</span> 543) to Ceylon with a large following. The Rakshasis or female demons inhabiting it captivated them by their charms; but Vijaya, warned in a dream, escaped on a wonderful horse. He collected an army, gave each soldier a magic verse (<em>mantra</em>), and returned. Falling upon the demons with great impetuosity, he totally routed them, some fleeing the island and others being drowned in the sea. He destroyed their town, and established himself as king in the island, to which he gave the name of Sińhala.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-016" href="#sec-10-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-87"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="87" data-folio="45" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n86_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 45">[p. 45]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-009"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-009_p0087.webp" alt="LANDING OF VIJAYA IN CEYLON (ABOUT 543 B.C.). — [To face p. 44." loading="lazy"><figcaption>LANDING OF VIJAYA IN CEYLON (ABOUT 543 B.C.). — <em>[To face p. 44.</em></figcaption></figure><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-89"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="89" data-folio="45" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n88_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 45">[p. 45]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I shall now present a very important and interesting series of representations of ships which are found not in India but far away from her, among the magnificent sculptures of the Temple of Borobudur in Java, where Indian art reached its highest expression amid the Indian environment and civilization transplanted there.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Most of the sculptures show in splendid relief ships in full sail and scenes recalling the history of the colonization of Java by Indians in the earlier centuries of the Christian era. Of one of them Mr. Havell<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-017" href="#sec-10-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> thus speaks in appreciation: “The ship, magnificent in design and movement, is a masterpiece in itself. It tells more plainly than words the perils which the Prince of Gujarat and his companions encountered on the long and difficult voyages from the west coast of India. But these are over now. The sailors are hastening to furl the sails and bring the ship to anchor.” There are other ships which appear to be sailing tempest-tossed on the ocean, fully trying the pluck and dexterity of the oarsmen, sailors, and pilots, who, however, in their movements and looks impress us with the idea that they are quite equal to the occasion. These sculptured types of a 6th or 7th century Indian ship—and it is the characteristic of Indian art to represent conventional forms or <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-90" data-page="90" data-folio="46" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n89_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 46">[p. 46]</button> types rather than individual things—carry our mind back to the beginning of the 5th century <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, when a similar vessel also touched the shores of Java after a more than three months’ continuous sail from Ceylon with 200 passengers on board including the famous Chinese pilgrim Fa-Hien. It is noteworthy that “astern of the great ship was a smaller one as a provision in case of the larger vessel being injured or wrecked during the voyage.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-018" href="#sec-10-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The form of these ships closely resembles that of a catamaran, and somewhat answers to the following description of some Indian ships given by Nicolo Conti in the earlier part of the 15th century: “The natives of India build some ships larger than ours, capable of containing 2,000 butts, and with five sails and as many masts. The lower part is constructed with triple planks, in order to withstand the force of the tempests to which they are much exposed. But some ships are so built in compartments that should one part be shattered the other portion remaining entire may accomplish the voyage.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-019" href="#sec-10-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These ships will be found to present two types of vessels. To the first type belong Nos. 1, 3, 5, 6. They are generally longer and broader than the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-93" data-page="93" data-folio="47" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n92_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 47">[p. 47]</button> vessels of the second type, have more than one mast, are many-ribbed, the ribs being curved, not straight. These vessels are built so narrow and top-heavy that it is necessary to fit outriggers for safety. An outrigger is a series of planks or logs joined to the boat with long poles or spars as shown in Fig. 1. It is customary when a large amount of sail is being carried for the crew to go out and stand on the outrigger as shown in Fig. 5.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-91"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="91" data-folio="47" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n90_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 47">[p. 47]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-010"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-010_p0091.webp" alt="INDIAN ADVENTURERS SAILING OUT TO COLONIZE JAVA. No. 3 has got square sails and one stay-sail in front. In No. 5 the crew appear to be setting sail or taking sail down. No. 6 has been interpreted by Mr. Havell as representing sailors “hastening to furl the sails and bring the ship to anchor,” but this suggestion seems to be contradicted by the sea-gulls or albatrosses of the sculpture flying around the vessel, which without doubt indicate that the ship is in mid-ocean, far away from land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No. 1 shows probably a wooden figure-head and not a man; so also do Nos. 3, 5, 6. There is also a sort of cabin in each of the vessels of the first type. Again, in No. 1 the figure aft appears to be a compass.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-020" href="#sec-10-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-94"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="94" data-folio="48" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n93_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 48">[p. 48]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No. 5 appears to be in collision with some other vessel, or perhaps it shows a smaller vessel which used to be carried as a provision against damages or injury to the larger one from the perils of navigation. This was, as already pointed out, true of the merchantman in which Fa-Hien took passage from Ceylon to Java. No. 5 illustrates also the use of streamers to indicate the direction of winds.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There is another type of ships represented in Nos. 2 and 4. The fronts are less curved than in the first type; there is also only one mast. No. 2 shows a scene of rescue, a drowning man being helped out of the water by his comrade. No. 4 represents a merrier scene, the party disporting themselves in catching fish.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Some of the favourite devices of Indian sculpture to indicate water may be here noticed. Fresh and sea waters are invariably and unmistakably indicated by fishes, lotuses, aquatic leaves, and the like. The <em>makara</em>, or alligator, showing its fearful row of teeth in Fig. 2, is used to indicate the ocean; so also are the albatrosses or sea-gulls of Fig. 6. The curved lines are used to indicate waves.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-95"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="95" data-folio="49" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indianshippinghi00mookrich/page/n94_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 49">[p. 49]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-012"><img src="/figures/indian-shipping/fig-012_p0095.webp" alt="INDIAN ADVENTURERS SAILING OUT TO COLONIZE JAVA. Thus there has been a remarkable find of some Andhra coins on the east coast, belonging to the 2nd and 3rd century <span class="sc">A.D.</span>, on which is to be detected the device of a two-masted ship, “evidently of large size.” With regard to the meaning of the device Mr. Vincent Smith has thus remarked: “Some pieces bearing the figure of a ship suggest the inference that Yajña S’rī’s (<span class="sc">A.D.</span> 184–213) power was not confined to the land.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-022" href="#sec-10-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Again: “The ship-coins, perhaps struck by Yajña S’rī, testify to the existence of a sea-borne trade on the Coromandel coast in the 1st century of the Christian era.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-023" href="#sec-10-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> This inference is, of course, amply supported by what we know of the history of the Andhras, in whose times, according to R. Sewell, “there was trade both by sea and overland with Western Asia, Greece, Rome, and Egypt, as well as China and the East.”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-024" href="#sec-10-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In his <em>South Indian Buddhist Antiquities</em>,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-025" href="#sec-10-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Alexander Rea gives illustrations and descriptions of three of these ship-coins of the Andhras. The rudder, in the shape of a post with spoon on end, projects below. The deck is straight, and on it are two round objects from which rise two masts, each with a cross-tree at the top. Traces of rigging can be faintly seen. The obverse of the second shows a ship to the right. The device resembles that of the first, but the features are not quite distinct. The deck in the specimen is curved. The obverse of the third represents a device similar to the preceding, showing even more distinctly than the first. The rigging is crossed between the masts. On the right of the vessel appear three balls, and under the side are two spoon-shaped oars.
