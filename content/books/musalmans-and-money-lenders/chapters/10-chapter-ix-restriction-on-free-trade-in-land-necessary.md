<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-105"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="105" data-folio="93" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924013743848/page/n104_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 93">[p. 93]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER IX.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">RESTRICTION ON FREE TRADE IN LAND NECESSARY.</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Statistics of land alienated to money-lenders still imperfect.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Fifteen</span> years ago, when reviewing the Revenue Report of the Punjab for 1871-72, the Lieutenant-Governor remarked :— "There do not appear to be any statistics available for showing the amount of land which generally, since annexation, or during any particular year, has been sold or mortgaged to money-lenders by zemindars." Some figures having been prepared, the Lieutenant-Governor in the following year "resolved" that they were "indicative of remarkable prosperity and a very moderate land revenue assessment." Since that year first regular settlements and the revisions of such settlements earlier made, have been completed or are in progress in 22 <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-001" href="#sec-10-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> districts, and efforts have been made to obtain reliable statistics as to transfers of rights in land, but with small success ; for, writing on December 24th, 1885, the Lieutenant-Governor, reviewing the Revenue Report for 1884-85, remarked that such returns were still admittedly defective and uncertain in meaning. <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-002" href="#sec-10-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">†</a></sup> He then proposes</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>"In the Punjab the sale of landed property is not decreed by the civil court as an ordinary process for recovering a debt, and is only sanctioned in special cases, where there is no other possible way of satisfying a creditor. The number of such sales has been insignificant. The transfers of land by private sale and mortgage in the three years 1874-77 have concerned 850,000 acres, or an eightieth part of the area of the Province. The consideration <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-106" data-page="106" data-folio="94" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924013743848/page/n105_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 94">[p. 94]</button> that typical villages should be selected, and the history of transfers worked out in them, so that by the “patient study of particular instances,” Government “may be enabled to generalize with confidence on a wider field.”</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Why 15 years of inquiry have resulted in nothing.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Such is the disheartening outcome of 15 years of perfunctory investigation—except in the case of zealous Settlement and District Officers—into the broad facts of the statistics of land alienations, to say nothing of the minutiæ of the remoter causes of agricultural indebtedness. The explanation probably is, that it is in the nature of things that the attitude of most Provincial Governors on the land question of their charges should be one of <em>laisser aller</em>, until the conservative optimism of 30 years of Indian service is roused into spasmodic action by some catastrophe, such as occurred in the Bombay Deccan, in 1875.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">A catastrophe necessary to rouse Government to action.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Were it not so, agriculturists would not have risen in 1857, as they did, against their auc-tion-purchase landlords in the Gangetic Valley. Nor could the Famine Commission, 23 years later, have recorded the discreditable fact that 27 per cent. of the whole land of the N.-W. Provinces had, by 1873, passed into the hands of the trading <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-107" data-page="107" data-folio="95" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924013743848/page/n106_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 95">[p. 95]</button> classes. It is small consolation to know that, owing to later changes in the Code of Civil Procedure, the annual rate of transfer has since been slower. We are drifting in the same way in Lower Burmah, in which the holdings of the peasant cultivators are, I believe, being bought up by money-lenders and speculating capitalists, and no doubt a similar exploitation of Upper Burmah has already begun also.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">All necessary statistics could be collected without expense in six months.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">All material facts on which to base reform are well-known.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">But to return to the case of the Punjab, in which, except in regions of small rainfall, the evil of peasant expropriation is not yet generally serious. Assuming, as the Lieutenant-Governor says, that before legislation it will be wise to ascertain all obtainable particulars as to the causes of the transfers of rights in land and of indebtedness in typical villages, why spend 15 or 20 years in considering the matter? Such information could be collected in six months for the whole Province at little or no cost. Let a good Settlement Superintendent or Extra Assistant Commissioner be sent to each district; let him go to each selected village, and publicly investigate on the spot the history of each peasant's indebtedness and alienations of land, and of each money-lender's position, and the thing is done. Here, in the Punjab, all the material facts on the land question are known to every officer in it who has ever mixed freely with the people, or even read the available literature on the subject.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Facts are reduced into six propositions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Formulated as six propositions, those facts are:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gift of property in land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I.—The gift of full individual proprietary right—in which term is included the rights of occupancy tenants—so extended each man's credit that it enables him to borrow up to the market value of his holding.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-108"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="108" data-folio="96" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924013743848/page/n107_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 96">[p. 96]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Unfitness of peasants for such a gift.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">2.—Punjab agriculturists were, and still are, unfit for such a gift, the vast majority of them being to-day, after 36 years of British rule, almost as rude, ignorant,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-10-fn-004" href="#sec-10-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-10-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> and imprudent as they were upon annexation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Fixed cash assessments a mistake in rainless tracts.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">3.—In tracts in which the outturn is uncertain the rigidity of the land revenue system— which demands crop or no crop after each harvest-time a cash payment equal to about the value of one-sixth of an average yield—together with a vicious but natural propensity on the part of the people to live for the day only, have involved a considerable and annually increasing percentage of them in serious or hopeless indebtedness.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Over-legislation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">4.—The Acts, Codes, and Rules affecting the relations between ignorant debtors and educated Bunniah-creditors, all tend to benefit the latter at the expense of the former.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Expropriation of peasantry by money-lenders.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Education amongst Musalman peasants.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">5.—Making due allowance for the consideration shown to agricultural debtors in the new Civil Procedure Code (Act XIV of 1882), and in the Law of Contract as interpreted in recent Chief Court Circulars and Decisions, the fact remains that agricultural indebtedness is increasing, and that if the acquisition of rights in land by Bunniahs be not arrested, agrarian troubles will shortly arise.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-109"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="109" data-folio="97" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924013743848/page/n108_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 97">[p. 97]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Its danger for a government of foreigners.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">6.—No Government, particularly a handful of foreign sojourners, such as we are, can permit the hereditary land-holding classes, who compose "the people of the country," to be subjected to 20,000 or 30,000 money-lenders—all men of no political weight. In the western half of the Punjab, this consideration derives additional force from the fact that the "people" all belong to warlike Mahomedan tribes, and therefore look upon Hindu money-lenders with contempt.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Direction of reform.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The above six propositions being accepted, the corollary follows that for the Western Punjab, if not for the whole Province, the process of expropriation must be arrested as soon as possible by wise reforms in our land revenue system for tracts of deficient rainfall, and in our civil law and procedure generally; and, moreover, the aim of some of the reforms must be to so diminish credit that a careless man will only borrow under the compulsion of necessity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Interesting but immaterial issues.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Whether 25 or 50 per cent. of the borrowing classes are heavily involved; whether the percentage of peasant proprietors, indebted land-lords or occupancy tenants, is the largest; whether the first serious debt, or the transition from extricable to hopeless embarrassment, arose from misfortune, <em>e. g.</em>, a bad season, or loss of plough-cattle, or preventible causes, such as unnecessary expenditure; what ratio the mortgage value of land bears to the selling value; whether the consideration is generally overstated in saledeeds in order to defeat the possible claims of pre-emptors, and many other matters,—are all questions, no doubt, interesting in themselves, but of no vital importance whatsoever as solvents of the great land problem before us.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-110"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="110" data-folio="98" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/cu31924013743848/page/n109_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 98">[p. 98]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Our duty stated.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">What we have to do is to stop unnecessary borrowing ; to minimise involuntary indebtedness ; to preserve to the hereditary peasant proprietors of the Province the possession of the land and of its fruits ; to simplify the law of contract, and the procedure by which it is administered in our Courts; and, generally, to adapt our system of civil justice to the means and capacities of the peasantry : not to elaborate—as is the tendency—the system so as to make it profitably comprehensible only to the superior intelligence of money-lenders and legal practitioners.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Heads of reforms classified.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">With these preliminary remarks I shall proceed to state and discuss the various reforms which, after long consideration of the subject, commend themselves to me as most practicable and susceptible of easy introduction, without materially affecting any vested rights or shocking any prejudice. The reforms, of which I shall write, may be grouped under the following heads:—(1) <em>Land Tenure</em>; (2) <em>Land Revenue System</em>; (3) <em>Civil Procedure</em>; (4) <em>Specific Miscellaneous Reforms</em>.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-10-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-10-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">*.</a> <span class="fn-body">Detail is, first regular settlements completed in seven districts, revisions in eleven, and in four more still in progress.</span></li><li id="sec-10-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-10-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">†.</a> <span class="fn-body">The available information on the subject was summed up by the Famine Commission at page 126, para. 7, Part II of their Report of 1880, as follows :—</span></li><li id="sec-10-fn-003" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><strong class="fn-backlink fn-unlinked">[Unlinked note, p. 94]</strong> <span class="fn-body">paid has amounted to 145 lakhs of rupees, of which half was contributed by agriculturists and half by non-agriculturists.
