June 6.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">HAVING finished my business at <em>Seringapatam</em>, on the 6th of June 1800, I left it early in the morning, and assembled my people at a small village, named <em>Gaynangur</em>, which is situated among the hills north from the river <em>Cávery</em>. The fields that are at present occupied having now been all ploughed, I observe that a large proportion of the arable land is left waste.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Minerals.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Gaynangur</em> there is much <em>talc</em>; but the laminæ are not large enough to serve for glass. It occupies the rents and small veins in an extensive chain of quartz, which is in a state of decomposition, and of which some parts are red, and some white. By digging deep, it is probable that larger pieces of the <em>talc</em> might be found. The inhabitants are wonderfully ignorant of the mineral productions of their country; for they did not know the limestone nodules, so common in this place, when shown to them in their own fields. All their lime comes from the city.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Mundium.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">7th <em>June</em>.—I went to <em>Mundium</em>. Not above one third of the arable land appears to be now occupied.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Locusts.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The flight of locusts that I saw when I was here last (p. 57), settled at a village to the eastward, and ate up all the young <em>Jola</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Watered land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The rice land here is watered entirely from tanks or reservoirs; and the cultivation is never commenced till there be a sufficiency of water in the tank to ensure the crop. When the rains set in early, and fill the tank timely, the farmers have yearly two crops; but when the early part of the season is dry, they take a <em>Hainu</em> crop of <em>Wull’ Ellu</em>, <em>Udu</em>, <em>Hessaru</em>, or <em>Carlay</em>; and afterwards, when the tank is filled, plough for a crop of <em>Caru</em> rice.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-172"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="172" data-folio="138" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n171_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 138">[p. 138]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. The <em>Mulu Butta</em> is never sown, except when there is a deficiency of water. The only cultivation here is the <em>Mola Butta</em>, or sprouted-seed ; the manner of preparing which is as follows : Steep the seed in water all night ; next morning mix it with cow-dung, and fresh plants of the <em>Tumbay Sopu</em>, or <em>Phlomis esculenta</em>, Roxb. MSS., and put it in a <em>Mudy</em>. On the <em>Mudy</em> place a heavy stone, and on the two following days sprinkle it with water. On the third day it is fit for sowing.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For the <em>Hainu</em> crop, the ploughings, from about the 1st of June till the 11th of July, are nine in number. Dung and leaves are then put on the field, and trampled into the mud. The water is now let off, until no more than a depth of one inch remains ; afterwards, the seed is sown, and a slight sprinkling of dung is laid over it. A watering once in three days is then given ; and after the third time, the field is inundated till the grain ripens. The weeds are removed on the 20th, 40th, and 60th days. The <em>Caru</em> cultivation is exactly the same, only the ploughings are between the 21st of November, and the 20th of December.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In both kinds of cultivation, and in every species of rice, an equal quantity of seed is sown on the same extent of ground, and the produce is nearly equal. By measuring a plot of ground, and reducing to the English standards the farmer's estimate of its seed and produce in a middling good crop, I find the seed to be for an acre, 1 bushel 1,624 gallon, and the produce to be 36 bushels 0,720 gallon, or thirty fold. The quantity of seed here is smaller, and the produce greater, than in the land watered by the river <em>Cávery.</em></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-173"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="173" data-folio="139" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n172_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 139">[p. 139]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8. <em>Madura.</em> Weather.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">8th <em>June.</em>—I went to <em>Madura.</em> Since the 4th instant, when we had a heavy storm of wind, rain, and thunder, the weather has been remarkably pleasant. The sun is in general clouded, and the temperature of the air like moderate summer weather in England.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Depopulation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The proportion of ground in actual cultivation does not appear to exceed one tenth part of the country. At <em>Madura</em> there is a very fine reservoir, which indeed waters more ground than the inhabitants can cultivate; for they are much reduced in number. In <em>Hyder’s</em> time they amounted to 250 houses; and they rose to 400 in the early part of the Sultan’s government. By the expeditions of Lord Cornwallis the houses sunk to 250, and <em>Tippoo’s</em> late oppressions have reduced them to 100. The people, however, seem to be much satisfied with the protection they enjoy under the British forces, and are betaking themselves with industry to the re-establishment of their country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Reservoir.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Irrigation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reservoir, and an old pagoda in the fort, are said to be the work of <em>Vishnu Verdana Raya</em>, a prince who, about 700 years ago, had very extensive dominions in this country. The reservoir is a very valuable work, and ought to render this prince’s name venerable to the latest posterity. It receives a supply from the river by means of a dam and canal; and, with the repairs which it is now about to receive, is expected to be able to supply with water, through the whole year, all the fields under the level of its bank. The extent of this land is very considerable; but the supply of water having been for some years deficient, the farmers have been obliged, on many fields, to content themselves with a <em>Hainu</em> crop of <em>Carlay</em>, and a <em>Caru</em> crop of <em>Jola.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Two crops of rice are never taken from the same field in one year; but in order to divide the labour, part of the rice ground is cultivated in the <em>Hainu</em>, and part in the <em>Caru</em> season.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-174"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="174" data-folio="140" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n173_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 140">[p. 140]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. a farmer to his own field, asking him how much seed it required, and measuring it. The measures were then changed into English; and the other farmers were interrogated concerning the quantity that each kind of rice should produce on an equal extent of the different kinds of soil. The produce of the same kind of rice, in the same soil, whether cultivated as <em>Hainu</em> or <em>Caru</em>, or as <em>Mola</em> or <em>Nati</em>, should be nearly the same.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">All the kinds of rice may be raised either as <em>Hainu</em> or <em>Caru</em> crops, or by the <em>Mola</em> or <em>Nati</em> modes of cultivation. No <em>Puneji</em> is ever attempted. The <em>Mola</em> cultivation is exactly the same as at <em>Mundium</em>. The seedlings for transplantation, in the <em>Nati</em> cultivation, are always raised as <em>Niragy</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8. Saline earth.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cultivation of sugar-cane being somewhat different from that used at <em>Seringapatam</em>, I shall enter into the particulars. The <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-175" data-page="175" data-folio="141" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n174_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 141">[p. 141]</button> only cane cultivated here is the <em>Restali</em>. The preparation of the ground occupies about three months previous to the end of <em>Phalguna</em>, which happened this year on the 14th of March. The steps taken in this preparation are as follow. Water the field. Allow it three days to dry, then plough it six times, and break the clods with the <em>Col Kudáli</em>. Manure with leaves, and plough again; manure with dung, and plough three times. The field, having been thus prepared, is divided into beds, as at <em>Seringapatam</em> (p. 96). Four holes, about a cubit's distance from each other, are formed in the breadth of each bed. Two cuttings of three joints each, are put in each hole, and covered first with an inch of earth, and then with five inches of dung. Water is given to each hole, and every morning for fifteen days this is repeated. The holes are then dug up with a sharp stick, and more dung is given. For fifteen days more the watering must be again repeated. The whole field must then be hoed with the <em>Yella Kudáli</em>, and in each bed a winding channel must be formed, passing through between the rows of holes, as in the explanatory sketch (Fig. 7). When there is no rain, these channels must once a week be filled with water. At the end of a month the ground must be again hoed, and the channels formed afresh. Between the 24th of May and the 22d of June, the canes become a cubit high. The plants of each hole must then be tied together with a binding of leaves. A third hoeing is now given, and the earth heaped up round the roots of the cane. The waterings once a week must be continued. When the plants have grown another cubit, which will be about the beginning of September, they must be tied a second time; and again a third time about the beginning of November, the watering once a week having been regularly continued. About the end of December, the works are repaired; in the tenth month from planting the crop commences, and must be finished in the eleventh. The cane is succeeded by <em>Sesamum</em>, that by rice, and that again by sugar. The <em>Sesamum</em> and rice occupy one year, and the sugar-cane another, alternately. The <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-176" data-page="176" data-folio="142" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n175_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 142">[p. 142]</button> best fields for this cultivation are composed of a sandy red soil. The low black clays are reserved entirely for rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In this part of the country much of the soil is impregnated with saline matter, and called <em>Soulu munnu</em>. Of this there are two kinds ; one chiefly impregnated with carbonate of soda, the other with the muriates of soda and magnesia. The latter would produce nothing : the former is cultivated, although it produces poor crops. The manure used for it is formed of the branches of the <em>Euphorbium Tirucalli</em>, which in this part of the country are never used on any other kind of rice-ground. In the country near <em>Madras</em> they are, for all soils, the most esteemed manure.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Sri Vaishna vam Bráhmans.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Having procured a <em>Sri Vaishnavam Bráhman</em>, esteemed a man of great learning, I examined him concerning the peculiarities of his sect ; but with very little satisfaction. However well these men may be instructed in certain dogmas, and the art of disputation, they are not qualified to give any satisfactory information concerning the origin of their order, or the means by which it came to prevail over others ; for, of the sectaries which differ from themselves, such as those of <em>Budha</em>, <em>Jaina</em>, or <em>Siva</em>, they profess an almost total ignorance, and sovereign contempt.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This man allows, that in the existing <em>Védas</em> no mention is made of any division of the <em>Bráhmans</em> into sects ; but he contends, that from the very beginning of the universe all the three sects of <em>Smartal</em>, <em>Aayngar</em>, and <em>Madual</em>, existed ; and he says, that they are mentioned in the eighteen <em>Puránas</em>, which, next to the <em>Védas</em>, are by the <em>Bráhmans</em> esteemed as most holy. Although the <em>Bráhmans</em> have existed from the beginning of time, yet in the ninth century of the era of <em>Sáliváhana</em>, or tenth century of Christianity, twenty-one heretical sects had arisen in <em>Bhárata-khanda</em>, and had turned from the true worship almost the whole of its inhabitants. Each of these sects had a <em>Bhásha</em>, or book explaining their doctrine, founded partly on dogmas derived from the <em>Védas</em>, and explained in the last six of the eighteen <em>Puránas</em>, and partly on tenets contrary <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-177" data-page="177" data-folio="143" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n176_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 143">[p. 143]</button> to the books esteemed sacred by the <em>Bráhmans</em>. The most remarkable of these sects were the <em>Buddhists</em>, the <em>Jainas</em>, and the <em>Sarvakas</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">About this time arose a celebrated doctor of the <em>Bráhmans</em>, named <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, who belonged to the sect of <em>Siva</em>. The eighteen <em>Puránas</em> are divided into three distinct doctrines, called <em>Satwika</em>, <em>Rájasa</em>, and <em>Támasa</em>; the <em>principles</em> of which, from their tendency, are compared to God, to a king, and to the devil; the first and last resembling God and the Evil Spirit, while the <em>Rájasa</em> is of a princely nature, partly good and partly bad. <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, as a <em>Smartal</em>, acknowledged the two first parts to be the proper guide for the conduct of <em>Bráhmans</em>; and wrote a <em>Bhásha</em>, or commentary, called after his own name; in which he explained the doctrine of the first twelve of the eighteen <em>Puránas</em>, so as to reconcile it with the tenets of six of the prevailing sects, of whom the most remarkable were the <em>Savaram</em>, <em>Ganapatyam</em>, <em>Saivam</em>, and <em>Vaishnavum</em>. By this method he gained a strong party; and having, among others, brought over the prince of <em>Sringa-giri</em>, where he lived, he commenced a violent persecution against the heretical doctrines.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the year of <em>Sáliváhana</em> 932 (A. D. 1009), at <em>Sri Permaturu</em>, or <em>Srivaram P’huthur</em>, near <em>Madras</em>, was born <em>Ráma Anuja Achárya</em> of the <em>Sri Vaishnavam</em> sect of <em>Bráhmans</em>, and who, of course, followed the authority of the first six only of the eighteen <em>Puránas</em>. These six are called <em>Vaishnavam</em>, <em>Náradyam</em>, <em>Bágawatam</em>, <em>Garudam</em>, <em>Padmam</em>, and <em>Varáham</em>. The second division of the eighteen <em>Puránas</em> is read by this sect of <em>Bráhmans</em>, although they do not found on it any of their doctrines. They look with horror on the third division. On arriving at the age of discretion, <em>Ráma Anuja</em> became a <em>Sannyási</em>, and wrote a commentary, in which he confuted the works of <em>Sankara Achárya</em>, and demonstrated, that of the twenty-one sects, the only one that ought to be tolerated was that called <em>Vaishnavam</em>. The commentary of <em>Ráma Anuja</em> is now the chief guide of the <em>Sri</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-178" data-page="178" data-folio="144" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n177_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 144">[p. 144]</button> <em>Vaishnavam Bráhmans</em>. They worship <em>Vishnu</em>, and the gods of his family only, and all over the <em>Decan</em> are almost exclusively the officiating priests in the temples of these deities. They allege <em>Brahmá</em> to be a son of <em>Vishnu</em>, and <em>Siva</em> the son of <em>Brahmá</em>, and consider them as the creative and destructive powers in the universe; but they abhor the worship of these gods. <em>Vishnu</em> they consider as the same with <em>Para Brahmă</em>, or the Supreme Being: yet they worship him in nine only of his ten incarnations. <em>Budha</em>, although the tenth incarnation of <em>Vishnu</em>, is never worshipped by them, nor, I believe, by any <em>Bráhman</em>. The reason assigned for this is as follows: one of the <em>Asuras</em>, or demons, named <em>Tripura</em>, possessed a city, the inhabitants of which were very troublesome to the inhabitants of <em>Brahma Lóka</em>, heaven of <em>Brahmá</em>, who attempted in vain to take the place; it being destined not to fall, so long as the women who resided in it should preserve their chastity, which hitherto had been inviolate. The angels at length offered up their prayers to <em>Vishnu</em>, who took upon himself the form of a most beautiful young man, and became <em>Budha Avatára</em>. Entering then into the city, he danced naked before the women, and inspired them with loose desires; so that the fortress, being no longer defended by the shield of purity, soon fell a prey to the angels. As the <em>Bráhmans</em> cannot defend this action of the god, they never invoke him by the name or in the form of <em>Budha Avatára</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Ráma Anuja Achárya</em> having had great success both against the <em>Smartal</em>, and the heretical sects, especially the <em>Jainas</em>, formed a hierarchy for his followers. He divided the whole into eighty-four portions; and ordered, that each portion, and their descendents, should be subject to a <em>Guru</em> or <em>Swámalu</em> of his appointment, and to the successors of this <em>Guru</em>. The number of <em>Gurus</em> belonging to this sect are therefore eighty-four; of whom five are <em>Sannyási</em>, and seventy-nine are married hereditary chiefs.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Matams</em>, or places where the five <em>Sannyási Gurus</em> chiefly reside, are <em>Ahobalum</em>, <em>Totadri</em> near <em>Ráméswara</em>, <em>Tripathi</em>, <em>Sri Rangam</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-179" data-page="179" data-folio="145" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n178_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 145">[p. 145]</button> and <em>Kunji</em>. When one of these <em>Sannyásis</em> observes the approach of death, he appoints some <em>Vidwansa</em>, or man of learning and piety, to be his successor. If the person chosen give his consent, he must forsake his wife, children, and goods, part of which goes to his children, and part is given in charity; that is to say, to the <em>Bráhmans</em>. The new <em>Sannyási</em> shaves his head, and throws aside the thread by which <em>Bráhmans</em> are distinguished. The virtues and powers belonging to his high rank he receives along with an <em>Upadésa</em>, which is delivered to him by his predecessor. <em>Upadésa</em> is a mysterious sentence, which the <em>Hindus</em> receive from their <em>Gurus</em>, and constantly mutter when at their devotions. That of the <em>Bráhmans</em> is entirely different from what is bestowed on the lower casts; and is again very inferior to that given to the <em>Sannyási Gurus</em>, which, according to them, has most wonderful powers. In case of sudden death, the followers of the <em>Mata</em> meet, and choose from among themselves a <em>Sannyási</em>, who gets an <em>Upadésa</em> from one of the others. These <em>Gurus</em> frequently give an <em>Upadésa</em>, and some images, to a favourite disciple, and appoint him a kind of deputy to manage their affairs at a distance. Thus the <em>Ahobalam Swámalu</em> has sent a deputy to <em>Mailcotay</em>, who resides at that great place of <em>Hindu</em> worship, and there watches over the interests of his superior. These deputies observe the rules of <em>Sannyási</em>, but have no power to appoint a successor. When one of them dies, the followers send back the images to their <em>Guru</em>, and request that he would depute another representative.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Among the <em>Sri Vaishnavam Bráhmans</em> the office of an hereditary <em>Guru</em> descends in the male line according to primogeniture; but, when one of them has no children, he must adopt his nearest male relation, who succeeds him as his son. Kindred by the female line is considered as not forming a tie of blood. These hereditary chiefs, once in two or three years, make a circuit round the places where their followers live. They also send agents to transact their business. An infant may succeed, and during his minority the business <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-180" data-page="180" data-folio="146" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n179_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 146">[p. 146]</button> is carried on by the nearest male relation, or by some other <em>Vaidika Bráhman</em>, whom the family appoints.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Sannyásis</em> and hereditary <em>Gurus</em> seem to be totally independent of each other, and to possess nearly the same authority and powers over their followers. When a <em>Guru</em> of any sect comes near a place, the whole inhabitants of a pure descent, whether they be his followers or not, must go out to receive him with the utmost respect. What is meant by the followers of a <em>Guru</em>, are certain families attached to him, to whom he performs certain ceremonies, and over whom, in all matters connected with religion, he possesses a jurisdiction. In general, every man follows the <em>Guru</em> of his father: but this seems to be a voluntary submission; and it is commonly allowed, that a man, whenever he pleases, may change his <em>Guru</em>. The ceremonies bestowed by the <em>Sri Vaishnavam Gurus</em> on their followers are chiefly <em>Upadésa</em> and <em>Chakrántikam</em>. The <em>Upadésa</em> I have already explained. It is delivered orally to the follower; and to write it down, or reveal it, are crimes of such an enormous magnitude as to be quite unknown. The <em>Chakrántikam</em> is performed with the spear of the god <em>Vishnu</em>, which is made hot, and applied by the <em>Guru</em> to the shoulder of the disciple, so as to burn the skin. During life this is frequently repeated; the <em>Upadésa</em> is only delivered once.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Neither of these ceremonies are ever bestowed on a person of an impure birth; so that the <em>Whalliaru</em> and <em>Madigaru</em> must content themselves with praying to God for his blessing to avert evil, or bestow good. This however not being satisfactory, these poor people frequently attack the <em>Bráhmans</em> for an <em>Upadésa</em>. In order to be quit of their importunity, the <em>Bráhmans</em> sometimes tell them the name of any god, the constant muttering of which pleases the man much better, than the offering up his requests to the deity in the pure language of the heart. So powerful is the influence of ceremony over that of reason.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 8.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In their judicial capacity the <em>Gurus</em> possess great authority. They take cognizance of all omissions of ceremonies, and actions that are <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-181" data-page="181" data-folio="147" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n180_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 147">[p. 147]</button> contrary to the rules of cast. Small delinquencies they punish by pouring cow-dung and water on the head of the guilty person, by fine, and by whipping. For great offences they excommunicate the culprit; which is done by shaving his head. This excludes a man from all society, even from that of his nearest connections; for his very wife would incur a similar punishment by giving him any assistance. The excommunication may be removed by the <em>Guru</em>; in which case he purifies the repentant sinner by a copious draught of cow’s urine. Though the deputies have no proper authority to punish delinquents, yet they frequently make people voluntarily submit to their correction. They threaten any person to send a complaint to his <em>Guru</em> of some crime laid to his charge, and an order to proceed to the residence of the <em>Guru</em> to answer the complaint. Most persons, however, choose to submit to whatever the deputy dictates, rather than undertake the trouble of a long journey; at the end of which they might be more severely punished by the <em>Guru</em>, than they would have been at home by the deputy.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">When a <em>Guru</em> is accused of any misdemeanor, he is called before a <em>Trimatustaru</em>, or assembly of the most eminent <em>Vaidika Bráhmans</em> of all the three sects, who have the power of inflicting six different punishments, all of which are very severe.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 9. <em>Chenapattana Rajas.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">9th <em>June.</em>—I went to <em>Chinapatam</em>, or <em>Chenapattana</em>, which was formerly the residence of a <em>Polygar</em> family called <em>Jacadéva Rayas</em>. They were <em>Teliga Banijigaru</em>, and seem to have risen into power about five centuries ago. They continued till very lately possessed of considerable territories; and were reduced by the <em>Mysore Rajas</em>, no long time before these, in their turn, became subject to the Mussulmans. The direct heir of the family, in the male line, now resides here in great poverty; and, being a petty trader, is called <em>Jiva Raja Chitty</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manufacture of glass.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Glass-ware is one of the manufactures of this place. It is made by two operations. In the first, from the raw materials, are formed masses of glass; in the second, these masses are wrought up into small bottles, and ornamental rings for the arms of women.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-182"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="182" data-folio="148" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n181_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 148">[p. 148]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. In doing this, a girl seldom escapes without drawing blood, and rubbing part of the skin from her hand: and as every well-dressed girl has a number of rings on each arm, and as these are frequently breaking, the poor creatures suffer much from their love of admiration: but in the female breast, this is a more powerful motive than the dread of any common pain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Soda, or fossile alkali.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 9.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>soda</em>, or fossile alkali, is found in the soil near <em>Madura</em>, and at <em>Gutalu</em>, a town east from <em>Mundium</em>. In the hot season the glass-makers go to these places, and prepare as much as they want. They collect the <em>Soulu Munnu</em>, or saline earth, into heaps; and near these <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-187" data-page="187" data-folio="151" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n186_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 151">[p. 151]</button> dig three pits, which I shall call A, B, and C. The pit A in the center is a square of four cubits in extent, and one foot in depth. The pit B is circular, three and a half cubits in diameter, and two and a half in depth. The pit C is a small circular cavity capable of containing four or five pots of water, and communicates with the pit A by means of a small channel, which can be occasionally shut up with clay. When all things are ready, the pit B is filled nearly with <em>Soulu Munnu</em>, and then is added a little water, which is mixed well with the saline earth, so as to form thin mud. This is then put into the pit A for two hours; when the earth subsides, and leaves a clear water impregnated with alkali, which is allowed to run off into the pit C. Some of this water is then put into a fourth pit, and mixed with cow-dung. The mixture is allowed to putrify for three days, and is then spread thin on forty mats by means of the twig of a tree, which retains its leaves. When dry, this forms a very thin crust on the mats, which are then laid on the ground, and exposed to the sun. Every day afterwards these mats are sprinkled with fifty pots of the clear brine from the pit C, procured as before mentioned. This is continued for twenty days; when a cake of <em>soda</em>, about half an inch in thickness, is formed all over the mats. It is very impure, and sells for two <em>Paissas</em> the <em>Cucha Seer</em>, or 13<em>s.</em> 9¼<em>d.</em> the hundred weight. It is used for washing, and for making glass. I shall preserve his words, only reducing the weights and prices to the English standard.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 9.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">“ The mode of preparing country steel, before it is drawn into wire, is by taking any quantity, and heating it in a charcoal fire <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-188" data-page="188" data-folio="152" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n187_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 152">[p. 152]</button> until it be red hot; when it is taken out, beaten into a long thin plate upwards of an inch in breadth, and rolled up into an oval or round form, leaving a small space between each of the folds. It is then put into the fire again, well heated, and hammered out as before. This process is repeated eight times, by which the weight of the steel is reduced to one fifth of the original quantity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“When this is done, it is ready for being formed into wire, and is again heated, and beat into small square rods. It is heated again, and drawn through a small hole, in a plate of common steel, into wire by means of pincers. In this plate there are several holes, of various dimensions, for the purpose of gradually reducing the wire to the size required.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“After it has been once drawn, it is necessary to heat it again, before it can be drawn a second time, which is done through a hole somewhat smaller than the former one. It afterwards requires no farther heat; but is drawn eight or ten times more until it be sufficiently fine; and this is partly ascertained by the sound it gives, when struck by the finger on being stretched out.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“At the time of drawing it through the plates, a small quantity of oil is applied to it, to make it pass easily.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“The length of time taken up in making four pieces of wire, nearly of the same weight, and as fine as the larger of the specimens” (its weight 169 grains, its fineness 2 grains a foot), “from the time of commencing the preparation of the steel, will occupy one man four days. One <em>Rupee</em> weight (177 grains) of this wire sells for one <em>Sultany Fanam</em> (<em>8d.</em>). The price of the smaller one is double the other, being much finer.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“The steel of the <em>Bazar</em>, or market, is sold at the rate of one <em>Seer</em> for one small silver <em>Fanam</em> (or near 3¼<em>d.</em> a pound). When refined, and ready for being made into wire, one <em>Seer</em> might be purchased for eight <em>Sultany Fanams</em> (or 2<em>s.</em> 1½<em>d.</em> a pound); but there being no demand for it, it has no sale.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 10.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">10th <em>June.</em>—I passed this day in examining the forests of this <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-189" data-page="189" data-folio="153" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n188_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 153">[p. 153]</button> neighbourhood; but shall defer giving an account of the particulars, till I have visited some other parts of the same chain of woody hills, and shall be thus enabled to give at once a view of the whole. Owing to the badness of the soil in the hills near <em>Chinapatam</em>, few of the trees come to great perfection.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 11.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Palm gardens.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">11th <em>June.</em>—I remained at <em>Chinapatam</em>, taking an account of the palm gardens in the vicinity. A tract of land runs near this from <em>Madura</em> towards <em>Magadi</em>, which is about eighteen miles in length, and varies from one mile to a quarter of a mile in width. The whole of this, except a few barren spots intersecting it, is planted with coco-nut and betel-nut palms. All this ground might be cultivated for rice, and has a supply of water from reservoirs; but the plantations are much more valuable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Areca</em>, or betel-nut palm.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The betel-nut palm, or <em>Areca</em>, called by the Mussulmans <em>Supári</em>, requires a rich black soil, and is planted in such places only as produce water on digging a well two cubits deep. There are here two varieties of the <em>Areca</em>, the one bearing large, and the other small nuts. The produce of both kinds is nearly equal in value and quantity. The nut of this country is said by the people here to be as good as any brought to market at <em>Madras</em>, that from <em>Coimbetore</em> excepted; but I afterwards found that they were misinformed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manner of forming a betel-nut garden.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The following account was given me, by the proprietors, of the manner in which they make a new betel-nut garden.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A plot of ground, having been selected for a nursery, is dug to the depth of one cubit. When the seed is ripe, which happens between the 15th of January and the 13th of February, trenches must be formed in the nursery a span broad, and a cubit deep. The trenches are half filled up with sand, on the surface of which is placed a row of the ripe betel-nuts. These are again covered with five inches of sand, and two inches of rich black mould, and watered once in three days for four months, at which time they are fit for being transplanted into the garden.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The garden having been fenced with a hedge of <em>Euphorbium</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-190" data-page="190" data-folio="154" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n189_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 154">[p. 154]</button> <em>Tirucalli</em>, or <em>Jatropha Curcas</em>, is dug to the depth of a cubit at the same time with the nursery, and planted with rows of plantain-trees at the distance of three cubits. When the young palms are fit for being transplanted, the garden must be dug again to the former depth, and two young <em>Arecas</em> must be set in one hole between every two plantain-trees. When there is no rain, they must have water every third day. When the rainy season commences, a trench must be dug between every third row of trees; that is to say, so as between every trench to form beds, each of which contains two rows of the <em>Areca</em>. These trenches serve to carry off superfluous water, and to bring a supply from the reservoir, when wanted. The garden must be dug twice a year, to keep it clear of weeds. At the end of three years the original plantain-trees are removed, and a row is set in the middle of each bed, and kept up ever afterwards, in order to preserve a coolness at the roots of the <em>Areca</em>. When the betel-nut trees are about five feet high, which requires about five years, they receive no more water than what is given to the plantain-trees, which in dry weather must be watered twice a month. The tree, when five years old, begins to produce fruit, and lives from thirty to forty years.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Each tree pushes out three or four spadices, which from about the 21st of August until the 16th of November, become fit for cutting, at different intervals of twenty or thirty days, one after the other. When the nuts have been cut, the skin is removed with an iron knife, and a quantity is put into a pot with some water, in which it must be boiled till the eyes be separated. The nut is then cut into three or four pieces, and for three or four days dried on mats exposed to the sun, when it becomes fit for sale. Each tree is reckoned to produce yearly the value of one <em>Sultany Fanam</em>, or 8 pence, which is the usual price of two <em>Cucha Seers</em>, or nearly 1⅕ lb. Squirrels are very troublesome, and destroy a great deal of the fruit; but it is considered as sinful to kill them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">These plantations are interspersed with coco-nut, mango, lime, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-191" data-page="191" data-folio="155" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n190_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 155">[p. 155]</button> jack, and <em>Humteca</em> (<em>Spondias dulcis</em>) trees; which add to the shade, and to the freshness of the soil. Under the trees are cultivated ginger, and all kinds of fruits and roots that are called <em>Tarkári</em>; but no greens, or <em>Sopu</em>. The whole, however, is kept in a very slovenly manner; only the fences are good.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The betel-nut that is raised here, is sold to the merchants and shop-keepers for ready money; the farmers never receive any advance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Coco-nut palm.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Chinapatam</em> there are four varieties of the coco-nut; 1st, red; 2d, red, mixed with green; 3d, light green; and 4th, dark green. These varieties are permanent; but, although the red is reckoned somewhat better than the others, they are commonly sold promiscuously. Their produce is nearly the same.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The soil does not answer, unless water can be had on digging into it to the depth of three or four cubits; and in such situations a light sandy soil is the best. The black clay called <em>Eray</em> is the next best soil. The worst is the red clay called <em>Cabbay</em>; but with proper cultivation all the three soils answer tolerably well.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manner of forming a coco-nut garden.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The manner of forming a new coco-nut garden is as follows:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The nuts intended for seed must be allowed to ripen until they fall from the tree; and must then be dried in the open air for a month, without having the husk removed. A plot for a nursery is then dug to the depth of two feet, and the soil is allowed to dry three days. On the <em>Ugadi</em> feast (26th March) remove one foot of earth from the nursery, and cover the surface of the plot with eight inches of sand. On this, place the nuts close to each other, with the end containing the eye uppermost. Cover them with three inches of sand, and two of earth. If the supply of water be from a well, the plot must once a day be watered; but, if a more copious supply can be had from a reservoir, one watering in the three days is sufficient. In three months the seedlings are fit for being transplanted. By this time the garden must have been enclosed, and hoed to the depth of two feet. Holes are then dug, for the reception of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-192" data-page="192" data-folio="156" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n191_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 156">[p. 156]</button> seedlings, at twenty feet distance from each other in all directions; for when planted nearer, they do not thrive. The holes are two feet deep, and a cubit wide. At the bottom is put sand seven inches deep, and on this is placed the nut with the young tree adhering to it. Sand is now put in until it rises two inches above the nut, and then the hole is filled with earth and a little dung. Every day for three years, except when it rains, the young trees must have water. While the trees are young, the garden is cultivated for all kinds of <em>Tarkári</em> stuffs, which serves for weeding. When they have grown up, the ground is ploughed, and cultivated for sugar-cane, betel-leaf, <em>Cara Butta</em> rice, <em>Sesamum</em>, <em>Huts' Ellu</em>, <em>Tadaguny</em>, <em>Carlay</em>, <em>Hessaru</em>, <em>Udu</em>, <em>Huruli</em>, <em>Shamay</em>, <em>Navonay</em>, or <em>Ragy</em>, according as the soil is fitted for either of these crops. Mango and jack trees are also planted in these gardens, but greatly to their prejudice; for no cultivation can be carried on under these trees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The coco-nut palm begins to produce when seven or eight years old, and lives so long that its period of duration cannot readily be ascertained. I was shown some that were said to have been planted by <em>Jacadeva Raya</em>, and the people believe that they will live for a thousand years. Young trees, however, produce most fruit, which comes forward at all seasons of the year. A good tree gives annually a hundred nuts. A few are cut green on account of the juice, which is used as drink; but by far the greater part are allowed to arrive at some degree of maturity, although not to full ripeness; for then the kernel would become useless. The cultivator in general removes both husk and shell, and sells nothing but the kernel to the merchants, as they transport them even so far as to <em>Madras</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mats for thatching huts.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The kernel of the coco-nut enters much into the food of the richer natives, both in its raw state, and dressed after various fashions; and it yields by far the finest oil of <em>India</em>, provided the nut is fresh, and the oil used soon after expression. The husks of the green coco-nuts are sold to the <em>Whalliaru</em> for making ropes, at the rate of two thousand the <em>Sultany Fanam</em> (about 8<em>d</em>.); but the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-193" data-page="193" data-folio="157" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n192_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 157">[p. 157]</button> husk of the ripe nut is not fit for this purpose. At <em>Chinapatam</em>, <em>Tári</em>, or palm wine, is never extracted from the coco-nut tree, as the practice injures its growth. Two old leaves in general fall annually from every tree, and each of these forms two of the mats which are used in thatching huts. These mats sell at sixty for the <em>Sultany Fanam</em>, and are put on as the first coat, which is afterwards covered with grass or straw: but in this neighbourhood thatched roofs are not much esteemed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Merchants from <em>Seringapatam</em>, <em>Bangalore</em>, <em>Colar</em>, <em>Ballapura</em>, <em>Hosso-cotay</em>, and <em>Devund-hully</em>, come here to purchase the produce of these gardens.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures of palm gardens.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although the soil is considered as the property of the government, yet when a man plants a palm garden, the trees are considered as his property, and he may at pleasure sell them. He pays one half of the produce to the government, as ground-rent; but pays nothing for the fruit-trees that are intermixed, nor for the vegetables or grains that are cultivated under them. On this account the proprietors seem to be very careless in planting new trees, in room of those that have died, or that are in a state of decay; for the older and thinner the palm trees are, the less they injure the crops under them. The <em>Amildars</em>, it is alleged, expect presents of the fruit, <em>Jagory</em>, and other articles that are cultivated in these gardens, and that do not pay rent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Palmira</em>, or <em>Borassus flabelliformis.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this vicinity the <em>Palmira</em> tree thrives remarkably well, and is planted in barren dry spots, where the other palms will not succeed. It is only used for <em>Tári</em>, or wine, and that is never distilled, and seldom made into <em>Jagory</em>. Its stem is considered as much better for building than that of the coco-nut.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Chinapatam</em> a family of <em>Linga Banijigaru</em> have the art of making very fine white sugar. The process has always been kept a profound secret by the head of the house, who instructs his successor a short time only before his death. The sugar is made for the sole use of the court, who allow the maker 27 <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Maund</em>, or <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-194" data-page="194" data-folio="158" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n193_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 158">[p. 158]</button> 4<em>l</em>. 3<em>s</em>. 7<em>d</em>. a hundred weight, and furnish him with the juice of the cane, which he boils down, at the furnace of the cultivators, to <sup>3</sup>/<sub>7</sub>ths of the original bulk, and then carries it home to complete the operation. This family is also allowed a village rent-free, as being sugar-makers to the court. Such a miserable monopoly of good things is a favourite practice in the arbitrary governments of <em>Hindustan</em>. Instead of wishing to procure articles of the best quality by giving a high price for what they want, the princes of <em>India</em> are contented with preventing their subjects, by a monopoly, from getting such good things as they can; not reflecting that a prince, by his superiour wealth, can always in the fair market procure better things than his subjects. I examined the head of this family of sugarboilers; but what he said concerning the manner of conducting the operation was evidently false; and I did not think it fair to press him too closely, as a discovery of the art might injure his property.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Both <em>Putta Putti</em> and <em>Restali</em> canes are cultivated, and of both the white sugar can be made; but cane that is raised on a rich soil will not answer for this purpose, as its juice can never be made to granulate.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Taking with me some sensible cultivators, and a <em>Parputty</em>, I shewed them a small plot of cane, and asked how much <em>Jagory</em> it would produce: they said, 400 balls at 1 <sup>3</sup>/<sub>4</sub> <em>Seer</em> each. By measuring the field, and reducing the measures and weights to the English standard, I found this to give 13 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub> hundred weight an acre.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the black soil called <em>Eray</em> the crop-season commences at <em>Sivarátri</em> feast, or on the 12th of February. In the sandy soil called <em>Marulu</em> it begins a few days earlier. By this time all the implements are brought to the works, and the iron boiler is hired from the government at the rate of one <em>Fanam</em> (about 8<em>d</em>.), and one ball of <em>Jagory</em>, a day.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Boiling-house.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The boiling-house is a thatched hut, about forty feet long and twenty broad, with a door in front, but without windows. The walls</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-196"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="196" data-folio="158" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n195_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 158">[p. 158]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Vol. I. p. 159.</em></p></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-023"><img src="/figures/journey-from-madras-vol-i/fig-023_p0196.webp" alt="PLATE VII. — Plan of the upper end of the mortar" loading="lazy"><figcaption><em>PLATE VII.</em> — <em>Plan of the upper end of the mortar</em></figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Sugar-mill of Chinapatam.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Fig. 17.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>view of the mortar</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Vertical section

It is sunk perpendicularly into the earth, leaving one end two feet above the surface. The hollow (b b) is conical, truncated downwards, and then becomes cylindrical (c), with a hemispherical projection (d) in its bottom, in order to allow the juice to run freely to the small opening (e), that conveys it to a spout (f ), from which it falls into an earthen pot. Round the upper mouth of the cone is a circular cavity (g g), which collects any of the juice that may run over from the upper ends of the pieces of cane; and from thence a canal (h h) conveys this juice down the outside of the mortar to the spout (f ).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The beam (i i i) is about sixteen feet in length, and six inches in thickness, and is cut out from a large tree that is divided by a fork <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-198" data-page="198" data-folio="160" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n197_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 160">[p. 160]</button> into two arms. In the fork an excavation is made for the mortar, round which the beam turns horizontally. The surface of this excavation is secured by a semicircle of strong wood. The end towards the forks is quite open, for changing the beam without trouble. On the undivided end of the beam sits the bullock-driver (k), whose cattle are yoked by a rope (l), which comes from the end of the beam; and they are prevented from dragging out of the circle by another rope (m), which passes from the yoke to the forked end of the beam. On the arms a basket (n) is placed to hold the cuttings of cane; and between this and the mortar sits the man (o) who feeds the mill. Just as the pestle comes round, he places the pieces of cane sloping down the cavity of the mortar; and, after the pestle has passed, he removes those which have been squeezed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The lever (p p) is a piece of timber nearly of the same length with the beam. Its thicker and lower end is connected with the undivided end of the beam by the regulator. Some way above its junction with the regulator, a piece of <em>Sujalu</em>, which is a very hard wood, is dovetailed into the lower side of the lever; and in this piece (q) is made a smooth conical hollow, which rests on the head of the pestle. The upper end of the lever is fastened to the two arms of the beam by two ropes (r r).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The pestle (s) is a strong cylindrical piece of timber, about four feet in length. At each end it is cut to a point, so as at the upper end to form a cone, and at the lower a pyramid of from twelve to fifteen sides, surmounted by a short cylinder. The cavity in the lever being towards one end, makes the position of the pestle always oblique; so that as it passes round it rubs strongly against the sides of the mortar. Its cylindrical point rubs on the top of the hemispherical projection (d) that is in the bottom of the cylindrical cavity of the mortar.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The regulator (t) is a strong square piece of timber, which passes through the undivided end of the beam, and is secured below by part of its circumference being left for checks. It is perforated by <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-199" data-page="199" data-folio="161" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n198_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 161">[p. 161]</button> eight holes, in the lowest of which is placed a pin to prevent the regulator from falling when the strain is removed. A pin in one of the upper holes of the regulator, and another in one of the holes in the thick end of the lever, serve to secure in their place the ropes that bind closely together these two parts of the machine. According as these pins are placed higher or lower, the relative direction of all the moveable parts of the machine is altered, and the balance of the beam is so regulated, that it goes round without any friction, but yet with its fork closely applied to the mortar. The only frictions in this machine, it must be observed, are at the two extremities of the pestle; and that which is at the lower end is entirely employed in bruising the cane, which is the object in view; still, however, it is a machine badly contrived for the purpose to which it is applied.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Making <em>Jagory.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">When the works and machinery have been prepared for making <em>Jagory</em>, all the proprietors of sugar-cane in the village assemble, and work together a day at each man's field, in rotation, until the whole is finished. A sufficient number of people bring the canes to a man, who cuts them into pieces about six inches long, and puts them in the square cavity (a) in the boiling-house. From thence one man supplies the basket of the person who feeds the mill, and who is the third man employed at the works. The fourth man drives the bullocks; a fifth carries the juice to the boiler; a sixth attends the fire; and a seventh manages the boiler. The mill goes night and day, and gives 56 pots of juice, containing in all about 218 ale gallons. The bullocks are changed, after having expressed three pots, and do no more work that day, having been obliged to go very fast. Two of them are in the yoke at a time.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cane raised on black mould gives about a fifth part more juice than that produced on sandy soil: but then nine pots of the latter give a hundred balls of <em>Jagory</em>, while it requires twelve, or even fourteen, pots of the former to produce the same quantity. The workmen always put into the boiler as much juice as will yield <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-200" data-page="200" data-folio="162" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n199_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 162">[p. 162]</button> a hundred balls of <em>Jagory</em>. It is strained into the boiler through a cotton cloth, and there is added to it a proper quantity of lime-water. In a boiler full of rich juice, from cane raised on sandy soil, there is put half a <em>Seer</em> of lime-water, or about 34 cubical inches ; and poorer juice from the same kind of soil requires double that quantity. The boiler full of juice from black mould requires five or six <em>Seers</em>, which is added by degrees. The boiler performs his operations three times in the twenty-four hours. When the juice has been evaporated to a proper consistence, it is put into a large pot, and allowed to cool for three hours. It is then poured into the mould, which consists of a long thick plank, in which a hundred holes are formed, each in the shape of a quadrilateral inverted pyramid. The <em>Jagory</em>, or inspissated juice, is allowed to dry in the mould for four hours ; when the plank being turned over, the balls, or rather pyramids of <em>Jagory</em>, fall down. They are dried by placing them on leaves for a day, and are then fit for sale. These balls weigh 1¾ <em>Seer</em>, or 10,617 lb. ; and, if made from cane raised on black mould, sell for about seven balls for the <em>Sultany Fanam</em>, or 8<em>s</em>. 0½<em>d</em>. the hundred weight. If made from cane raised on sandy soil, six balls cost a <em>Fanam</em>, which is at the rate of 11<em>s</em>. 9¾<em>d</em>. a hundred weight. The <em>Jagory</em>, it would thus appear, contains both the sugar and molasses, and is similar to what in <em>Jamaica</em> comes out of the cooler before it is taken to the curing-house. It is, however, somewhat more inspissated ; for which an allowance must be made, if we wish to compare the strength of the sugar-cane juice in the two countries. By the foregoing account it requires about 37 gallons of the best juice to make a hundred weight of <em>Jagory</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">By the account of a man, who came into my tents from another village, twelve pots of juice from a black mould give 165 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Jagory</em> ; which agrees very well with what was told me in the presence of the <em>Parputty</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 11.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The government and the farmers share the produce of <em>Jagory</em> equally. An acre produces 13,<sup>4</sup>/<sub>10</sub> hundred weight, at a medium of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-201" data-page="201" data-folio="163" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n200_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 163">[p. 163]</button> 10<em>s.</em>; the average rent of sugar land is therefore 3<em>l.</em> 6<em>s.</em> 6<em>d.</em> an acre. The farmers allege, that the <em>Amildars</em>, in order to favour the court sugar-maker, who generally supplies them also, take all the juice that is produced on sandy soil, and estimating the quantity of <em>Jagory</em> which would fall to the farmer's share, repay him with <em>Jagory</em> made of cane raised on black mould; but this seems too paltry a kind of imposition to be practised, and shows that they have little real cause of complaint when they mention one so trivial.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 12.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Desolation.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">12th <em>June.</em>—I went to <em>Ráma-giri.</em> A part of the way I had travelled before; but, on coming to the <em>Arkawati</em> river, I turned to the north, and passed through a valley naturally beautiful, but which appeared dismal on account of its having been in a great measure deserted. Near its head I found a few small villages surrounded by a little cultivation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Ráma-giri.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tigers.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 12.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Donays, which supply water.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Since the accession of <em>Tippoo</em>, <em>Ráma-giri</em> has been strangely agitated. The town, which was then considerable, he removed from the west side of the river, and placed close under the hill upon which the fort is built. It was then surrounded by a wall, and some other defences of no great importance. The army of Lord Cornwallis summoned the fort; and the garrison, intimidated by the taking of many strong places which they had seen fall, surrendered without any resistance, and for some time our troops kept possession. After the peace <em>Tippoo</em> dismantled the fort, and now the <em>Amildar</em> has again removed the town to the west side of the river, and placed it lower down than its original situation. During the incursions of Lord Cornwallis the inhabitants were deprived of the means of subsistence, and a large proportion of them perished of hunger. I give this, and other similar accounts of the state of population, from the information of the natives, which I believe is just, and rather partial to the British side, partly from flattery, and partly from their being sensible that they never before were under the protection of a people so humane, just, and powerful. The place is dreadfully infested by tigers, especially the fort, which occupies a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-202" data-page="202" data-folio="164" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n201_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 164">[p. 164]</button> large rocky hill, capable of a very tedious defence, even without any assistance from art. Several <em>Bráhmans</em> reside near the summit, for the place is reputed holy; but it is kept in a very slovenly state. It is plentifully supplied with water from several large cavities, or chasms in the rock, which receive the rain, and by their coolness prevent its sudden evaporation. Such cavities are called by the natives <em>Donays</em>, and in all the rocky hills of this great chain of mountains are very common. In the hottest season they never become dry; but they have no springs to give a supply of fresh water, and of course afford but unwholesome drink.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Granite.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The granite, of which the rock of <em>Ráma-giri</em> consists, is very beautiful, and is composed of small grains of black mica, or perhaps micarelle, and of white glassy quartz, in which are immersed large masses of red felspar. It seems to agree exactly with Mr. Kirwan’s definition of granitic porphyry. It is a very elegant stone, and might be procured in very large masses.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cattle.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Cattle seem to be the principal object of the people of <em>Ráma-giri</em>. On account of the great quantity of prickly bushes, and a number of what they call wild dogs, no sheep can be kept; but there are considerable numbers of goats and cows. The inhabitants seem to be uncommonly cleanly; as they gave me some butter that was free from any bad smell or taste, a circumstance that I never before met with in <em>India</em>, except where the butter was purposely made for the use of an European.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Goats.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Very few wethered goats are kept here, most of the males being sacrificed when young. Three males are reckoned sufficient for a hundred females. The females begin to breed when a year old, and twice annually have a kid. They give milk eight months in the year; that is to say, four months after each kid, and yield daily half a <em>Seer</em>, or about an ale pint. They are killed when eight years old. A young goat, male or female, sells for five or six <em>Fanams</em>, or for 3<em>s</em>. 4¼<em>d</em>. or 4<em>s</em>. 0¼<em>d</em>. The tigers are very destructive to this kind of cattle.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-203"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="203" data-folio="165" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n202_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 165">[p. 165]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 12. Ox kind.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The farmers who are in easy circumstances keep from ten to thirty breeding cows; but the number of cattle has been exceedingly reduced by a most fatal disorder that prevailed among them last year. It is attributed by the natives to a contagion introduced by the <em>Brinjaries</em>, who followed the British army; and no doubt it raged with the utmost violence in the camps before <em>Seringapatam</em>; but a similar disease frequently occurs at the same season, although it was never before known to prove so fatal. I am therefore inclined to believe, that it is rather endemic than contagious. It commenced in April, and lasted three months. The cattle seized by this disease pass bloody, or at least very red, urine, and are reduced by a purging; but, although very few recover, the disease does not kill suddenly; they live under it from two to three months. It is alleged, that last year nine tenths of the whole cattle in this district perished from this cause.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The cows are always kept in a house at night, and by some are littered with straw; but by others this is neglected. At night they always get straw to eat. In the morning, about an hour after sun-rise, all, except the working oxen, are turned out to the woods under the charge of a man, who takes care of fifty head. They are much exposed to tigers, and are not guarded by dogs. At noon they have water, and again in the evening, when they are brought home. The milch cows on this occasion have for their drink the water in which the grain of the family has been boiled.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cow begins to breed at about three years of age, but at no particular season of the year. She goes nine months with calf, and does not breed oftener than once in two or three years. She continues to give milk, until within three months of calving. For the first month the calf has all the milk; afterwards it continues to have a little every day, till the mother becomes dry. A middling good cow gives, twice a day, three quarters of a <em>Seer</em> of milk, or about a pint and a half, besides what the calf draws. A cow lives until she is twenty years of age, but does not breed after sixteen. The idea <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-204" data-page="204" data-folio="166" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n203_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 166">[p. 166]</button> of putting a cow to labour shocks the natives here exceedingly. They cannot hear it mentioned with patience; and relate, with marks of great satisfaction, that for this offence their last <em>Raja</em> put to death several <em>Brinjaries</em>; for it is customary with that people to make cows carry their baggage.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The working oxen begin to plough at sun-rise, and continue until sun-set, with an intermission of three hours at noon; at which time they have a feed of straw, as they have also at night. They have water at noon, at three o'clock, and at sun-set. The cattle bred here are not fit for the road. The richest man of the village keeps a bull or two, as there may be occasion, and these serve all the cows without hire. The best calves are kept for this purpose; and occasionally a good bull is purchased from some distance, to improve the breed. The bull begins to propagate at three years of age, and continues till ten. Those intended for labour are emasculated at three years of age, by bruising the parts between two sticks. They very seldom die in consequence of this operation; but for about fifteen days are in pain from the swelling, and for a month do not begin to work. In the <em>Decan</em>, castration is not practised on any quadruped. The young bulls are frequently wrought, and the ox continues to labour until he is ten or twelve years of age; when, worn out by hunger, fatigue, and bad usage, he dies a premature death, and his carrion is devoured by the <em>Whalliaru</em>. The late emasculation of the cattle intended for labour is done with a view of giving the ox spirit; but I am inclined to think that it produces a great degeneracy in the breed; for the cows, in consequence, are frequently impregnated by the poorest creatures of the herd.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In all diseases of the ox kind, the grand remedy is the actual cautery, applied very fancifully in different places, and to a different extent, according to the supposed nature of the disease. The animal is thrown down, his mouth and legs are tied, and long lines are burned with a hot iron, so as to bring off both hair and skin. Three lines are often thus drawn, on each side, the whole length of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-205" data-page="205" data-folio="167" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n204_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 167">[p. 167]</button> animal's body. Although the killing an animal of this kind is by all <em>Hindus</em> considered as a kind of murder, I know no creature, whose sufferings equal those of the labouring cattle of <em>Hindustan</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Before the fatal disease of last year, the usual price of a middling ox five years old, was from 25 to 30 <em>Fanams</em>, or from 16<em>s</em>. 9½<em>d</em>. to 1<em>l</em>. 0<em>s</em>. 1¾<em>d</em>.; and of a cow, from 20 to 25 <em>Fanams</em>, or from 13<em>s</em>. 5<em>d</em>. to 16<em>s</em>. 9½<em>d</em>. The price of an ox is now 40 <em>Fanams</em>, or 1<em>l</em>. 6<em>s</em>. 10<em>d</em>.; and of a cow, 30 <em>Fanams</em>, or 1<em>l</em>. 0<em>s</em>. 1¾<em>d</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Cad' Eriligaru, or Cat' Chensu.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this hilly tract, there is a race of men called by the other natives <em>Cad' Eriligaru</em>; but who call themselves <em>Cat' Chensu</em>. Here they live in little huts near the villages, and have a small piece of blanket, or cotton cloth, to cover their nakedness. They are reconciled to the other natives, and pay a trifling capitation tax to government. Where the woods are more extensive, they are terrified at the sight of any civilized being, and live absolutely without any clothing, but cover their nakedness with a few leaves. In these forests they dwell in caves, or under bushes, which they make a better shelter from the weather, by adding small branches from other trees. When the civilized part of this tribe go into the woods to visit their relations, or to trade with them, they must throw off their rags, lest they should be mistaken for a villager, in which case none of the <em>Chensu</em> would approach.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The language of the <em>Chensu</em> is a dialect of the <em>Tamul</em>, with occasionally a few <em>Karnata</em> or <em>Telinga</em> words intermixed; but their accent is so different from that of <em>Madras</em>, that my servants did not at first understand what they said. Their original country, they say, is the <em>Animalya</em> forest below the <em>Ghats</em>, which is confirmed by their dialect. Those who live in the villages have taken the <em>Pancham Banijigaru</em> as their chiefs; they trade chiefly with them, and call them their <em>Swámis</em>, or lords; but, although they have learned to invoke the name of <em>Siva</em>, they do not wear the <em>Lingam</em>. Those in the woods have either no religion, or some simple one with which those here are unacquainted. The people of this country attribute <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-206" data-page="206" data-folio="168" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n205_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 168">[p. 168]</button> to the <em>Chensu</em> the power of bewitching tigers; and my <em>Bráhman</em> gravely informed me, that the <em>Chensu</em> women, when they went out to procure food, left their infants in charge of one of these ferocious beasts. The <em>Chensu</em> of course deny their possessing any such power; but allege, that the art is known to another rude tribe named <em>Soligaru</em>, who inhabit the southern <em>Ghats</em> which separate this country from <em>Coimbetore</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Chensu</em> here live upon game, wild roots, herbs, and fruits; and a little grain, which they purchase from the farmers. They are enabled to do this by collecting some drugs, honey, and wax. It is on account of their having the exclusive privilege of collecting these two last articles, that they pay a poll-tax, which is annually fifteen <em>Fanams</em>, or 10<em>s.</em> 0¾<em>d.</em> for each family.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bees.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The bees are of two kinds: one, smaller than our bee, builds its nest on the twigs of trees, and is easily procured; the other is a large bee, which builds in the clefts of rocks, and its honey is obtained with great difficulty. The wax sells at 2½ <em>Seers</em> for the <em>Fanam</em>, or 1<em>l.</em> 8<em>s.</em> 4<em>d.</em> a hundred weight. The honey sells at 2 <em>Seers</em> for the <em>Fanam</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Drugs.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The drugs collected by the <em>Chensu</em> are as follow: <em>Agulusunti</em>, and <em>Hegguntigay</em>, two roots used in medicine. <em>Popli</em>, a bark used as a red dye. The plant that produces it is a scandent shrub, the flower or fruit of which I could never find; nor did Dr. Roxburgh know it by the dry specimen of the branches in leaf. It seems, however, to be nearly related to the <em>Ventilago</em>. The <em>Muddi</em>, or bark of the root of at least two kinds of <em>Morinda</em>, is also used as a dye; as is likewise the <em>Capily Podi</em>. It is the red dust shaken from the fruit of the <em>Rotleria tinctoria</em>. The merchants of <em>Bangaluru</em> and <em>Colar</em> buy up these articles, paying to the <em>Chensu</em> a <em>Fanam</em> for 32 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Popli</em>, and <em>Muddi</em>, or 3<em>s.</em> 10½<em>d.</em> a hundred weight, and a <em>Fanam</em> for one <em>Seer</em> of <em>Capily Podi</em>, or 1<em>s.</em> 1¼<em>d.</em> a pound.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 12.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When ordered, the <em>Chensu</em> collect gum from various trees; but they never do it without a special commission, and the quantity <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-207" data-page="207" data-folio="169" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n206_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 169">[p. 169]</button> that they could procure is inconsiderable. The beasts are killed with a match-lock, which the farmers give to the <em>Chensu</em>, with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-208" data-page="208" data-folio="170" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n207_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 170">[p. 170]</button> powder and ball, on condition of receiving a part of the game. The <em>Chensu</em> possess no domestic animals.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Lac.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Lac</em> is produced in several of the neighbouring hills, upon the tree called <em>Jala</em>, which seems to be of the same genus with the <em>Shorea</em> of Gærtner, and this is probably not different from the <em>Vatica</em> of Linnæus. The tree is never planted, but grows naturally; and the persons who rent the <em>Lac</em> carry the insect from one tree to another. The tree grows to a large size; and there are a great many, on which no insects have been put. The <em>Chensu</em> and <em>Woddar</em> are the persons who commonly rent it; but they allege, that they are discouraged from the employment, by the want of leases for a number of years. <em>Stick-lac</em> sells here at three <em>Fanams</em> for the <em>Maund</em> of 40 <em>Seers</em>, or 9<em>s.</em> 4½<em>d.</em> a hundred weight.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 13. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">13th <em>June.</em>—I went to <em>Magadi</em>, which in our maps is called <em>Maghery.</em> This stage was very fatiguing for my cattle; and the road passed through a wild but romantic country, which consists of low hills, intermixed with little cultivated vallies. The soil of these is tolerably good; and, like the <em>Ráma-giri</em> valley, they are cultivated with dry grains only. The higher parts are covered with trees, which, owing to the poverty of the soil, are in most places very small; but near <em>Savana-durga</em>, and in a few other parts, the timber and <em>Bamboos</em> grow to a good size. The summits of all the ridges of hills are bare rocks of the granitic porphyry, and often rise into high sharp peaks, or immense masses of naked stone. In order to assist the imagination, I give rude sketches (Fig. 18 and 19) of the longitudinal and transverse sections. In the front, the mound (1) is twenty-two inches high, and three feet broad. Above this is placed a large tile (16); and the empty spaces between this and the mound (1) are filled up with moist clay (17). It is an immense bare rock, which has many fortifications on its summit. A lower rock, but yet one of great strength, is fortified, and is situated at the base of the larger, towards the small river, which runs in a very deep ravine; and a large space between the ravine and rock is also enclosed by a stone wall, and surrounded by thick forests. This place formerly contained several temples, and some large gardens belonging to <em>Magadi Kempa Gauda</em>; and served as a place of refuge for the inhabitants of all the neighbouring country, who in case of invasion retired hither with their grain and cattle. A few families of <em>Bráhmans</em> remain near the ruinous temples; and the site of the gardens is evident from a number of fruit and flowering trees. Every other part of the enclosure is overgrown with forest trees and Bamboos.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Magadi Kempa Gauda.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Magadi Kempa Gauda</em>, or the red head-man of <em>Magadi</em>, was a wealthy farmer, who, having gathered together a number of followers, built at his native village two large temples, and the fortress of <em>Savana-durga</em>, and became a <em>Polygar</em> of great distinction; as he possessed also <em>Ráma-giri</em>, and a great extent of the neighbouring hilly tract. About five years ago, <em>Tippoo</em>, with his usual policy of removing every monument of <em>Hindu</em> government, destroyed <em>Magadi</em>, and forced the inhabitants to settle in a new town, which he erected in the woods, and called <em>Ali-nagar</em>. The people are now deserting this place, and returning to their old abode in <em>Magadi</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Forests.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the hollow ground near the river are some of the best forests in the country, the trees growing to a considerable size. The cattle of the inhabitants never go into them; nor can any one cut the timber without an order from government. Much of the lower land in this forest might be cleared and cultivated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 15. <em>Baydaru.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Throughout these hills, which extend northward from <em>Capaladurga</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-219" data-page="219" data-folio="179" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n218_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 179">[p. 179]</button> are many cultivated spots, in which, during <em>Tippoo’s</em> government, were settled many <em>Baydaru</em>, or hunters, who received twelve <em>Pagodas</em> (4<em>l</em>. 5<em>s</em>.) a year, and served as irregular troops whenever required. Being accustomed to pursue tigers and deer in the woods, they were excellent marksmen with their match-locks, and indefatigable in following their prey ; which, in the time of war, was the life and property of every helpless creature that came in their way. During the wars of <em>Hyder</em> and his son, these men were chief instruments in the terrible depredations committed in the lower <em>Carnatic</em>. They were also frequently employed with success against the <em>Polygars</em>, whose followers were men of a similar description. At present, as they receive no pay, they are obliged to apply more closely to agriculture ; for in that way they always employed their leisure; and there is a prospect of their becoming a quiet and industrious people, although they still retain their arms, and an anxious desire for plunder.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 16. Iron mines at <em>Ghettipura.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 17.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 17.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">16th <em>June</em>.—I remained at <em>Magadi</em>, endeavouring to complete my collection of the various timber trees. I sent also to <em>Ghettipura</em> to inquire after the iron mines : but was informed by the officers of government, that, nobody having wrought them for some years, their situation was not now known. After a long search, however, they had found a few stones, which they sent, believing that they might be iron-ore. I then sent for the man who had given me the information ; and on the following day, 17th <em>June</em>,—I took him along with me to <em>Ghettipura</em>, where I not only found the ore in several places, but also the pits, from which the people were then actually taking it to supply their furnaces. I am at some loss to account for this desire of concealment relative to minerals, which also extends to every kind of quarry throughout the country, and which equally pervades the officers of government and the other inhabitants. Men, who have given me apparently correct information relative to their farms, have eagerly denied a knowledge of the fossile kingdom, which they no doubt possessed, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-220" data-page="220" data-folio="180" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n219_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 180">[p. 180]</button> and for which denial I can assign no plausible motive. The late Sultan, indeed, is said to have harassed his subjects exceedingly, by making them work at quarries, and also to have been very severe on the smelters of iron; and the people may have suspected, that my inquiries might lead to similar oppressions; but according to the iron-smelters’ own account, the Sultan gave them a high price for their iron, and by his great demand afforded them constant employment. It is probable, however, that he compelled them to work much harder than they were inclined to do, and that they were defrauded by those who were entrusted with the payment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Much steel was formerly made at <em>Ghettipura</em>, from whence it derives its name, which signifies literally <em>hard town</em>. It is a small village situated by the compass W. S. W. from <em>Savana-durga</em>, and is distant from <em>Magadi</em> about seven miles. Near it are many cultivated fields intermixed with low rocky hills. The ore is found both in the fields and hills.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The iron ore of the fields consists of small irregular masses separated by thin layers of earthy matter, and is found in beds that are from five to ten feet deep, which have only been wrought in a few places, where they come so near the surface that they have been discovered by the plough. It is probable, that by digging deep they might be found to be of great extent. The small masses are easily beaten into powder, and then the black sand is readily separated, by washing, from the clay and sand that are the other ingredients in their composition. This ore is of two kinds; one efflorescing into red ochre, the other into yellow. Intermixed with both these kinds of ore, which are called female stones, are many lumps of what the natives call male stone. It appears to me to be composed of the same materials with the female stone, but is so hard, that the imperfect manipulations of the natives cannot reduce it to a powder, and of course they cannot separate the earthy matter. It is, therefore, looked upon as useless, fluxes being totally unknown to the miners of <em>Mysore</em>. The female stone appears to me to be the male in a state of decay.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-221"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="221" data-folio="181" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n220_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 181">[p. 181]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 17.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The iron ore of the hills is also male and female; the latter being the only one used; and this is also, in my opinion, the male in a state of dissolution. The male stone in the hills bears a much larger proportion to the female, than it does in the fields. This ore also is found on digging a very little depth into the soil, and seems to be the source from whence most of the black sand of the country is washed by the rain. It appears to me to differ from the quartz impregnated with iron, which I mentioned in the account of the <em>Pedda Náyakana durga Ghats</em>, only by containing a larger quantity of metal. The female stone is very easily reduced to a powder; and the iron sand is readily separated, by washing, from the quartzose sand, which is the other ingredient in the ore. It is not so rich in metal as the ore found in the fields. These two ores are called <em>Aduru Cullu</em>, or stones containing iron sand.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On the surface of the hills is found another iron ore called <em>Ipanada</em>, which is scattered among the gravel in small lumps, from the size of an egg downwards. They are a pure ore, and are put in the furnace without any preparation, except breaking the larger pieces into bits about the size of a filbert. The quantity of <em>Ipanada</em> required for one furnace is exactly the same, by measure, as that of <em>Aduru</em>; but the weight of <em>Ipanada</em> is of course less, there being more space occupied by interstices, from the greater size of the pieces. The produce of iron from both is the same. The surface of the lumps of <em>Ipanada</em> is often covered with a kind of black enamel.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 18. It may be improved, however, by soaking it in water for some months after it has been cut.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Hadaga.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A small tree; but its timber is used for furniture, door frames, and other purposes which require strong materials.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Mitly, Trophis aspera</em> Koenigii.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A small tree; but its timber is much esteemed on account of its being hard, and taking a good polish.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Easy, Premna tomentosa.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Reported to be bad timber; but apparently without foundation. It is put as a frame work in the middle of mud walls in order to give them strength.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Bewu, Melia azadirachta.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A large timber tree, that is much used here, and from which a gum exudes.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Mara halay, Nerium tinctorium</em>, Roxb. MSS.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 19.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The natives are acquainted with its dyeing quality. Is a small tree, but makes beams, planks, and posts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Elichi, Rhamnus jujuba</em>, L. Grows crooked; but its timber is hard, and is used for small furniture.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Heb Hessary, Uvaria tomentosa</em>, Roxb. A small tree that is also used for furniture.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Chica Hessary, Uvaria cerasoides</em>, Roxb. Useless.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Timbu Bayla, Ægle marmelos</em>, Roxb. A large tree, producing strong timber.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Nai Bayla, Limonia crenulata</em>, Roxb. Useless.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Bideru, Bambusa.</em> The <em>Bamboo</em> here is divided into two kinds : one solid, or nearly so, and called by the natives <em>Chittu</em> ; the other hollow, and called <em>Doda.</em> They are not considered as distinct species, the solidity of the former being attributed to its slow growth in dry stony places. Not having had an opportunity of examining the fructification, I cannot determine how far this opinion is well founded. It is the only kind found among these hills ; and, although not of great size, is very strong and heavy. For common purposes I do not think it so useful as the hollow kind : but it is admirably adapted for the shafts of spears, and by <em>Tippoo</em> was applied to that use for his cavalry.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Muruculu, Chirongia glabra</em>, Buch. MSS. In many parts, and especially near <em>Chinapatam</em>, this is the most common tree. Its wood is not much valued ; but it produces large quantities of a dark-coloured gum. The fruit is esculent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Hulu Muruculu, Antidesma alexiteria.</em> Of no use.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-230"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="230" data-folio="190" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n229_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 190">[p. 190]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. A small tree, that produces very hard timber fit for bolts, and small beams.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Narwully</em>, <em>Cordia monoica</em>, Roxb. Ropes are made of its bark. The fruit is esculent, but tasteless.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Cambi</em>, <em>Gardenia</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Hay Cambi</em>, <em>Gardenia latifolia</em>, Roxb. The dried stems answer excellently for match, as they burn slowly, and without flame.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Gheru</em>, <em>Anacardium semecarpus</em>. A beautiful but useless tree.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Nelli</em>, <em>Phyllanthus emblica</em>. The timber is bad, yet the poor use it for beams and rafters. The use of the fruit, in cleaning water, is known to the natives.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It must be observed, that the account I have given of the qualities of the timber trees is derived from the natives. I have had no opportunity yet of ascertaining their nature by experiments: but I have procured specimens of most of them; and from these specimens their real qualities may be hereafter determined. For this purpose, they have been transmitted to the Honourable Court of Directors, in whose Museum they have been deposited.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 20. Appearance of the country.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">20th <em>June</em>,—In the morning I went to <em>Taveri-caray</em>, by a road passing the whole way through woods. I saw only one small village, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-231" data-page="231" data-folio="191" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n230_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 191">[p. 191]</button> which was occupied by iron smelters, and surrounded by a little cultivated land. The country round <em>Taveri-caray</em> is well cleared, and seems to have suffered little from the wars.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Two wild animals of the dog kind.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It is said, that in the great forests round <em>Savana-durga</em>, there is a small animal called the <em>Shin-Nai</em>, or red dog, which fastens itself by surprise on the neck of the tiger, and kills him. On this account the tiger is not so common in these large forests, as in the smaller woods. The <em>Shin-Nai</em> is quite distinct from the wild dog, which is said to be very common here, to grow to a large size, and to be very destructive to sheep. By this wild dog the natives probably mean the wolf. I have seen native drawings of the <em>Shin-Nai</em>, which appear to represent an animal not yet described.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">June 21. Face of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">21st June.—I went from <em>Taveri-caray</em> to <em>Bangalore</em>. Much of the country is covered with bushes, and consists of a very poor soil. The greater part of the arable lands near <em>Bangalore</em> are cultivated : but at some distance from it many fields are waste, owing to a want of people.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Irrigation.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER III. June 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reservoirs are numerous, but small ; many of them are designed for supplying cattle with drink, and not for cultivation, and are of the kind called <em>Cuttay</em>. The tank formed, like those in <em>Bengal</em>, by digging a square cavity into the ground, is here called <em>Gunta</em>. Above the <em>Ghats</em>, however, this manner of procuring water is not very common ; but the most usual manner of coming at a spring is by digging a large square pit with sides almost perpendicular, and called <em>Boudy</em>. The workmen dig till they find the water, which is often twenty or thirty feet from the surface. Afterwards a narrow passage, with a gentle slope, is cut in one of the sides, and a stair is formed in it, by which the women descend to bring up the water in earthen pots. It is from these wells, chiefly, that water is drawn by the <em>Capily</em>, or leather bag wrought by two bullocks descending on an inclined plane. When applied to this use, the <em>Boudy</em> is called <em>Capily Bamy</em>. If the water rise so near the surface, that it can be thrown out, to irrigate the land, by two men swinging a basket fixed to <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-232" data-page="232" data-folio="192" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n231_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 192">[p. 192]</button> ropes, the <em>Boudy</em> is then called <em>Guday Bamy</em>. Narrow wells, such as are commonly dug in Europe, are seldom used in <em>Mysore</em>, except for the <em>Yatam</em>, and rarely supply the inhabitants with drink.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Aletris nervosus.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The leaves of the <em>Aletris nervosus</em>, Roxb: are used here for making cordage. Before they are beaten to separate the fibres, they are steeped in water fifteen days, in order to rot the useless parts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On this day’s journey I observed the cultivation of a kind of sugar-cane called <em>Maracabo</em>, or stick cane. This kind never grows thicker than the finger, and is very hard, and unproductive of juice; but it requires less water than the <em>Restali</em>. It seems to have been the original sugar-cane of the <em>Colar</em> district, of which all the country on this side of the central chain of hills forms a part. The farmers have lately introduced the <em>Puttaputti</em> from the lower <em>Carnatic</em>, and are extending its cultivation as fast as they can procure cuttings.</p></div>
