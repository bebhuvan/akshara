July 18.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">T<span class="sc">HE</span> 18th July I went two cosses to <em>Burra</em>, <em>Pedda</em>, <em>Doda</em>, or Great <em>Bala-pura</em>, as it is called in the Mussulman, <em>Telinga</em>, <em>Karnata</em>, and English languages. All the country through which I passed has formerly been under cultivation; but now it is almost entirely unoccupied.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Doda Bala-pura.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">On the dissolution of the <em>Vijaya-nagara</em> kingdom, <em>Náráyana Swámi</em>, the <em>Polygar</em> of <em>Bala-pura</em>, assumed independency; and in the fort, remains of his castle, surrounded as usual by temples, may still be traced. On the invasion by the <em>Mogul</em> army under <em>Cossim Khán</em>, the <em>Polygar</em> was obliged to give up this open part of his country, and to retire to <em>Chica Bala-pura</em>, situated nearer his strong-holds. <em>Doda Bala-pura</em> formed then one of the seven districts of the <em>Sira</em> government; but it was soon wrested from the Mussulmans by the <em>Marattahs</em>. On their decline again, after the battle of <em>Pan</em>-<em>niput</em>, it was seized by the <em>Nizam</em>, who gave it as a <em>Jaghir</em>, or feu, to <em>Abbass Khuli Khán</em>, a native of the place. He enlarged the fort to its present size, made very good gardens after the Mussulman fashion, and built a palace with all conveniencies suitable to his rank. On the growth of <em>Hyder's</em> power, however, he was under the necessity of giving up the place without resistance; but not choosing to enter into that adventurer's service, whom he considered as his inferior in rank, he returned with his children into the lower <em>Carnatic</em>, and entered into the service of the <em>Nabob</em> of <em>Arcot</em>. One of his wives and her grandson refused to follow him; and these live now in the fort upon a small pension that was granted them by <em>Hyder</em>, and which has been continued by the Company.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-395"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="395" data-folio="347" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n394_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 347">[p. 347]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 18.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The fort, considering that it is built entirely of mud, is very large, and very strong. All within, as usual, is a sad heap of rubbish and confusion. The <em>Assur Khana</em> of <em>Abbass Khuli Khán</em> is however a handsome building. In this kind of temple the Mussulmans of the <em>Decan</em>, infected by the superstition of their neighbours, worship <em>Allah</em> under the form of a human hand, painted on a board between two figures that represent the sun and moon.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">One side of the fort is surrounded by gardens; and the other three sides by the town of <em>Bala-pura</em>, which contains 2000 houses, and is fortified with a mud wall and hedge. In this town was born <em>Meer Saduc</em>, the detestable minister of the late <em>Sultan</em>. He adorned his native place by a garden, which, together with that of <em>Abbass Khuli Khan</em>, is kept up by the <em>Rája</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">19th and 20th <em>July</em>.—I remained at <em>Doda Bala-pura</em>, making some inquiries.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the Gollaru.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Gollaru</em>, or, as they are called in their own language, the <em>Gollawanlu</em>, are a tribe of <em>Telingana</em> descent, and must be distinguished from the <em>Cadu</em>, or <em>Carridy Goalaru</em>, who keep cattle; with whom they neither eat in common, nor intermarry. They are one of the tribes of <em>Súdra</em>, whose duty it is to cultivate the ground, and to act as the village militia. This cast has, besides, a particular duty, the transporting of money, both belonging to the public and to individuals. It is said, that they may be safely intrusted with any sum; for, each man carrying a certain value, they travel in bodies numerous in proportion to the sum put under their charge; and they consider themselves bound in honour to die in defence of their trust; of course, they defend themselves vigorously, and are all armed; so that robbers never venture to attack them. They have hereditary chiefs called <em>Gotugaru</em>, who with the usual council settle all disputes, and punish all transgressions against the rules of cast. The most flagrant is the embezzlement of money intrusted to their care. On this crime being proved against any of the cast, the <em>Gotugaru</em> applies to the <em>Amildar</em>, or civil magistrate, and, having <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-396" data-page="396" data-folio="348" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n395_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 348">[p. 348]</button> obtained his leave, immediately causes the delinquent to be shot. Smaller offences are atoned for by the guilty person giving an entertainment. In cases of adultery, the chief collects four elders, who admonish the woman to a more decent conduct. If she be repentant, the husband takes her back ; but if she be impudent, he divorces her. After the age of puberty the girls continue to be marriageable, and a man may marry as many of them as he can maintain, or procure ; for the former is not difficult, the women being very industrious, both in the field and in spinning. They are divided into several families, <em>Mutsarlu</em>, <em>Beinday</em>, <em>Molu</em>, <em>Sadalawanlu</em>, <em>Perindalu</em>, and <em>Toralay</em>. These are like the <em>Gótrams</em> of the <em>Bráhmans</em> ; the intermarriage of two persons of the same family being considered as incestuous. They call the proper god of the cast <em>Krishna Swámi</em>, who is one of the incarnations of <em>Vishnu</em> ; and they allege, that he was born of their cast both by father's and mother's side. The <em>Bráhmans</em> allege, that the mother of this great warrior was of the <em>Goala</em>, or cow-keeper cast ; in which, perhaps, they are well founded ; and they pretend, that a <em>Bráhman</em> condescended to impregnate her, which is not improbable. The <em>Gollawanlu</em> offer sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>. They pray to <em>Kála Bhairava</em> (terrific time) ; but the women do not appease his wrath by sacrificing their fingers, like the female <em>Morasu</em> above described. They think, that after death good men become a kind of gods ; and they offer sacrifices to these spirits : bad men become devils. They know nothing of transmigration. They bury the dead, and sometimes take the vow of <em>Dáseri</em>. They are allowed to eat animal food, and to drink spirituous liquors. Although their <em>Guru</em> wears the <em>Linga</em>, they do not. He is a <em>Jangama</em>, named <em>Malaiswara Swámi</em>, who lives at <em>Mapákálí Conda</em>, about 14 miles north from hence. On his followers he bestows holy water ; and for every marriage accepts of a <em>Fanam</em>, although he does not attend the ceremony. This tribe seems not to be much attached to any sect ; as its members also take holy water from the <em>Gurus</em> of the <em>A'ayngar Bráhmans</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-397" data-page="397" data-folio="349" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n396_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 349">[p. 349]</button> and bestow on those persons charity in money and grain. At their marriages, at the new moons, at births, and at the <em>Todanu</em>, as the annual commemoration of the death of their parents is called in the <em>Telinga</em> language, the <em>Panchánga</em>, or village astrologer, reads prayers (<em>Mantrams</em>), which are by them reckoned of great efficacy, as they are in a language which they do not understand.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Cunsa</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Cunsa Woculigaru</em> are a tribe of <em>Súdra</em> of <em>Karnata</em> descent, who are properly cultivators, and who formed a part of the <em>Candashara</em>, or native militia. Their hereditary chiefs are called <em>Gaudas</em>, whether they are head-men of villages or not. The <em>Gauda</em> by excommunication, or by the mulct of an entertainment, settles disputes, and punishes transgressions against the rules of cast. In cases of adultery, the head-man, assisted by his council, inquires into the matter. If the man has been of the same cast, the adulteress is only reprimanded, the husband of course retaining the power of giving her corporal punishment, although he rarely proceeds to such extremities; but if the man has been of a strange cast, the adulteress is excommunicated. They can all intermarry, and the men are allowed to take several wives. The women are very industrious spinners, and labourers in the field, and continue to be marriageable after the age of puberty. Widows ought to be buried alive with their husband’s bodies; and some of the more strict people regret that the custom has become entirely obsolete. They are allowed to eat animal food, but not to drink spirituous liquors. Some of them can read, and write accompts. They all worship the <em>Saktis</em>, by sacrificing animals, which they afterwards eat. They believe, that after death the spirits of good men become a kind of gods, and, by sending dreams, warn men of what is to happen. Bad men, after death, become devils, but have no power over the living. To the sainted spirits they offer sacrifices. Some of them take the vow of <em>Dáseri</em>, and some pray to <em>Dharma Rája</em>. The <em>Panchánga</em>, or village astrologer, reads <em>Mantrams</em> to them at marriages and births, and in some places attends at the annual <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-398" data-page="398" data-folio="350" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n397_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 350">[p. 350]</button> commemoration of their parents’ death; but in other places those who have taken the vow of <em>Dáseri</em> attend at this ceremony. They are divided into two religions. One sect worships <em>Siva</em>: these do not wear the <em>Linga</em>; but their <em>Guru</em> is a <em>Lingabanta Einaru</em>, called <em>Nanjaya</em>, who lives near <em>Colar</em>: he comes occasionally, distributing holy water, and accepting charity. The other sect worships <em>Vishnu</em>, and follows the hereditary chiefs of the <em>A’ayngar</em>, who on their occasional visits distribute holy water, and accept of charity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Lali</em>-<em>Gundaru.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Lali-Gundaru</em> deny their being <em>Súdras</em>, and say that they are <em>Linga Banijigas</em>; but that race will neither eat in their houses, nor give them their girls in marriage. They are a tribe of <em>Karnataka</em> descent. They are farmers, bullock-hirers, gardeners, builders of mud walls, and traders in straw and other small merchandize: but they never take service as <em>Batigaru</em>, or hinds. They have hereditary chiefs called <em>Ijyamánas</em>; who, as usual, with the assistance of a council, settle disputes, and punish transgressions against the rules of cast, by mulcting the offender in an entertainment, or by a temporary excommunication. In cases of adultery, the chief and his council first investigate the business. If they find it proved, that a woman has been guilty of a connexion with a man of a strange cast, the priest (<em>Wodear</em>) is called, and in his presence she is excommunicated; but, if she has only betowed her favours on a man of the cast, her husband turns her away, and she may live with any unmarried person of the cast as a concubine. The men are allowed to have a number of wives; and even after the age of puberty the women continue to be marriageable. The sex are very industrious, both at spinning, and working in the fields. This cast bury the dead; and, although they offer sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>, are not allowed either to drink spirituous liquors, or to eat animal food. They pray to the spirits of good men, thinking that they are the occasion of dreams which foretel future events; but they know not what becomes of the spirits of bad men after death. Some of them are worshippers of <em>Vishnu</em>, and some of <em>Iswara</em>. The <em>Guru</em> of the former <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-399" data-page="399" data-folio="351" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n398_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 351">[p. 351]</button> is a <em>Sri Vaishnavam Bráhman</em> residing at <em>Ahobalum</em>. The <em>Guru</em> of the <em>Siva</em> side lives at <em>Meilar</em>, and is called <em>Linguppa</em>. He wears the <em>Linga</em>, as do also his followers; and he is a <em>Sannyási</em>, but of what kind the people here do not know. In his excursions, which do not happen above once in ten years, he distributes holy water, and receives contributions under the name of charity. It is at their marriages only that the <em>Panchánga</em> reads <em>Mantrams</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Bheri</em> who wear the <em>Linga</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I have already mentioned the customs of the <em>Nagaratras</em>, or <em>Bheri</em>, who worship <em>Vishnu</em>, and of those who worship <em>Siva</em> without wearing the <em>Linga</em>. I had here an opportunity of examining those who wear that indecent badge of their religion. They will neither eat nor intermarry with either of the other two sects; but the whole submit to the authority of the same hereditary chiefs, whatever their religious opinions may be. They say, that all <em>Bheri</em> were formerly of the <em>Vishnu</em> side, and that about five hundred years ago they separated from it. Yet they contend, that even before this secession, they and all other <em>Nagarataru</em> were under the authority of <em>Dharma Siva Achárya</em>, a <em>Smartal Sannyási</em> residing in the lower <em>Carnatic</em>. For this extraordinary circumstance they can assign no reason. This <em>Bráhman</em> at their marriages bestows on them a thread, like that which is worn by the three higher casts; for they pretend to be <em>Vaisyas</em>. For each thread, which ever after marriage they continue to wear, they pay one <em>Fanam</em>. Under the name of <em>Dharma</em> (duty), they also give contributions to this <em>Bráhman</em> whenever he comes to the place. On such occasions he punishes by whip and fine all those who have transgressed against the rules of cast. They are also subject to <em>Muníswara Swámi</em>, a person of their cast, who lives at <em>Baswana-pura</em>, near <em>Cangundy</em>, in the <em>Bára Mahál</em>. He bestows on them the <em>Linga</em>, and an <em>Upadésa</em>; but his power in punishing for delinquencies extends only to fines. The first <em>Muníswara Swámi</em> is believed to have sprung from the earth at <em>Kalyána Pattana</em>; and his successors acknowledge no superiors, but are considered as <em>Iswara</em> in a human form. The office is hereditary, and of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-400" data-page="400" data-folio="352" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n399_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 352">[p. 352]</button> course the <em>Swámi</em> marries. The eldest son, on the death of his father, becomes an incarnation of <em>Siva</em>; while the younger brothers are considered merely as holy men, but follow begging as their profession; for in this country that is esteemed the most honourable employment. They reside in the <em>Matam</em>, or college, with their brother, and accompany him in his travels among the disciples. The daughters of this sacred family never marry persons of lower birth; but when there is a scarcity of women for the use of the men, they condescend to take the daughters of the <em>Emulnaru</em>, who among this sect are a kind of nobility. These do not intermarry with the populace; but they follow lay professions, and are not in exclusive possession of the office of hereditary chief. In the two other sects of this cast, there are no <em>Emulnaru</em>. The <em>Bráhman Guru</em>, and <em>Muníswara Swámi</em>, are considered as of equal rank. The <em>Lingabanta Jangamas</em> are not by this cast considered as their <em>Gurus</em>; but they receive charity, by which a kind of authority is implied. They give nothing to the <em>Dévánga Jangamas</em>. They do not know that <em>Muníswara Swámi</em> is possessed of any books: when he bestows the <em>Linga</em>, he prays extemporarily in the vulgar tongue. At marriages, and the ceremonies which are performed for their deceased parents, the <em>Panchánga</em> and mendicant <em>Bráhmans</em> attend, and read <em>Mantrams</em>. On these occasions the <em>Jangamas</em> also attend, and besides receive the whole profit of births and funerals. They bury the dead, and their widows ought to accompany them in the grave; but this custom has become obsolete. Widows cannot marry again: such an action, indeed, being considered as intolerably infamous, my informers lost all patience when I asked the question. The men are allowed a plurality of wives, but cannot divorce them for any cause except adultery. They are not allowed to eat animal food, nor to drink spirituous liquors.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Farther account of the customs of the <em>Whallias</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I here find, that besides the tradesmen, there are three divisions among the <em>Whalliaru</em>; and that the customs of each differ considerably in different villages, as might be naturally expected among <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-401" data-page="401" data-folio="353" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n400_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 353">[p. 353]</button> a people who have no written rule. There are two tribes of <em>Whallias</em> that speak the language of <em>Karnata</em>; one called simply <em>Karnata</em>, and the other <em>Morasu Whalliaru</em>. These last deny that they have any <em>Guru</em>; but say, that they give presents to the priest at the temple of <em>Kála Bhairava</em>. They offer sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>, to whom they are never <em>Pujáris</em>; and in this place they never take the vow of <em>Dáseri</em>. The <em>Karnata Whalliaru</em> say, that they have a god named <em>Cadri Singuppa</em>, which is one of the names of <em>Vishnu</em>. The <em>Pujári</em> at this idol's temple is a <em>Vaishnavam</em>, and acts as their <em>Guru</em>. He sends annually a deputy to bestow holy water, and receive charity. They also sacrifice to the <em>Saktis</em>. The <em>Teliga Whalliaru</em> call themselves <em>Maliwanlu</em>, and retain the <em>Telinga</em> language. Their religion here is the same with that last mentioned. They have no idea of a future state. They never marry two wives; but, to keep up the family, if the wife has no children, they may take one concubine. Some men do not marry, and these may keep as many concubines as they please. The <em>Gotugaru</em>, or chief of the cast, here, is not an hereditary office, but a person appointed by the <em>Amildar</em> to collect the house-rent. He is a <em>Parriar</em> from the lower <em>Carnatic</em>; for, as I have before observed, the <em>Parriar</em> of the <em>Tamuls</em>, is the same cast with the <em>Whalliaru</em> of <em>Karnata</em>, and the <em>Maliwanlu</em> of <em>Telingana</em>. He settles all disputes; and on all delinquents imposes a mulct of an entertainment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Siva</em> sect of <em>Teliga Dévángas</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Teliga Dévángas</em> of the <em>Siva</em> sect intermarry with those who worship <em>Vishnu</em>; and the wife always adopts the religion of her husband. Even after the age of puberty the women may marry; and, except for adultery, cannot be divorced. Polygamy is allowed to the men, but they do not confine their women. Widows were formerly expected to bury themselves alive with their husbands bodies, but the custom has become obsolete. The people of this cast are allowed to eat animal food; but not to drink spirituous liquors. They offer sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>, and have the same opinion concerning a future life, that the <em>Canara Dévángas</em> entertain. They can read <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-402" data-page="402" data-folio="354" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n401_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 354">[p. 354]</button> and write accompts. Although they do not wear the <em>Linga</em>, they reject the <em>Bráhmans</em> as <em>Gurus</em>, and follow <em>Cari-Baswa Uppa</em>, who admonishes them to wash their heads, and to pray to <em>Iswara</em>. He as usual receives <em>Dharma</em>, or charity, and on every marriage has a small fee. At births, marriages, funerals, new moons, and the annual celebration of their parents’ decease, the <em>Panchánga</em> reads <em>Mantrams</em>; but the <em>Jangamas</em> share in the profits, as on all these occasions they receive charity.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Smartal Bráhmans, and origin of the family distinction called Gótram.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Rishis.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">I found here three <em>Smartal Bráhmans</em>, who were reckoned men of learning. They said, that the sacred cast is divided into at least two thousand tribes, which, from hatred to one another, never intermarry; for they might do so without infringing the rules of cast. It is considered as incestuous for two persons of the same <em>Gótram</em> to intermarry. The origin of the <em>Gótrams</em> is thus explained. The first <em>Bráhmans</em> that sprung from the head of <em>Brahmá</em> when he created mankind are still alive, and are called <em>Rishis</em>. They are endowed with wonderful powers, being able to induce the gods to perform whatever they please. This power they obtained by long fasting and prayer; and they continue to pass their time in these exercises, living in very retired places, and having been very seldom seen, especially in these degenerate days. Each of these <em>Rishis</em> had children, and each became thus the founder of a <em>Gótram</em>; all his descendants in the male line constituting one family. Every <em>Gótram</em> possesses <em>Vaidika</em>, <em>Lokika</em>, and <em>Numbi</em>, or <em>Siva-Brahmana</em>, as this last set are called by the <em>Smartal</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Commerce of Doda Bala-pura.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Merchants from <em>Tadepatry</em>, on the <em>Pennar</em> river, come to <em>Doda Bala-pura</em>, and bring chintses, muslins, turbans, and handkerchiefs: they take away <em>Jagory</em> and cash. The merchants of <em>Saliem</em> bring muslins, cotton cloths with red borders, blue cotton cloths, and turbans: they take away money, with which they repair to <em>Bangalore</em>, and purchase <em>betel-nut</em>. From <em>Naragunda</em>, in the <em>Duab</em>, merchants bring blue cotton cloth, cotton thread, <em>Terra japonica</em>, and dates: they take away <em>Jagory</em> and cash; with which, on the road back <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-403" data-page="403" data-folio="355" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n402_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 355">[p. 355]</button> they purchase coco-nuts. From <em>Chintamony</em>, north from <em>Colar</em>, merchants bring coloured cotton cloths with silk borders, muslins, turbans, and coarse cloths made by the <em>Togotas</em> and <em>Whalliaru</em>; and they take back cash. The manufacturers of the place carry their cloths to <em>Seringapatam</em>. All the cotton wool is imported by merchants from <em>Balahari</em> and <em>Naragunda</em>, who take back <em>Jagory</em> and cash. The commerce of the place is inconsiderable; the traders seem to want enterprise, and never venture from home; but they complain of the want of capital. The <em>Sultan</em>, after having as usual distressed them, by forcing upon them a quantity of goods at an extravagant rate, removed them to a new town which he was building at <em>Nandi-durga</em>; and they had thrown most of their valuable effects into that fortress when it was stormed by the army of Lord Cornwallis. By these misfortunes they are reduced to great poverty. Five years ago, the <em>Sultan</em> having then given up the caprice of a new town, they were allowed to return home.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gardens.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">There are here many kitchen gardens, which pay a higher rent than the ground employed for the cultivation of grain. This soil is rather poor, but the water is near the surface. They do not cultivate <em>Ragy</em>, wheat, nor turmeric; and the most valuable productions that they have are onions, garlic, and capsicum. The maize thrives better than at <em>Silagutta</em>, growing seven or eight feet high, and producing four or five heads. The gardeners, however, remove all except one; and allege, that the plant is not able to bring more to perfection. The same prejudice against the grain prevails here, as elsewhere in this country. When I asked if they ever made it into flour, my question was considered as a joke, or perhaps as an absurdity, at which the people could not help laughing. As a second crop, radishes follow maize.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Amildar</em> is endeavouring to introduce the manufacture of sugar. He has made advances to the <em>Bráhmans</em> who understand the art; and, to begin the experiment, has planted 50,000 holes. He thinks to be able to undersell the sugar-candy of China at <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-404" data-page="404" data-folio="356" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n403_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 356">[p. 356]</button> <em>Seringapatam</em>. He says, that the manner of refining the raw sugar is by boiling it with milk; which, by its coagulation, would no doubt answer the purpose, but the process must be expensive.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Puckally</em>, or <em>Capily</em>, a machine for raising water.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In some places of this vicinity, the ground for sugar-cane is watered by the machine which the Mussulmans call <em>Puckally</em>, and the natives <em>Capily</em>. It consists of two bags of skin raised by a cord passing over a pulley, and drawn by two oxen, or buffaloes, descending on an inclined plane. The great imperfection of this contrivance seems to be, that the cattle are forced to reascend the inclined plane backwards; but it appears to be a manner of raising water very capable of being improved, so as to become highly valuable. One man manages both the cattle; but these work only one half of the day; so that the <em>Puckally</em> requires the labour of one man and four beasts. The cultivators here reckon, that one <em>Puckally</em> will raise as much water as nine men working with the largest <em>Yatam</em>, on which two men work the lever; or as seven men each working a single <em>Yatam</em>. This seems to confirm my opinion of the superiority of this last mentioned machine. The cost of the cattle is not reckoned to be more than that of one man, as they get no other provision than the straw of the farm, which they convert into manure, and which would otherwise be lost. Those who raise sugar-cane have two fields, on which they alternately raise that plant and <em>Ragy</em>. If they use the <em>Capily</em> for watering their cane, they pay a money-rent, which is reduced in proportion to their trouble; but if they obtain a supply of water from a reservoir, the government takes one half of the crop.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Breed of asses.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 19, 20.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Above the <em>Ghats</em> asses are a kind of cattle much used. Every washerman keeps three or four females, and a male. The superfluous males, as I have had occasion to mention, are sold to various kinds of petty traders. The breed is very small, no pains being taken to improve it; nor indeed to keep it from growing worse, unless it may be considered as having already arrived at the ultimate degree of imperfection. For the purpose of breeding mules, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-405" data-page="405" data-folio="357" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n404_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 357">[p. 357]</button> the late <em>Sultan</em> introduced some fine asses from Arabia; but the prejudices of his subjects were so strong, that nothing could be done. The animal is indeed considered so impure, as to be beneath the notice of every person who has any kind of claim to rank; and my questions on the subject were rather disagreeable. Black asses are not uncommon, and white ones are sometimes to be seen; but neither constitute a different breed. The asses get nothing to eat, except what, in the intervals of labour, they can pick up about the village. When the crop is on the ground, they are tied up at night; but at other seasons they are allowed to roam about, and, in order to prevent them from wandering too far, their fore feet are tied together. The males are never castrated, and the best are always sold off by the washermen, which are principal causes of the degeneracy of the breed. At three years of age the females begin to breed, and some have every year a colt, while others breed once only in three years. The colt sucks, till its mother is again big. The idea of the milk being ever used by men is reckoned too absurd to be credible. A common mark of disgrace for criminals is the being forced to ride on an ass; and even the washermen are unwilling to acknowledge that they ever defile themselves so far as to ride on this animal. A good male, three or four years old, sells for 10 <em>Fanams</em> (6<em>s.</em> 8½<em>d.</em>); a female of the same age sells for the same price. An ass’s burthen is reckoned thirty-six <em>Seers</em> of <em>Ragy</em>, or about 76 lb.; with which they will daily travel two cosses, or nearly seven miles.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 21. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">21st <em>July.</em>—I went five cosses to <em>Tonday Bava</em>, near <em>Mahá-káli</em>-<em>durga</em>, passing chiefly through a barren hilly country, totally uncultivated, and covered with bushes or coppice-wood. It is part of a hilly chain that comes toward the west from the north of <em>Colar</em>, and meets at right angles the chain that extends north from <em>Capala-durga</em>. This chain running east and west is called a <em>Ghat</em>, and the country to the north of it is said to be below the <em>Ghats</em>. The whole of it is watered by branches of the <em>Utara Pinákaní</em>, or <em>Pennar</em>. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-406" data-page="406" data-folio="358" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n405_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 358">[p. 358]</button> The nature of the crops here is very different from that in the southern parts of the country.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 21. Customs of the Baydaru.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 21.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 21.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Baydaru</em> are of two kinds, <em>Karnata</em>, and <em>Telinga</em>. The former wear the <em>Linga</em>, and are said to be numerous near <em>Raya-durga</em>. Those in the north-eastern parts of the <em>Mysore Rája's</em> dominions are of <em>Telinga</em> descent, and retain that language. They seem to be the true <em>Súdra</em> cultivators and military of <em>Telingána</em>, and to have been introduced in great numbers into the southern countries of the peninsula, when these became subject to <em>Andray</em> or <em>Telingána</em> princes. The <em>Telinga Baydas</em> neither intermarry, nor eat in common with those of <em>Karnata</em> extraction. Among themselves they can all eat together; but, in order to keep up the purity of the race, they never marry, except in families whose pedigree is well known. Like the <em>Bráhmans</em>, they are divided into a number of families, of which a male and female can never intermarry. They have also among them a race of nobles called <em>Chimalas</em>. Among these are the hereditary chiefs, who punish transgressions against the rules of cast, and who are called <em>Gotugaru</em>. From this class of nobles were also appointed the feudal lords, vulgarly called <em>Polygars</em>; but who assumed to themselves the <em>Sanskrit</em> title of <em>Sansthánika</em>. Civil differences in this tribe are made up in assemblies of the heads of families, the hereditary chiefs having become almost extinct. No heavier punishment was ever inflicted by these than the mulct of an entertainment. The <em>Baydaru</em> ought by birth to be soldiers, and hunters of tigers, boars, deer, and other noble game, and ought to support themselves by cultivating the ground. They are both farmers and hinds, and sometimes act as <em>Talliari</em>, a low village officer. They are permitted to eat fowls, sheep, goats, hogs, deer, and fish, and to drink spirituous liquors. The men are allowed to take many wives, but can only divorce them for adultery. The women are very industrious, both at home and in the field; and even after the age of puberty continue to be marriageable. Widows are not expected to sacrifice themselves to the manes of their husbands; but they <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-407" data-page="407" data-folio="359" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n406_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 359">[p. 359]</button> cannot marry a second time. In some families of the <em>Baydaru</em>, however, they may be received as concubines. They bury the dead. They believe, that after death wicked men become devils, and that good men are born again in a human form. The spirits of men who die without having married, become <em>Vírika</em>; and to their memory have small temples and images erected, where offerings of cloth rice, and the like, are made to their manes. If this be neglected, they appear in dreams, and threaten those who are forgetful of their duty. These temples consist of a heap, or <em>cairn</em> of stones, in which the roof of a small cavity is supported by two or three flags; and the image is a rude shapeless stone, which is occasionally oiled, as in this country all other images are. Female chastity is not at all honoured in this way. This superstition seems rather local, than as belonging to this cast; for it is followed by all the <em>Súdras</em> of this part of the country, and I have not observed it any where else. The <em>Baydaru</em>, in consequence of vows made in sickness, take <em>Dáseri</em>, that is, dedicate themselves to the service of God, both perpetual and temporary. The proper god of the cast is <em>Trimula Dévaru</em>, to whom a celebrated temple is here dedicated. It is an immense mass of granite on the summit of a low hill. Under one side of it is a natural cavity, which is painted red and white with streaks of reddle and lime. In this cavity is placed a rude stone, as the emblem of the god; and it is attended by a priest or <em>Pujári</em> of the cast called <em>Satánana</em>. To this place all the <em>Baydaru</em> of the neighbourhood once a year resort. The <em>Pujári</em> then dresses some victuals; and having consecrated them, by placing them before the idol, he divides them among the people. <em>Trimula</em>, it must be observed, is the name of the hill at <em>Tripathi</em>, on which the celebrated temple of <em>Vishnu</em>, under the name of <em>Vencaty Rámana</em>, is built. The <em>Baydaru</em> never pray to any of the <em>Saktis</em>, except <em>Marima</em>, who inflicts the small-pox on those who offend her. To this terrible power they offer sacrifices, and eat the flesh. Their <em>Guru</em> is <em>Trimula Tata Achárya</em>, an hereditary chief of the <em>Sri Vaishnavam Bráhmans</em>, who gives <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-408" data-page="408" data-folio="360" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n407_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 360">[p. 360]</button> them <em>Chakrántikam</em>, <em>Upadésa</em>, and holy water, and, when he visits the place, receives from each person one <em>Fanam</em>. At marriages, and at the annual commemoration of deceased parents, the <em>Panchánga</em> acts as <em>Puróhita</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 22. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">22d <em>July</em>.—I went three cosses to <em>Assauru</em>, a village inhabited by cultivators, and said to contain five hundred houses, but which looks wretchedly poor. On the way, we passed a place which, although not so large, was better built. It is called <em>Bomma Samudra</em>. The country in general is level, but contains several ridges of barren hills. It is intersected by the channels of several mountain torrents, which are wide, and full of sand ; but even now they contain no water. I am informed, that sometimes, for a little while after very heavy rains, they are full. The soil in many places is a rich black clay ; and, there being no made roads in any part of this country, the travelling in the rainy season is very bad. The cultivation is wretched, and slovenly ; a great deal, that has formerly been cultivated, is now waste ; and much that appears to possess a very good soil has never been reclaimed. I observed several of the reservoirs out of repair. The people attribute this state of the country, partly to the oppression of the former government, and partly to an uncommon scarcity of rain that has prevailed for ten years. The width and dryness of the channels made by the torrents of former seasons seem to confirm the last mentioned cause. They say, that the country does not want people ; but that, by long sufferings, they are disheartened from working. Last year they had no sickness among their cattle, but this fatal disorder has now begun to make its appearance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 23.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 23.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">23d <em>July</em>.—I went three cosses to <em>Doda Bailea</em>, a fortified village inhabited by farmers, which contains about fifty houses. By the way I passed two other such places, near which there was some cultivation ; but the whole of the other parts of the country was covered with bushes or coppice-wood. The ground was no where too steep for cultivation ; and, except in a few stony places, the soil <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-409" data-page="409" data-folio="361" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n408_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 361">[p. 361]</button> seemed tolerably good. I saw no appearance of its ever having been cultivated. There is here a small river, from the bed of which, as from the channel of the <em>Palar</em> at <em>Vellore</em>, trenches may be drawn, so as at all seasons to give the fields water. At present it contains no water above ground. Many of the torrents between this and the <em>Pennar</em>, in the newly acquired dominions of the <em>Nizam</em>, afford a similar supply of water. In others, the streams are shut up by dams or <em>Anacuts</em>, and forced into large reservoirs. The people in the <em>Nizam's</em> share of the <em>Sultan's</em> dominions have already experienced the imbecility and rapacity of that government; and have begun to retire into the dominions of the <em>Mysore Rája</em>, where there is plenty of room. For a century past this place has been subject to <em>Mysore</em>, although it was separated from the capital by the Mussulman government of <em>Sira</em>. In the mean time many of the neighbouring <em>Durgas</em>, or hill forts, with the territories belonging to them, continued subject to their original <em>Polygars</em>, who were mostly robbers, till the whole were finally expelled by <em>Tippoo</em>. In the war of Lord Cornwallis this place suffered extremely, as <em>Purseram Bhow's</em> army was encamped some days in the neighbourhood. This, joined to the famine, and to <em>Tippoo's</em> government, both before and since, has reduced the country and population to a very low state.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 24.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">24th <em>July</em>.—I went two cosses to <em>Madhu-giri</em>, or Honey-hill, a strong <em>Durga</em>, which is surrounded on all sides by hills. From <em>Bailea</em>, these hills appeared as a connected chain, and are a part of that ridge which runs north from <em>Capala-durga</em>; but on entering among them, I found narrow vallies winding through in all directions. The hills are rocky and bare; but in many places the soil of the vallies is good. In some places there are coco-nut gardens; but many of the cleared fields are now unoccupied, and a great deal of good ground seems never to have been reclaimed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Madhu-giri.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 24.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The view of <em>Madhu-giri</em>, on approaching it from the east, is much finer than that of any hill-fort that I have seen. The works here <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-410" data-page="410" data-folio="362" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n409_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 362">[p. 362]</button> make a very conspicuous appearance; whereas in general they are scarcely visible, being hidden by the immensity of the rocks on which they are situated. On the fall of the <em>Vijaya-nagara</em> monarchy, this place belonged to a <em>Polygar</em> named <em>Chiccuppa Gauda</em>; but more than a century ago it came into the possession of the <em>Mysore</em> family. <em>Mul Rája</em> built the fortress of stone, which formerly had been only of mud. Here also he built a palace; in the suburbs he rebuilt a large temple; and near it he made fine gardens, and the handsomest building for the reception of travellers that I have seen in India. Unfortunately, it is now ruinous. The fortifications were improved to their present form by <em>Hyder</em>; the place in his time was a considerable mart, and possessed some manufactures, having a hundred houses occupied by weavers. A <em>Marattah</em> chief, named <em>Madi Row</em>, held it for seven years of <em>Hyder's</em> government, having seized it after the victory which his countrymen gained at <em>Tonuru</em>. When he was forced to retire, he plundered the town of every thing that he could carry away; and with the exaggeration usual in <em>Hindustan</em>, the place is said to have then been so rich, that he disdained to remove any thing less valuable than gold. The oppressions of <em>Tippoo</em> had nearly ruined the place, when the destruction was completed by the <em>Marattah</em> chief <em>Bulwunt Row</em>, one of <em>Purseram Bhow's</em> officers. Although he besieged the fort five months, he was unable to take it. His army was numerous, exaggerated by native accounts to 20,000 men; but they were a mere rabble, a banditti assembled by the <em>Polygars</em>, who formerly were dispossessed of the neighbouring strong-holds, and who then had ventured back under the protection of Lord Cornwallis. When that nobleman gave peace to the <em>Sultan</em>, these ruffians had entirely ruined every open place in the neighbourhood; but they were immediately afterwards dispersed by the <em>Sultan</em>, who pursued with so much activity the 500 <em>Marattah</em> horse which had joined this rabble, that twenty only escaped with their chief. The place has ever since been in a very languishing <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-411" data-page="411" data-folio="363" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n410_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 363">[p. 363]</button> condition, but is beginning to revive. <em>Purnea</em> has appointed a brother-in-law of his own to be <em>Amildar</em>, and gives the inhabitants considerable encouragement.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation in the country north from the <em>Ghats</em> of <em>Nandi-durga</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the 25th till the 29th of <em>July</em>—I remained at <em>Madhu-giri</em>, chiefly employed in taking an account of the cultivation of this country; which, as I have observed before, differs considerably from that to the southward of <em>Nandi-durga</em>. It also differs from that on the west side of this ridge of hills towards <em>Sira</em>; and its extent may be reckoned from thence east to near <em>Chica Bala-pura</em>, and from <em>Nandi-durga</em> north to <em>Penu-conda</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Weights and Measures.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Previous to examining the state of agriculture here, it must be observed, that <em>Madi Row</em> introduced a new set of weights and measures, which, notwithstanding all <em>Tippoo's</em> efforts to the contrary, continue still to be used. In all the districts near this, the rate of exchange, for different coins, is nominally regulated according to the <em>Niruc</em>; that is, made by the officer commanding at <em>Pauguda</em>, or <em>Paughur</em> as we call it; but the <em>Shrofs</em> (<em>Saráf</em>), or money-changers, vary a little from this; not from a greater accuracy in the valuation of the coins, but for reasons that are prevalent all over India, and the nature of which is <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-412" data-page="412" data-folio="364" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n411_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 364">[p. 364]</button> known to this class of men alone. The <em>Batta</em>, or money paid to the <em>Shrof</em> for exchanging a <em>Sultany Pagoda</em> into <em>Fanams</em>, is 2 <em>Dudus</em>, or nearly 0,9 per cent. and by those who have lived in Bengal must be considered as very moderate. Besides, the <em>Shrof</em> values the <em>Fanam</em> at <sup>1</sup>/<sub>221</sub> part less than the <em>Niruc</em> does; yet the value put on it by the officer is less than its intrinsic worth; for the regulation which he has made fixes its value at <sup>1</sup>/<sub>13</sub> of a <em>Sultany Pagoda</em>; and 12,913 <em>Fanams</em> contain as much pure gold, as the <em>Sultany Pagoda</em>. In all calculations I shall use the <em>Seringapatam</em> rate of exchange, and take the <em>Fanam</em> at <sup>1</sup>/<sub>12</sub> of a <em>Pagoda</em>. The Company’s <em>Rupee</em> passes here for 56 <em>Dudus</em>, and the <em>Sultany</em> for 59 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>4</sub>; whereas the real proportion is 56 to 56 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Trimula Nayaka.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The first day that I passed here was very disagreeable; as I detected the people lying to me in the grossest manner; and on account of the <em>Amildar’s</em> connections, the messenger who accompanied me was afraid to speak. I at length met with an acting <em>Gauda</em>, or renter of some villages, named <em>Trimula Nayaka</em>, from whom I received the intelligence which I consider as the most accurate that I procured during my whole journey. <em>Trimula Nayaka</em> is the family name of the <em>Madura Rájas</em>, and in fact my new acquaintance was of that house. His ancestor was a brother of the then reigning prince, who, in a dispute, was savage enough to threaten the life of so near a relation. The younger brother in this necessity was forced to emigrate, and came to this country, where many other <em>Polygars</em> of <em>Telinga</em> extraction then lived; for it must be observed, that the last race of <em>Madura Rájas</em> were of <em>Telinga</em> descent; and were <em>Polygars</em>, who assumed independence on the overthrow of their sovereign, the king of <em>Vijaya-nagara</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Division of lands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cultivated lands in this country, defined, as before, as extending from <em>Nandi</em> to <em>Penu-conda</em>, and from <em>Chica Bala-pura</em> to <em>Madhu-giri</em>, are as usual divided into two kinds: <em>Nirarumba</em>, or watered-lands; and <em>Pyrarumba</em>, or dry-field.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Nirarumba</em>, or watered-lands.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The watered-land here includes all the grounds called <em>Tota</em>, or <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-413" data-page="413" data-folio="365" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n412_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 365">[p. 365]</button> <em>Bagait</em>, whether employed for palm plantations, or for kitchen-gardens. It is watered partly by reservoirs, or <em>Carays</em>; partly by <em>Callivays</em>, which are channels cut from rivers; and partly by the machines called <em>Capily</em>, and <em>Yatam</em>. The quantity of watered-land is nearly equal to that of dry-field; and, besides that reserved for palm trees and kitchen stuffs, and which is not considerable, is divided into two kinds. These two divisions are nearly equal in extent; the one is cultivated chiefly for rice, transplanted <em>Ragy</em>, and <em>Jola</em>; the other with wheat, <em>Carlay</em>, <em>Mentea</em>, and <em>Jirigay</em>. The extent, however, of all the watered land is reckoned by the quantity of rice seed that would be required to sow it. By measuring two fields, and taking the medium, I estimate the <em>Candaca</em> of watered-land to be 30 acres.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside 367]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I shall now detail the other circumstances which attend their cultivation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On the first division of the watered-lands, rice is the greatest crop; and, when there is plenty of water, the same ground in the course of the year gives two crops, which, from the respective times of harvest, are called the <em>Kártika</em> and <em>Vaisákha</em> crops. The former, provided two crops are taken, is the most productive; but, if the <em>Kártika</em> be omitted, the <em>Vaisákha</em> gives a greater return than the <em>Kártika</em> alone would have given; not, however, equal to the produce of both crops. The quality of the grain in both crops is the same. For the reasons mentioned at <em>Colar</em>, the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop, although raised in the dry season, is the one most regularly taken. For this crop all the kinds of rice may be sown; for the <em>Kártika</em> crop the <em>Bily Sanabutta</em>, and <em>Cari Chaningy</em>, are never sown; as with rain they are apt to lodge. The soil used for <em>Tripetty-Sanabutta</em>, <em>Bily-Chaningy</em>, <em>Cari-Chaningy</em>, and <em>Put’-Rajah</em>, is <em>Marulu</em>, or sandy. The others require a clay, which in the low grounds is always black. The red soil is always confined to the rising grounds, and is therefore never cultivated for rice, except when it can be watered by machines; and, if the water be more than 31½ feet from the surface, these are never used. Two men and four oxen can, by means of the machine called <em>Capily</em>, supply an acre and a half of ground with water sufficient to raise a crop of rice. One set works four or five hours in the morning; and the other as much in the evening. In the day the men do little jobs; but the cattle do no other work. When this machine is used, the government does not divide the crop with the farmer; but, on account of his extraordinary labour, takes a fixed rent of four seeds, or else contents itself with one quarter of the produce. Sixteen seeds may, therefore, be considered as the average crop of this country; but then the seed, it must be observed, is sown very thick. Little rice is, however, watered by machinery; and the kinds chosen are those which require the shortest time to come to maturity.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-416"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="416" data-folio="368" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n415_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 368">[p. 368]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c. Manner of preparing the seed.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only manner of cultivating rice, that is in use here, is the <em>Mola</em>, or sprouted-seed; the manner of preparing which is as follows. The ears must be cut off, the grain beat out immediately, and then dried in the sun three or four days. It must be preserved in straw or in jars. When wanted for sowing, it must be exposed to the sun for a day, and soaked in water all the following night. It is then put upon a layer of the leaves of the <em>Yecada</em>, or <em>Asclepias gigantea</em>, or of the <em>Harulu</em>, or <em>Ricinus Palma Christi</em>, mixed with sheeps dung, and is surrounded by stones, so as to keep it together. It is then covered with <em>Bandury</em> leaves (<em>Dodonea viscosa Willd:</em>), and pressed down with a stone. Next morning the upper leaves are removed, and a pot of water is thrown on the seed, which must be turned with the hand, and then covered again with the leaves and stone. Daily, for three or four times, this operation must be repeated, and then the sprouts from the seed will be almost an inch long.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation for the <em>Kártika</em> crop.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">For the <em>Kártika</em> crop, plough seven times in the course of thirty days, the ground all the while being inundated. In the next place manure the ground with leaves, and tread them into the mud. Then let off the water, and sow the seed broad-cast, covering it with a little dung. On the 4th day cover the ground with water, and immediately afterwards let it run off. Repeat this daily, till the eighth time, after which the field must be kept constantly inundated to the depth of one inch for ten days, and four inches for the remainder. The weedings are at the end of the 6th, 10th, and 12th weeks from sowing. The season for ploughing continues all the months of <em>Jyaishtha</em> and <em>Ashádha</em>, which this year was from the 24th of May to the 21st of July.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation for the <em>Vaisakha</em> crop.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">For the <em>Vaisakha</em> crop the same process is followed; but the ploughing season is from the 15th of <em>Aswaja</em> till the last of <em>Márgasírsha</em>; which, this year, will be from the 3d of October till the 16th of December. By this time the whole seed must be sown; and the nearer it is done to it, the better.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-417"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="417" data-folio="369" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n416_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 369">[p. 369]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c. Manure of leaves.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The leaves used here as a manure for rice-land are those of the <em>Coghi</em>, or <em>Galega purpurea</em> ; of the <em>Haingay</em>, or <em>Robinia mitis</em>; of the <em>Yecada</em>, or <em>Asclepias gigantea</em> ; of the <em>Devadarum</em>, or <em>Erythroxylon sideroxyloides</em>, E. M.; of the <em>Calli</em>, or <em>Euphorbium Tirucalli</em>; and of the <em>Huts’ Ellu</em>, a plant not yet described.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Transplanted Ragy.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">From rice ground, in place of the <em>Kartika</em> crop, <em>Sussi</em>, or <em>Nat’ Ragy</em>, is often taken. This is the same with the <em>Tota Ragy</em>, before mentioned, and is transplanted and watered like rice. The following is the process for raising the seedlings. The season for sowing lasts <em>Vaisákha</em>, <em>Jyaishtha</em>, and <em>Ashádha</em> ; which this year is from the 23d of April till the 21st of July ; but the process in any one field is finished in from 22 to 24 days. With a hoe dig a small spot of ground to the depth of four inches, and manure it with dung. Then divide it into small squares, about two cubits each way; sow the seed very thick, cover it with dung, and water the squares with a pot. This must be repeated every other day until the plants are fit for removing, which is from 22 to 24 days. When the seed is sown, the field into which it is to be transplanted must have five ploughings. It is then dunged, and divided into plots about three or four cubits square, which are surrounded with small ridges to confine the water. These plots are filled with water, and the young <em>Ragy</em> is transplanted into them. In order to remove the plants, there is no occasion to water the plots in which they were raised, as the people of <em>Colar</em> recommended. To procure a full crop, the <em>Ragy</em> ought once in eight days to be watered ; but those who are indolent content themselves with giving water once in eleven or twelve days. On the 15th day after transplanting, it must be weeded with a small hoe called <em>Molu potu</em> (Figure 35). The <em>Ragy</em> raised on dry ground is reckoned of rather a superior quality ; but the produce of this is great. It thrives on any soil in which rice will grow. One machine of the kind called <em>Capily</em> will water 2¼ acres of <em>Ragy</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Bily Jola.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop of rice, <em>Bily Jola</em> is sometimes sown. This must be followed by a <em>Kartika</em> crop of <em>Ragy</em>, as after it the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-418" data-page="418" data-folio="370" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n417_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 370">[p. 370]</button> produce of rice would be very small.  The <em>Jola</em> also thrives best after a <em>Kartika</em> crop of <em>Ragy</em>.  The following is the process of cultivation.  Immediately after cutting the <em>Kartika</em> crop, in <em>Kartika</em>, <em>Márgasírsha</em>, and <em>Paushya</em> (19th October to the 14th January), plough five times, and manure with equal parts of dung, and of mud from the bottoms of tanks, mixed with the leaves of the <em>Robinia mitis</em>. A man then draws furrows with a plough, and another places the seed in the furrows at the distance of four or five inches.  By the next furrow it is covered.  Previous to being planted, the seed must have been soaked in water.  In place of using a rolling-stone, the field is then smoothed by drawing over it a plank, on one end of which a man stands, and by this means that forms a low ridge; and thus throughout the field, at the distance of the length of the plank, which is six feet, parallel rows of ridges are produced.  The intermediate spaces are divided into oblong plots by forming with the hand ridges, which at every eight or twelve cubits distance cross the others at right angles.  At the same time the areas of the plots are exactly levelled.  Before sowing, there must have been rain enough to moisten the ground, otherwise water must be given.  At the end of a month the field must have another watering ; and once in eight days, until the grain be ripe, this must be repeated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Agara Jola.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop on rice ground, the <em>Agara Jola</em> is sometimes sown.  It is cultivated exactly as the <em>Bily Jola</em>, but ripens in four months.  One <em>Capily</em>, requiring the labour of three men and four bullocks, can water 2 <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>11</sub> acres of <em>Jola land</em> that are divided into eight portions, of which one is daily allowed water.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Mobu Navony.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Kartika</em> crop, should there be no water in the reservoir, a species of <em>Navony</em>, called <em>Mobu</em>, is sometimes taken from rice ground, as the usual quantity of rain is sufficient to ripen it. In <em>Chaitra</em> and <em>Vaisákha</em>, or from about the middle of March till the middle of May, plough twice, manure with dung, and then plough twice again.  After a shower of rain, sow, either broad-cast, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-419" data-page="419" data-folio="371" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n418_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 371">[p. 371]</button> or with the drill. If the day then dry up, smooth the field with a bunch of thorns; but, if the day be damp, use the native harrow.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 25, &amp;c. <em>Bily Navony.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop, when there is a deficiency of water, the kind of <em>Navony</em> called <em>Bily</em> is sometimes sown on rice-ground. For this, plough four times in <em>Kártika</em> (19th October—16th November). Manure is not necessary, but may be given with advantage. The field must then be watered, and ploughed a fifth time; then it must be sown broad-cast, and divided into plots like a field of <em>Jola</em>. Once in eight days water is given. The crop of rice following <em>Navony</em> is not so good as that after <em>Ragy</em>, but better than that after <em>Jola</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Not having had an opportunity of comparing these two kinds of <em>Jola</em> and <em>Navony</em> when in flower, I cannot say whether or not, in a botanical sense, they are specifically different. It is probable, however, that they are mere varieties of the <em>Holcus sorghum</em>, and <em>Panicum Italicum</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Barugu</em>, or <em>Panicum miliaceum.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Kartika</em> crop, a very little <em>Barugu</em> is sometimes sown on rice-ground; but this is still worse for the succeeding crop of rice than even <em>Jola</em> is. It is chiefly sown by very poor people, who are in want of an immediate subsistence; for it ripens very quickly.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Sesamum.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop, <em>Sesamum</em>, of the kind called here <em>Gur' Ellu</em>, is also sown on rice-ground. For this, in <em>Kartika</em>, <em>Márgasírsha</em>, or <em>Paushya</em> (19th October—14th January) plough four times. Then water, and plough again. Sow the seed broad-cast, and divide the field into plots like those used for <em>Jola</em>; at the same time channels, for conducting the water, are of course formed. Once in the twelve days it receives water.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only other thing cultivated on this kind of watered-land is sugar-cane. Those who raise this valuable article divide their field into two equal portions, which are cultivated alternately, one year with sugar-cane, and the other with grain; the cane, however, thrives better, when the field, in place of being cultivated for grain, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-420" data-page="420" data-folio="372" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n419_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 372">[p. 372]</button> is allowed an intermediate fallow; but then the loss is heavy, as after cane the grain thrives remarkably. The grains cultivated are rice, <em>Ragy</em>, and <em>Jola</em>; the first injures the cane least, and the <em>Jola</em> injures it most. The kinds of cane cultivated are the <em>Restalli</em> and <em>Maracabo</em>. In <em>Kartika</em> and <em>Márgasírsha</em> (19th October—16th December) plough seven times, and manure with sheeps’ dung and leaves. Then with the hoe called <em>Yella Kudali</em>, form channels at a cubit’s distance. In these also, at a cubit’s distance, plant singly shoots of the cane, each about a cubit in length. If the soil be poor, they must be planted rather nearer. They are laid down in the channels, which are filled with water, and then people tread the shoots into the mud, by walking through each channel. A <em>Colaga</em> of land requires 18000 shoots, on which <em>data</em> it ought to contain 1 <sup>8</sup>/<sub>10</sub> acre, in place of 1 <sup>5</sup>/<sub>10</sub> that were given me by the measurement of two fields. In all my calculations, however, I have considered it as of the latter extent. If the soil be of a moist nature, the cane has water once in eight days; but, if it dry quickly, it must, until ripe, be watered once in six days, except when there is rain. At the end of the first month the field must be hoed with the <em>Cali Kudali</em> (Figure 35), which is the very same instrument, that in the cultivation of <em>Ragy</em> is called <em>Molu potu</em>. The misapplication of these names is thought to be unlucky. Near each cane, as a manure, some leaves of the <em>Robinia mitis</em> are then placed, and they are covered with a little mud; so that the channels are now between the rows of cane, and the canes grow on the ridges. When these are 2 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub> cubits high, they are tied up in bunches of three or four; and as they grow higher, this is three or four times repeated. Twelve months after planting the crop season begins, and in six weeks it must be finished. 250 <em>Maunds</em> of <em>Jagory</em> is here reckoned a good crop from a <em>Colaga</em> of land, which is very nearly 15 hundred weight an acre; 150 <em>Maunds</em>, which is about nine hundred weight, from the acre, is reckoned a bad crop. Black clay gives the greatest quantity of <em>Jagory</em>, but it <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-421" data-page="421" data-folio="373" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n420_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 373">[p. 373]</button> is of a bad quality. A sandy soil produces least <em>Jagory</em>, but that of a high value. One machine called <em>Capily</em> can water an acre and a half of sugar-cane-land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Bily Jola.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">A few fields of watered-land are entirely allotted for the cultivation of <em>Bily Jola</em>, or <em>Holcus sorghum</em>. The soil of these is a rich black mould, but does not require much water. It is often watered by the <em>Capily</em>, in which case the farmer pays a fixed money-rent. If it receive a supply from a reservoir or canal, government takes one half of the produce. Only one crop a year is taken. The produce is great; not only as an immense increase on the seed sown, but as affording a great deal of food. The produce of some kinds of rice is apparently greater; but it must be considered, that one half of that grain consists of husk, whereas the whole of <em>Jola</em> is eatable. Begin to plough in <em>Vaisákha</em> (23d April—23d May), and in the course of seven months plough eight or nine times. Then manure with dung, mud, and the leaves of the <em>Robinia mitis</em>; and, if there be no rain, water the field, and sow the seed in the manner before described. The waterings, after the first month, must be given once in twelve or fourteen days. In some villages the farmers weed the <em>Jola</em> when it is six weeks old; in others, they do not take this trouble. Some people around every field of <em>Jola</em> plant a row of <em>Cossumba</em> (<em>Carthamus tinctorius</em>) seeds, and the prickly nature of that plant keeps away cattle.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wheat-land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cultivation of the other division of watered-land, in this district, is reckoned the most profitable to the farmer. The soil must be a black clay, in any situation where a little water can be procured.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this ground, wheat of the kind called <em>Juvi Godi</em> is the most common crop. It seems to be the <em>Triticum monococcum</em> of Linnæus. It is but a poor grain, and five twelfths of it consist of husks. Any time in <em>Paushya</em> (17th December—14th January) plough once; next day, if there be no rain, water the field, and plough again across, dropping the seed in the same manner as in sowing <em>Jola</em>. The plots <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-422" data-page="422" data-folio="374" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n421_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 374">[p. 374]</button> must be formed in the same manner. It gets no manure nor weeding, and requires only three waterings, on the 40th, 60th, and 80th days. It is much subject to disease, and not above one crop in four is good. After reaping the wheat, the field, in order to expose the soil to the rain, must be immediately ploughed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Carlay</em>, or <em>Cicer arietinum</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Another considerable crop, raised on this ground, is <em>Carlay</em> mixed with <em>Cossumba</em>, or <em>Carthamus tinctorius</em>. No attention is paid to the alternation of this crop with the wheat. Sometimes they are every year changed; and again, for two or three successive years, the same crop is taken from the same ground. The <em>Carlay</em> is cultivated exactly in the same manner as the wheat, only it requires no water, and the field is not divided into plots. Throughout the field, at the distance of three cubits, the <em>Cossumba</em> seed is planted in drills. The dew resting on the leaves of the <em>Carlay</em> is said to be acid, and is esteemed a powerful medicine, especially for restoring the appetite. It is collected by spreading over the field at night a muslin cloth, from which in the morning the dew is wrung.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Mentea</em> and <em>Jirigay</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In some parts of this ground, which it is not necessary to choose very rich, are sown <em>Mentea</em> and <em>Jirigay</em>; the former is the <em>Trigonella Fœnumgræcum</em>, and the latter is an umbelliferous plant, which I did not see.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Mentea</em> cultivated in gardens is always used green. When intended for seed or for the grain, it is always raised in this manner: plough twice at the same season as for <em>Carlay</em>; divide the field into plots like a kitchen garden; sow the seed, cover it with the hand, and, according to the nature of the soil, water once in from ten to fifteen days. The ripe seed of this plant sells very high, and is reckoned the most delicate kind of pulse. The young leaves are used as greens, and the unripe legumes are put into <em>Curries</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Jirigay</em> is cultivated exactly in the same manner. Sometimes it is sown on the ground that is usually employed for transplanted <em>Ragy</em>; but there it does not thrive so well.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wheat called <em>Hotay</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When reservoirs dry up before the end of <em>Mágha</em>, or middle of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-423" data-page="423" data-folio="375" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n422_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 375">[p. 375]</button> February, which however is not often the case, their bottoms, which consist of very rich mould, are cultivated with a kind of wheat called <em>Hotay Godi</em>, which is the <em>Triticum spelta</em> of botanists. This is a much superior grain to the <em>Juvi</em>, and contains very little husk. Plough the ground as it dries up, and drop the seed in the furrows after the plough. Then, in place of a rolling stone, smooth the field with a plank. It ripens without farther trouble.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In this kind of ground are sometimes put <em>Carlay</em> and <em>Cossumba</em>. The crop in the bottom of reservoirs is divided into three shares; one of which goes to the person who furnishes the seed, one to government, and one to the cultivator.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dry-field Ragy, or <em>Cynosurus Corocanus</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The most considerable crop cultivated on <em>Pyrarumba</em>, or dry-field, is <em>Ragy</em>. Besides what is cultivated on watered land, it forms two-thirds of the whole dry-crop. On the dry-fields are raised two kinds of it; the <em>Gyda</em>, and the <em>Doda</em>. The former ripens in four months, and the latter in four and a half; and the latter is esteemed both the best in quality, and the most productive; but when the rains set in late, as it requires less time to ripen, the <em>Gyda</em> is preferable. The best soil for <em>Ragy</em> is red, next black, then ash-coloured, and the worst is <em>Marulu</em>, or that which contains much sand. The best soils are generally reserved for <em>Ragy</em>, which always requires more or less dung: and, if plenty of that could be procured, it might be raised on even the most sandy soils to great advantage. The first and second crops would be poor; but, when the field came to be saturated with manure, the <em>Ragy</em> would be as productive as usual on the good soils. Very few farmers here, however, have at any time a sufficient quantity of manure; nor can it be ever expected that they should, as the custom of stall-feeding cattle for slaughter is by them considered abominable. During <em>Chaitra</em>, <em>Vaisákha</em>, and <em>Jyaishtha</em>, or from about the middle of March till the middle of June, in the course of thirty days plough five times. After the first ploughing, put on the manure. The seed is sown with a drill, like that used at <em>Colar</em>. For every <em>Colaga</em> of land, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-424" data-page="424" data-folio="376" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n423_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 376">[p. 376]</button> which here is of the same extent for wet and dry grains, put into the <em>Curigay</em> (Figure 26) 24 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Ragy</em>, and ¼ <em>Seer</em> of <em>Sashivay</em>, or mustard, and into the <em>Sudiky</em> (Figure 26) put 9 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Avaray</em>, or of <em>Tovary</em>, or of both intermixed. Having drilled in the seed, cover it with the harrow. On the 20th day hoe with the <em>Cuntay</em>; on the 28th day repeat this, drawing the <em>Cuntay</em> in a direction which crosses its former one at right angles. On the 36th and 42d days repeat this. The mustard, as it ripens, is pulled, partly before, and partly after the <em>Ragy</em>. The pulses require six months and a half to ripen. The <em>Ragy</em> is improved by trampling, or by frequent repetitions of the hoe drawn by oxen. To answer the same purpose, a flock of sheep are sometimes driven over it. These processes destroy at least ¾ of the pulses. Although in my account of the cultivation near <em>Seringapatam</em>, I have hinted at an excuse, the farmers here can assign no reason, but custom, for sowing such an incongruous mixture. In a good crop, a <em>Colaga</em> of land will produce 13 <em>Colagas</em>, or 52 seeds of <em>Ragy</em>; 12 <em>Seers</em>, or 48 seeds of mustard; and 2 <em>Colagas</em>, or 21⅓ seeds of the pulses.</p></div><div class="ledger-table-block" id="tbl-037"><div class="table-toolbar"><span class="table-id-badge">TBL-037 · FOLIO 424</span><span class="table-csv-links"><a href="/downloads/journey-from-madras-vol-i/datasets/tables/tbl-037_p0424.csv" download>Diplomatic CSV</a> · <a href="/downloads/journey-from-madras-vol-i/datasets/tidy/tbl-037_p0424.tidy.csv" download>Tidy CSV</a></span></div><div class="ledger-table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Statistical table" tabindex="0"><table class="ledger-table"><thead><tr><th scope="col"></th><th scope="col">Pecks.</th><th scope="col"></th><th scope="col">Bushels.</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr class="row-data"><td>An acre sows of <em>Ragy</em></td><td class="num">2,221368</td><td>and produces</td><td class="num">28,877333</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>Mustard</td><td class="num">0,023139</td><td></td><td class="num">0,416509</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>Pulse</td><td class="num">0,556332</td><td></td><td class="num">4,4427</td></tr><tr class="row-total"><td>Total</td><td class="num">2,800839</td><td></td><td class="num">33,736542</td></tr></tbody></table></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Shamay</em>, or <em>Panicum miliare</em>, E. As they require the same length of time to ripen, they are sometimes sown separately, and sometimes mixed. The best soil for <em>Shamay</em> is red or ash-coloured, containing a good deal of sand, and in this country is common on high places. Without much manure, this ground does not bear constant cropping. After resting a year, or more, it is first cultivated for <em>Huruli</em>, and next season for <em>Shamay</em>. If manure can be procured, a crop of <em>Ragy</em> is taken, and then it has another <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-425" data-page="425" data-folio="377" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n424_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 377">[p. 377]</button> fallow. Dung being a scarce article, in place of the <em>Ragy</em> a second crop of <em>Shamay</em> is taken; but it is a bad one. If the fallow has been long, and high bushes have grown up, after burning these, the crop of <em>Huruli</em> will be great, and two or three good crops of <em>Shamay</em> will follow. When good <em>Ragy</em> soil has for a year or more been waste, and is to be brought again into cultivation, the first crop taken ought to be <em>Shamay</em>; for <em>Ragy</em> thrives very ill on land that is not constantly cultivated. In this case, the <em>Shamay</em> gives a great quantity of straw, but little grain. When the rains have failed, so that the <em>Ragy</em> has not been sown, or when, in consequence of drought, it has died, should the end of the season be favourable, a crop of <em>Shamay</em> is taken from the fields that are usually cultivated with <em>Ragy</em>. This crop also runs to straw, and the following crop of <em>Ragy</em> requires more dung than usual. In the course of thirty days, any time between the middle of April and middle of July, plough three or four times. Then after a good rain, or one which makes the water run on the surface of the ground, harrow with the rake drawn by oxen, and sow the <em>Shamay</em> seed with the drill, putting in with the <em>Sudiky</em> (Fig. 26) rows of the pulses called <em>Huruli</em> or <em>Tovary</em>. In four months, without farther trouble, it ripens. The seed for a <em>Colaga</em>-land is 24 <em>Seers</em> of the <em>Shamay</em> and 6 <em>Seers</em> of the pulse. In a good crop, the <em>Shamay</em> will produce 10 <em>Colagas</em> of <em>Shamay</em>, and two of <em>Tovary</em>, or one of <em>Huruli</em>. Except after <em>Car'-Ellu</em>, or upon new ground, it never succeeds. The longer the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-426" data-page="426" data-folio="378" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n425_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 378">[p. 378]</button> ground has been waste, especially if it has been overgrown with small bushes of the <em>Tayngadu</em>, or <em>Bandury</em>, (<em>Cassia auriculata</em>, and <em>Dodonea viscosa</em> Wild.) so much the better for <em>Huruli</em>. In <em>Srávana</em>, or from about the middle of July to the middle of August, burn the bushes; and either then, or in the course of the next month, plough once. After the next good rain sow the seed broadcast, and plough the field across the former furrows. A <em>Colaga</em>-land sows 12 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Huruli</em>, and in a good crop produces twenty seeds.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">One acre sows 1,110684 peck, and produces 5,55684 bushels.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The quantity of all the other crops is inconsiderable.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cotton.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In a particular quality of soil, of which the quantity is small, cotton and <em>Mobu Navony</em> are cultivated. It is a black clay, which contains small masses of lime-stone. On this ground <em>Ragy</em> will not grow; but on <em>Ragy</em> soil cotton will grow, although not well. In the course of a month, any time in the first quarter of the year, which commences about the vernal equinox, plough five times. Then, after a good rain, harrow with the rake drawn by oxen, and sow with the drill, the seed of the <em>Navony</em> being put in the <em>Curigy</em>, and that of the cotton in the <em>Sudiky</em> (see Figure 26); then harrow again. It gets neither weeding nor manure. In four months the <em>Navony</em> is reaped, and the space on which it grew is ploughed. The cotton, in 15 days afterwards, begins to give ripe capsules; and till <em>Vaisákha</em>, or about the middle of the following April, it continues to produce a good quantity. If the farmer be able, he then ploughs up the whole field, and sows it as at first; but if he be poor, or lazy, he weeds the field by ploughing between the rows of cotton, and cuts it down close by the ground. It shoots up fresh branches, and in the second year gives a poor crop. A <em>Colaga</em>-land requires 4 <em>Seers</em> of cotton seed, and nine of that of <em>Navony</em>, and produces 96 fold of the <em>Navony</em>. The produce of the <em>Navony</em> is equal to about 20 bushels on an acre. This land is sometimes let by a division of crops, and sometimes for a fixed rent. In such places nothing else will thrive. When the rain has set in so late as to prevent the cultivation of any thing else, the <em>Huts’-Ellu</em> is sown also on any land, especially on <em>Ragy</em> fields. Palm-gardens.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>betel-nut</em> or <em>Areca</em> gardens were here of some importance; but during the terror occasioned by the last Marattah invasion, many of them, from want of care, perished. Several are now replanting. The situation that is reckoned most favourable for them is a black soil, which contains calcareous nodules. It differs from that in which cotton is raised, by having the lime-stone a cubit or two deep; whereas the cotton requires it to be at the surface. For <em>Areca</em> gardens the people here do not approve of that ground which contains water near the surface; for they say, that the produce of such soils is of little value. The gardens at this place are watered from reservoirs, from canals, and from wells by means of the <em>Capily</em>. The trees are remarkably fine.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. <em>July</em> 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">To make a new garden, in <em>Srávana</em>, the 5th month after the vernal equinox, plough four times. Then with the hoe, called <em>Yella Kudali</em>, form the garden into beds six cubits wide. Between every two beds is a raised channel, for bringing a supply of water; and in the center of each bed is a deep channel, to carry off what is superfluous. The beds are divided into plots ten or twelve cubits long. Then plant the whole with shoots of the <em>betel-leaf</em> vine (<em>Piper Betle</em>), and for its support sow the seeds of the <em>Haluana</em>, <em>Agashay</em>, and <em>Nugay</em>. Then surround the whole with a thick hedge, and once a day for three months water with a pot. Whenever weeds grow, they must be removed; and at each time the <em>betel-vines</em> must get some dung. Between every two rows of the vines, in the 4th month, is put a row of young plantain trees (<em>Musa</em>). Once in four days afterwards the water is given from the reservoir or well. In six months the vines must be tied up to the young trees. At the same time, for every <em>Wocula</em>-land, 3,000 nuts of the <em>Areca</em> must be planted near the roots of the vines. When they are three years old, a thousand of them will be fit for use, and 800 are required to pland a <em>Wocula</em>-land, or about an acre and a half. They are planted distant in every direction from each other 5 cubits. At the same time plant on the inside of the hedge some rows of coco-nut palms, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-433" data-page="433" data-folio="385" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n432_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 385">[p. 385]</button> and orange, lime, <em>Mango</em>, or <em>Jack</em> trees. The eight hundred <em>Areca</em> palms, at five cubits distance, would only occupy about an acre ; but a considerable space is taken up by a walk, and by the rows of fruit trees between them and the hedge. In nine years, from the first formation of the garden, the <em>betel-vines</em>, and most of the trees that supported them, are removed. A few of the <em>Agashay</em>, and all the <em>plantains</em>, are allowed to remain. In the twelfth year the <em>Areca</em> palms begin to produce fruit. The remaining <em>Agashay</em> (<em>Æschynomone grandiflora</em>) trees, and one half of the <em>plantains</em>, are then removed. After this, the garden requires water only once in the eight days, when there is no rain ; and the whole is dug over, and formed like rice-ground into proper squares, and channels for distributing the water. One year it is manured with dung ; in the second with the leaves of the <em>Hoingay</em>, and <em>Coghi</em> (<em>Robinia mitis</em>, and <em>Galega purpurea</em>), and in the third year with mud from the bottom of a reservoir. So long as the garden lasts, this succession of manures should, if possible, be continued ; and when the palms attain their full growth, which is in the 14th year of the garden, the plantain trees are entirely removed. For 30 years, from its arriving at maturity, the palm continues vigorous, and for 14 years more gradually declines ; during which time a new garden ought to be formed, and then the old trees should be cut, and the ground cultivated with grain, till the second formed garden again begins to decay. In place of those that die, some poor farmers plant new trees, and thus constantly keep up a garden on the same spot ; but here this is looked upon as a bad practice. The crop season lasts two months before, and one after, the autumnal equinox. The nut, after being peeled, is cut into seven or eight pieces, and put up in a heap. Then take one <em>Seer</em> of the nut, one <em>Seer</em> of <em>Cut</em>, or <em>Terra Japonica</em>, and a hundred leaves of the <em>Piper Betle</em>, beat them together repeatedly with some water, and strain the juice thus obtained into a pot. Take 20 <em>Seers</em> of the bark of the <em>Cari Jali</em> (<em>Mimosa indica</em> E. M.), and boil it during a whole night in a large pot, with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-434" data-page="434" data-folio="386" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n433_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 386">[p. 386]</button> forty <em>Seers</em> of water. With this decoction mix the juice expressed from the former materials, and boil again. While it is boiling, put in the <em>Areca</em> nut, after it has been cut, until the pot be full. Immediately after, take it out with a ladle, and put in more, till the whole is boiled. In order to be dried, it must be three days exposed on mats to the sun, and is then fit for sale. It is bought up chiefly by the merchants of the place, and by those of <em>Gubi</em>. To enable the farmer to pay his rent, which is a certain fixed sum of money, it is customary for the merchant to make advances. Forty <em>Maunds</em> of dried nut is here reckoned the common produce of a <em>Colaga</em>-land, which is about six hundred weight and one third an acre, or for each tree about 1⅓ lb. At <em>Chandra-giri</em>, near this place, the produce is one half more, or 60 <em>Maunds</em>. The former custom was for government to give every person who undertook to make a plantation of palms an advance of 100 <em>Fanams</em> (3<em>l</em>. 7<em>s</em>. 1<em>d</em>.) and of 10 <em>Colagas</em> (about 33⅓ bushels) of grain, for every <em>Colaga</em> (1½ acre) of land that he engaged to plant. The first year's rent was 30 <em>Fanams</em>; the second year's rent 40 <em>Fanams</em>; from the third until the twelfth year 60 <em>Fanams</em>; the thirteenth year 100 <em>Fanams</em>; the fourteenth and subsequent years 166 <em>Fanams</em>. These rents, reduced to the acre at the <em>Seringapatam</em> exchange, and small fractions being omitted, will be:</p></div><div class="ledger-table-block" id="tbl-041"><div class="table-toolbar"><span class="table-id-badge">TBL-041 · FOLIO 434</span><span class="table-csv-links"><a href="/downloads/journey-from-madras-vol-i/datasets/tables/tbl-041_p0434.csv" download>Diplomatic CSV</a> · <a href="/downloads/journey-from-madras-vol-i/datasets/tidy/tbl-041_p0434.tidy.csv" download>Tidy CSV</a></span></div><div class="ledger-table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Statistical table" tabindex="0"><table class="ledger-table"><tbody><tr class="row-data"><td>1st year</td><td><em>£</em>. 0</td><td class="num">13</td><td class="num">5</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>2d year</td><td class="num">0</td><td class="num">17</td><td class="num">11</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>3d—12th years</td><td class="num">1</td><td class="num">6</td><td class="num">10</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>13th year</td><td class="num">2</td><td class="num">4</td><td class="num">7</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>Full rent</td><td class="num">3</td><td class="num">14</td><td class="num">3</td></tr></tbody></table></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The full rent at <em>Chandra-giri</em> is 250 <em>Fanams</em>, or about 5<em>l</em>. 10<em>s</em>. an acre. This high rent is, however, greatly less than one half of the produce.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Yams</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Among the <em>betel-leaf</em> gardens in this neighbourhood a few <em>Yams</em> (<em>Dioscorea</em>) are planted; and this is the only place above the <em>Ghats</em> where I found that valuable root cultivated. In the <em>betel-nut</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-435" data-page="435" data-folio="387" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n434_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 387">[p. 387]</button> gardens they would succeed, but it is imagined that they would injure the crop.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 25, &amp;c. <em>Navony.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only other thing cultivated in these palm gardens is <em>Kempa Navony</em>, which may be sown every year in the fifth month after the autumnal equinox. The ground in the middle of the squares is dug up, and in a garden of a <em>Colaga</em> of land 4 <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub> <em>Seers</em> of the seed are sown. The ground is then smoothed with the hand and dunged. The produce is only 96 <em>Seers</em>, and the principal intention of sowing it is to prevent the squirrels from hurting the nuts, by giving them a more favourite food. These little animals, though beautiful, are a very destructive vermin, and would be destroyed by the inhabitants of this place, were it practicable. In many other places their destruction would be considered as sinful.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Expence of a palm-garden.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In a garden here, watered by the <em>Capily</em>, and said to be a <em>Colaga</em>-land, or about an acre and a half, the water in the wells being about 20 feet below the surface, I found that there were constantly employed three men, two oxen, and a <em>buffalo</em>. This was said to be the rate of labour common in the country. At crop season, and when the whole garden was hoed, extraordinary labourers were hired.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Capily</em>, a machine for raising water.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A <em>Capily</em> which I examined, the water being 19 feet 8 inches below the surface, emptied its bucket, on an average, once every 36 seconds ; and at each time brought up 32 <sup>85</sup>⁄<sub>100</sub> ale gallons of water. One man and two oxen could work it for eight hours in the day, and thus draw up daily 26,280 gallons. Double the quantity may be had, from the same well, by a double set of cattle. Stops, however, frequently intervene, that very considerably diminish the quantity actually raised.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The rent here upon dry-field is not fixed ; but, before ploughing commences, a bargain is annually made between the acting <em>Gauda</em>, or renter, and the farmer. Dry soil fit for <em>Ragy</em>, which implies its being of a good quality, lets from 10 to 30 <em>Fanams</em> for a <em>Colaga</em> sowing, or about 6 acres ; or for from 13<em>d.</em> to 3<em>s.</em> 4<em>d.</em> the acre. If the same soil admits of being watered, so as to raise transplanted <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-436" data-page="436" data-folio="388" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n435_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 388">[p. 388]</button> <em>Ragy</em>, it pays from 20 to 25 <em>Fanams</em> for a <em>Colaga</em>-land, or from 9 to 11 shillings an acre. The same soil, in situations answering for kitchen gardens, pays from 40 to 70 <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Colaga</em>, or nearly from 18<em>s</em>. to 31<em>s</em>. for an acre and a half. None of the rents, however, are fixed, either to the person who rents the village from the government, or to the cultivators, except those on rice lands and <em>betel</em>-<em>nut</em> gardens. The cultivator is nevertheless considered as having a claim to certain lands; and, even if he have been absent for a number of years, he may return, and reclaim the lands formerly occupied by his family; and has a right to them, on paying the same rent which others offer. If a new man, however, has made any improvements, such as digging a well, or planting a garden, he must be paid for his trouble before the former occupant can resume his possession.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Gaudas</em> and renters.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">To each village there is an hereditary <em>Gauda</em>, who at any rate gets a share of the wet crops on their division, and performs the village sacrifices, which are here made to the <em>Cumba</em> (pillar), the image of the village god. The renter performs the other duties; and he agrees to give so much to the government, and makes as much as he can, consistently with the rules of the village. Each year the <em>Amildar</em> lets the village to the highest bidder. The renter finds security for his personal appearance when called upon, but not for the payment of his rent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manner of dividing the wet crops.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In dividing a heap of rice, between the cultivator and renter, the following is the custom of this place. The heaps contain, upwards, from 20 <em>Colagas</em>, or 1920 <em>Seers</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Seers</em> 6 are first taken for the gods, and are divided among the <em>Pujáris</em> of the village temples, and the <em>Panchánga</em>. 5 are divided between two <em>Jangamas</em>, and one <em>Dáséri</em>. 1½ is given to the <em>Panchánga</em>. <em>Seers</em> 12½</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The heap is then measured, and divided by the following rule: <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-437" data-page="437" data-folio="389" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n436_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 389">[p. 389]</button> If the field has been entirely watered by a reservoir, the cultivator gets one half. If he has used the <em>Capily</em> entirely, he gets two-thirds. If the water has come from a canal, he gets five-ninths. If the water in the tank lasts for 3 months, and afterwards the cultivator must use the <em>Capily</em>, he gets three-fifths.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A portion is left at the bottom; from which, for every <em>Candaca</em> that has been in the heap, are taken,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li><em>Seers</em> 144 to be divided equally among the <em>Gauda</em>, or hereditary chief, <em>Shanaboga</em>, or accomptant, iron-smith, <em>Talliari</em>, and <em>Toty</em>, or watchmen, washerman, barber, carpenter, and pot-maker.</li><li>26 for the <em>Nirgunty</em>, or conductor of water.</li><li>96 for the <em>Madiga</em>, or tanner who makes the <em>Capily</em>.</li><li>12 for the <em>Shanaboga</em>, or accomptant.</li><li>12 for the <em>Gauda</em>, or hereditary chief.</li><li>66 a scramble now takes place, and each of the above mentioned persons, the mendicants excepted, takes about 6 <em>Seers</em>.</li><li>12½ given before.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">366½ <em>Seers</em>, if the heap contained 1920, are thus given away. The proportion on a larger heap will be some trifle less. If any remain, it is divided between the cultivator and renter, by the same rule as before. The <em>Madiga</em>, or tanner, gets also the sweepings. Such a manner of division could only be continued from its offering great opportunities to defraud government.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Work performed by one plough, stock.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Servants wages.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It is here estimated, that a plough will cultivate as much dry-field as would sow 96 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Ragy</em>, or about 6 acres; or as much watered land as would sow three <em>Colagas</em> of <em>Paddy</em>, or about 4½ acres. If both be mixed, it would cultivate about 4½ acres of dry-field, and 3¾ of watered. A plough requires at least one man and two oxen; but if the farm be properly stocked, there ought to be three for each plough. Many farmers in every part of the country <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-438" data-page="438" data-folio="390" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n437_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 390">[p. 390]</button> are so poor, that they cannot stock a farm of one plough; and for this purpose two, or even three, are sometimes obliged to unite their capitals. A man who keeps three or four ploughs is a wealthy person. Some first-rate farmers possess as far as ten; yet the most favourable situation, of a proper mixture of watered land and dry-field, does not make his farm more than eighty-two acres and a half. A farm of this kind, fully stocked, constantly requires ten ploughmen, two other men, and ten women servants, besides some additional hands at seed-time and harvest. A man's wages here are 6 <em>Fanams</em>, or about 4<em>s</em>. a month; a woman's 5 <em>Fanams</em>, or 3<em>s</em>. 4<em>d</em>. The labouring servants, or <em>Batigaru</em>, live in their own houses. The old women of their families live at home, cook, spin, take care of the children, and do all domestic labour; the men, and their young wives, hire themselves out to the wealthy farmers, on the same conditions of service as at <em>Seringapatam</em>. Pregnancy occasions scarcely any interruption in the labour of the women, who are very hardy.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Frequent scarcity.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although almost every year the scarcity of rain, and the partial nature of that which comes, occasions in some part of the country above the <em>Ghats</em> a greater or less scarcity of grain; yet in the time of peace, famine seldom comes to such a height, that many die of absolute want. From those parts of the country that have been most favoured with rain, the superfluous corn is transported to the parts where the crop has failed; and although it is high priced, the poor are able to get as much as prevents them from immediately dying; although the scantiness of their aliment, no doubt, frequently induces disorders that terminate in death. It is said, that one fourth of the grain which, in times of plenty, the people usually consume, is sufficient to keep them alive, and enable them to work for their subsistence. It is when war is joined to scarcity, and interrupts the transportation of grain, that famine produces all its horrors. These were never so severely felt here, as during the invasion of Lord Cornwallis; when, the country being attacked on all sides, and penetrated in every direction by hostile armies, or <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-439" data-page="439" data-folio="391" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n438_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 391">[p. 391]</button> by defending ones little less destructive, one half at least of the inhabitants perished of absolute want, and repaid dearly for the miseries which they had formerly inflicted on the wretched people of the lower <em>Carnatic</em>. I do not mean, by this, to reflect on the noble leader of the British army: the people, every where that it came, seem sensible that he avoided, as much as was practicable, doing them any injury.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Gydda Cavila</em>, or keeper of forests.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In every <em>Taluc</em>, or district, where there are forests, there is a <em>Gydda Cavila</em>, who annually pays to the government a certain sum, and has the exclusive privilege of collecting honey, wax, and lac. On all such as cut timber for building their houses, he also levies a duty; and all the trees, except sandal-wood, are in fact his property. The government ought to pay him for all the trees which it requires; but this is generally omitted, an Indian government rarely paying for any thing which it can get by force. The keeper of the forest exacts also small duties on those who, without being privileged, feed their goats and cattle in the woods; on the women, who collect the leaves, which are used as platters by all ranks in this country; and on those who collect firewood, and grass for thatch.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sandal wood.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this district there are many sandal wood trees; but of so bad a quality, that they are never cut.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lac.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the hills in this vicinity, about a hundred <em>Maunds</em> of lac (almost 24 hundred weight) are annually procured; and there is more in several of the neighbouring districts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bees.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. <em>July 25, &amp;c.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The bees here are of four kinds: I. That from which most of the honey and wax is procured, is called <em>Hegenu</em>. This is a large bee, which builds under projections of the rocks, or in caverns. A large nest gives 8 <em>Seers</em>, <em>Seringapatam</em> weight, of honey=4 <sup>85</sup>⁄<sub>100</sub> lb., and 3 <em>Seers</em> of wax 1 <sup>32</sup>⁄<sub>100</sub> lb. A small hive gives about one third of this quantity. The honey is gathered twice a year, in <em>Ashádha</em> and <em>Mágha</em>, or in the month following the summer solstice, and the second after that of winter. Some people of the <em>Baydaru</em> cast make <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-440" data-page="440" data-folio="392" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n439_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 392">[p. 392]</button> the collecting of honey and wax a profession, and it is one attended with much danger. Having discovered a hive, some of them kindle a fire under the rock, and throw on it the leaves of the <em>Cassia fistula</em>, and of the <em>Puleseri</em>, which emit a smoke so acrid, that nothing living can endure it. The bees are forced to retire; and some others of the <em>Baydas</em>, so soon as the smoke subsides, lower down by a rope one of their companions, who with a pole knocks off the nest, and is immediately drawn up again; for, if he made any delay, the bees would return, and their stinging is so violent, that it endangers life. In order to fortify him against the sharp points of rocks, and against injury from the rope, which passes round his chest, the adventurous <em>Bayda</em> is secured, before and behind, by several folds of leather. II. The bee, that produces the next greatest quantity of honey is called the <em>Cadi</em>, or <em>Chittu Jainu</em>; that is, stick, or small honey. This bee is very small, and builds, around the branch of a tree, a comb of an oblong shape, and sharpened at both ends. It is found at all seasons, but is in the greatest perfection at the same time with the other. The honey is of the finest quality; but the whole comb seldom weighs more than two <em>Seers</em>, or 1 <sup>2</sup>/<sub>10</sub> lb. This bee does not sting, and is readily driven away by a twig switched round the comb. III. The <em>Tuduvay</em> is a bee of which the honey is of an excellent quality, but rarely procured; for it generally builds deep in the crevices of rocks, where it is totally inaccessible. Sometimes, however, it is found in hollow trees, and one hive will give from 20 to 25 <em>Seers</em> of honey, or about 12 or 15 pounds; but the quantity of wax is in proportion small. This is a large bee; but it very seldom stings those who plunder its hive. IV. The <em>Togriga</em> is a very small bee, that seldom stings. It takes possession of the deserted nests of the white ants (<em>Termes</em>), which in this country are very numerous in the wastes of red soil, such as is usually cultivated for <em>Ragy</em>. Of this stiff earth, the white ants raise hills resembling the stump of a tree, which are from four to six feet high, very hard, and able long to resist the heaviest rain. These, when deserted, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-441" data-page="441" data-folio="393" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n440_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 393">[p. 393]</button> most commonly become the lurking places of snakes; but sometimes give shelter to the <em>Togriga</em> bee. Its nest is therefore easily accessible; but it is very small, and contains only about a <em>Seer</em> of honey, and half a <em>Seer</em> of wax.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wild-date, or <em>Elate sylvestris.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the seed dropped by birds, or by accident, great numbers of the palm called <em>Ejalu</em> (<em>Elate sylvestris</em>) grow here wild. It will thrive on any good soil that does not contain lime, and grows indeed on the poorest lands; but in these it affords hardly any juice. To rear it requires no trouble, as the prickly nature of its leaves sufficiently deters cattle. The English use only one name for the juices of all the different palm-trees in India, and call them all <em>Toddy</em>, which seems to be a corruption of <em>Tári</em>, the Mussulman name for the juice of the <em>Palmira</em>, or <em>Borassus flabelliformis</em>. The natives have distinct names for each kind of juice; and, in fact, there seem to be considerable differences in their qualities. That of the <em>Elate</em> is by the Mussulmans called <em>Sindy</em>; in the <em>Karnata</em> language <em>Henda</em>; and in the <em>Telinga</em> and <em>Tamul</em> dialects <em>Callu</em>. The juice of the <em>Borassus</em>, although the tree grows well enough, is here never extracted, and the natives deny their extracting <em>Sindy</em>. The <em>Sindy</em> is never drunk by the natives till it has fermented, when it becomes exceedingly intoxicating, and in many villages great quantities are consumed. In this place it is never distilled; though, no doubt, it would afford a spirit that, by rectification and age, might be made palatable. Much of the <em>Sindy</em>, when fresh, is boiled down into <em>Jagory</em>, which sells for about ⅓ of the price of that made from sugar-cane, and is chiefly used for distillation. The process here is exactly the same as that described at <em>Waluru</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manner of letting these palms.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">All the <em>Ejalu</em> palms in this district are let to a person of the <em>Idiga</em> cast, who pays annually 120 <em>Pagodas</em>, or rather more than 40<em>l</em>. and lets them out again to the <em>Idigas</em> of the different villages. Each palm gives juice for three months in the year, and they will do this at any season; so that every man divides his trees into four <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-442" data-page="442" data-folio="394" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n441_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 394">[p. 394]</button> portions, and thus has throughout the year a regular supply and employment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The juice of the <em>Elate sylvestris</em> is extracted by cutting a deep horizontal gash into the stem, at some distance below the leaves, and then cutting towards this from below in a sloping direction. The juice exsudes from the pores of the sloping surface, and is collected in a notch formed at its lower extremity; whence it is conveyed into a pot by one of the divisions of the leaf, which serves as a gutter. According to his alertness, one man can collect the juice of from 30 to 50 palms. 50 good trees, or 100 very bad ones, give 70 <em>Pucka Seers</em>, or about 17 ale gallons; and this may be boiled into 70 <em>Cucha Seers</em> of <em>Jagory</em>, or about 46½ lb. At sun-rise it is put in earthen pots, and boiled until noon. When the ebullition becomes so violent as to endanger the running over of the liquor, it is allayed by a small quantity of the emulsion of <em>Ricinus</em> seed. Small holes are then made in the ground, and in the bottom of each are placed two cuttings of any twining plant. Over these are laid some leaves, upon which the boiling <em>Jagory</em> is poured. When it has cooled, it is lifted out by means of the projecting ends of the twining plant. This palm is of very little other use. Mats are made of its leaves, and its stem is used in building the wretched huts of the poorer class of inhabitants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Idigaru</em>, or <em>Inrawanlu</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Idigas</em>, or <em>Idigaru</em>, are a cast of <em>Telinga</em> origin; and, though they have lost all tradition concerning the time when they settled in this country, they still retain their original language. In this they are called <em>Inrawanlu</em>. They can all eat in common, but keep up the purity of the breed by marrying only in certain families whose descent is known. Like the <em>Shanar</em> of <em>Madras</em>, their proper business is to extract the juice of palm trees, to make it into <em>Jagory</em>, and to distil it into spirituous liquors; but some few of them have become farmers. They wish to be called <em>Súdras</em>; but their claim to be of a pure descent is not acknowledged by the <em>Bráhmans</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-443" data-page="443" data-folio="395" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n442_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 395">[p. 395]</button> and they appear never to have been permitted to carry arms. The <em>Idigas</em> can read and write accompts. Although they eat animal food, they are prohibited from drinking even palm-wine. The men are allowed a plurality of wives, but can divorce them for no cause except adultery. Adultresses and widows cannot marry again; they may, however, become concubines, or <em>Cutigas</em>. All the descendants of these form an inferior kind of breed, called also <em>Cutigas</em>, with whom those who are descended from chaste mothers will not intermarry. The women sell the produce of their husband's labour, and manage household affairs; but never toil in the fields. Even after the age of puberty they continue to be marriageable, and are not permitted to bury themselves with their husband's bodies. They have no hereditary chiefs; but the renter, with a council as usual, settles all disputes, and punishes by fine all transgressions against the rules of cast. At their marriages, and at the monthly and annual ceremonies performed in commemoration of their deceased parents, the <em>Panchánga</em>, or astrologer, reads <em>Mantrams</em>. Their <em>Guru</em> is of the cast called <em>Satánana</em>, and is named <em>Cadry Singaia</em>. Near this place he has two houses, and his office being hereditary, he is a married man. He reads to them the history of the gods, written in the <em>Telinga</em> language; gives them holy water, admonishes them to wear the mark of <em>Vishnu</em> on their foreheads, and from each person he receives two <em>Fanams</em> as charity. His visits are about once in two years. With such a <em>Guru</em>, the principal object of their worship is of course <em>Vishnu</em>; but they also offer sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>, and to the <em>Vírika</em>, or men who, on account of chastity, have been sainted. All other good men are supposed to become powerful spirits, but are not objects of worship. Bad men are punished in hell. This cast do not take the vow of <em>Dáséri</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Curubas</em>, properly so called.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. July 25, &amp;c.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 30.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Curubaru</em> are an original cast of <em>Karnata</em>, and, wherever they are settled, retain its language. They are divided into two tribes, that have no communion, and which are called <em>Handy Curubaru</em>, and <em>Curubaru</em> proper. These last again are divided into a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-444" data-page="444" data-folio="396" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n443_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 396">[p. 396]</button> number of families; such as the <em>Any</em>, or elephant <em>Curubaru</em>; the <em>Hal</em>, or milk <em>Curubaru</em>; the <em>Colli</em>, or fire <em>C.</em>; the <em>Nelly C.</em>; the <em>Sámanta C.</em>; the <em>Coti C.</em>; the <em>Asil C.</em>; and the <em>Murhindina Curubaru</em>. These families are like the <em>Gótrams</em> of the <em>Bráhmans</em>; it being considered as incestuous for two persons of the same family to intermarry. The proper <em>Curubas</em> have hereditary chiefs, who are called <em>Gaudas</em>, whether they be head-men of villages or not, and possess the usual jurisdiction. Some of them can read accompts, but they have no book. The proper duty of the cast is that of shepherds, and of blanket weavers; and in general they have no other dress than a blanket. A few of those who are rich have betaken themselves to the luxury of wearing cotton cloth next their skin; for all casts and ranks in this country wear the blanket as an outer garment. The dress of the women resembles that of the females of the kingdom of <em>Ava</em>. The blanket is put behind the back, and the two upper corners, being brought forward under the arms, are crossed over the bosom, and secured by the one being tucked under the other. As their blanket is larger than the cloth used by the women of <em>Ava</em>, the dress is more decent. The <em>Curubaru</em> were, besides, <em>Candachara</em>, or militia; cultivators, as farmers, as servants, and as gardeners; <em>Attavana</em>, or the armed men who serve the <em>Amildars</em>; <em>Anchay</em>, or post-messengers, and porters. They are allowed to eat animal food, but in most places are not permitted to drink spirituous liquors. In other places this strictness is not required; and almost every where they intoxicate themselves with palm-wine. The women are very industrious, and perform every kind of work except digging and ploughing. Even after the age of puberty they continue marriageable, and can only be divorced for adultery. In this cast the custom of <em>Cutiga</em>, or concubinage, prevails; that is, all adulteresses who are turned away by their husbands, and have not gone astray with a strange man, and all girls and widows, to whom a life of celibacy is disagreeable, may live with any man of the cast who chooses to keep them. They are looked down upon <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-445" data-page="445" data-folio="397" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n444_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 397">[p. 397]</button> by their more virtuous sisters; but still are admitted into company, and are not out-casts. Among the <em>Curubaru</em>, the children of concubines do not form a separate cast, but are allowed to marry with those of a pure breed. By a connection with any man, except a <em>Curuba</em>, a woman becomes an entire out-cast. The men take several wives; and, if they be good workers, do not always divorce them for adultery; but, as they thus incur some disgrace, they must appease the anger of their kindred by giving them an entertainment, and the <em>Guru</em> generally interposes his authority to prevent a separation. The <em>Curubas</em> believe, that those men who die without having been married become <em>Vírikas</em>, to whose images, at a great annual feast, which is celebrated on purpose, offerings of red cloth, <em>Jagory</em>, rice, &amp;c. are made. If this feast be omitted, the <em>Vírikas</em> become enraged, occasion sickness, kill the sheep, alarm the people by horrid dreams, and, when they walk out at night, strike them on the back. They are only to be appeased by the celebration of the proper feast. The peculiar god of the cast is <em>Bir'-uppa</em>, or father <em>Biray</em>, one of the names of <em>Siva</em>; and the image is in shape of the <em>Linga</em>; but no other person prays to <em>Siva</em> under this name, nor offers sacrifices to that god, which is the mode by which the <em>Curubas</em> worship <em>Bir'- uppa</em>. The priests who officiate in the temples of this deity are <em>Curubas</em>. Their office is hereditary, and they do not intermarry with the daughters of laymen. In some districts, the <em>Curubas</em> worship another god, peculiar, I believe, to themselves. He is called <em>Battay Dévaru</em>, and is a destructive spirit. They offer sacrifices to him in woods, by the sides of rivulets, or ponds. The carcases of the animals killed before the image are given to the barber and washerman, who eat them. Beside these, the <em>Curubaru</em> offer sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>, and pray to every object of superstition (except <em>Dharma Rája</em>) that comes in their way. They are considered as too impure to be allowed to wear the <em>Linga</em>, as their <em>Guru</em> does. This person is called a <em>Wodear</em>, or <em>Jangama</em>; but he is married, and his office is hereditary. His title is <em>Rávana Siddhéswara</em>, and he originally <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-446" data-page="446" data-folio="398" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n445_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 398">[p. 398]</button> lived at <em>Sarur</em>, which is near <em>Kalyána pattana</em>. At his visits he bestows consecrated ashes, and receives charity. He has a fixed due on marriages, and sends his agents to collect it. At some of their ceremonies the <em>Panchánga</em> attends, and acts as <em>Puróhita</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Badavana-hully.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">30th <em>July.</em>—I went four cosses to <em>Badavana-hully</em>, or the <em>poor man's village</em>; which is fortified with a mud-wall and a strong hedge, and contains about twenty houses of cultivators. In the former war it fell into the hands of <em>Purseram Bhow's</em> army; and, although the inhabitants have lived ever since in perfect security, it has not yet recovered one half of its former population. The disease among the cattle last year did not extend toward this quarter farther than <em>Chica Bala-pura</em>; but this year it has killed one half of the stock.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The country through which I came to-day consists of vallies interspersed with detached barren hills. In these vallies there has been formerly a good deal of cultivation; at present however they are not half peopled. A great part of the country is covered with the wild date palm, or <em>Elate sylvestris</em>, of which no care is taken. Even on bad soils it seems to be so thriving, that I have no doubt but that even there it is sufficiently productive of juice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">July 31. Appearance of the country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">31st <em>July.</em>—I went four cosses to <em>Sira</em>. The greater part of the country, through which I passed to-day, is covered with trees, which are rather higher than is usual in the wastes of this country. Among them were many wild date palms. The <em>Sultan</em>, as I have already mentioned, with a view of enforcing the doctrine of his religion, which forbids the use of intoxicating liquors, gave orders that all these should be cut. Like most of his other regulations, this seems to have been very ill obeyed; for in the central parts of his dominions no tree seems to be in such abundance. On the way, I passed two ruinous villages, and one still inhabited; but by far the greater number of the fields were uncultivated, and by far the greater part of the country shows no traces of its ever having been reclaimed, although it seems in very few places to be too steep or too barren for the plough.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-447"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="447" data-folio="399" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n446_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 399">[p. 399]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Aug. 1—6. State of the country near <em>Sira.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the 1st to the 6th of <em>August</em>, I remained at <em>Sira</em>, investigating the state of that neighbourhood; as being the principal place in the central division of the <em>Rájá’s</em> dominions north from the <em>Cavery.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Sira</em>, for a short time, was the seat of a government which ruled a considerable extent of country, and seems to have been at its greatest prosperity under the government of <em>Diláwur Khan</em>, immediately before it was conquered by <em>Hyder</em>. It is said, that it then contained 50,000 houses, of which Mussulmans occupied a large proportion. By this change of masters <em>Sira</em> suffered greatly; not owing to any oppression from <em>Hyder</em>, but from its being deprived of the expenditure attending the court of a <em>Mogul Nabob</em>. It was also much reduced by the <em>Marattah</em> invasions, which had nearly proved fatal to the rising power of its new master; and its ruin was accomplished by his son <em>Tippoo</em>, who removed twelve thousand families, to form near his capital the new town of <em>Shahar Ganjam</em>. About three hundred houses remained, when the <em>Marattah</em> army, under <em>Purseram Bhow</em> and <em>Hurry Punt</em>, took up their head quarters in the fort, which is well built of stone, and of a good size. These invaders did no harm to the town, but destroyed most of the villages in the neighbourhood, and many of these still continue in ruins. The town itself, although the seat of an <em>Asoph</em>, or Mussulman lord lieutenant, continued to languish till it came under the English protection. It is little more than a year since the army under General Harris encamped here on its route to <em>Chatrakal</em>; and since that time two thousand houses have been built; many of its former inhabitants, whom the <em>Sultan</em> had forced to <em>Seringapatam</em>, have returned to their native abode; and others are coming in daily from the country that has been ceded to the <em>Nizam</em>. The only building in the place worth notice is the monument of a Mussulman officer, who commanded here during the <em>Mogul</em> government; but it is abundantly supplied with tombs of men who by the <em>Muhammadans</em> are reputed saints, and near which the people of that <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-448" data-page="448" data-folio="400" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n447_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 400">[p. 400]</button> faith are anxious to be buried, as they consider the ground holy. The only considerable temple was pulled down by <em>Bahadur Khan</em>, the last <em>Asoph</em> of the place ; who was building a monument for his wife with the materials, when the arrival of the British army put a stop to such proceedings.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Villages strongly fortified.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Near <em>Sira</em> the quantity of watered ground is greater than that of dry-field ; but unfortunately it is situated in a very dry climate ; so that, during the last fourteen years, the tanks have been filled only five times so as to give a full crop. In the other nine years, by means of the little rain that fell, and by the use of the machine called <em>Capily</em>, the inhabitants have been able to raise a quarter of the full crop ; and one third of the whole grain consumed in the country has been brought from other places, especially from the banks of the <em>Cavery</em>. Scarcity is therefore a common evil ; and in the memory of young men, famine has several times spread all her terrors over this unfortunate place. Although in the immediate neighbourhood of a powerful garrison, all the villages are strongly fortified. On asking the reason of such precautions from a very intelligent chief of a village, from whom I took most of my information, he told me, that it was chiefly on account of robbers, who in the time of famine were very numerous. During this calamity the inhabitants of one village wish, by plundering their neighbours, to support life ; and of course, expecting the same treatment, each is shut up, and guarded from the nocturnal attacks of its neighbours, as if these were its most inveterate enemies. In war also the people have found these fortifications very useful. In their defence they employ few weapons except stones, which both men and women throw with great dexterity, and equal boldness. They do not attempt to defend themselves against any thing that wears the face of a regular body of men ; but they stone, with the greatest intrepidity, the irregular cavalry that attend all native armies, and who are seldom provided with fire-arms. On a visit which I made to the chief above mentioned, he boasted, that with ten men he had <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-449" data-page="449" data-folio="401" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n448_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 401">[p. 401]</button> beaten off 200 of the <em>Marattah</em> cavalry, of whom several men and horses were killed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Aug 1—6. Watered-land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In favourable years the greatest part of the watered-land is cultivated with rice. In dry seasons a little only of this grain is raised, and the cultivation consists chiefly of transplanted <em>Ragy</em>, wheat, <em>Jola</em>, and <em>Navony</em>, which require less water. Sugar-cane is always cultivated. Gardens occupy the remainder of the watered-land. The kitchen gardens, in the whole district, amount to only four or five <em>Colaga</em> lands; that is, to about six acres. The palm gardens now amount to ten <em>Candaca</em> lands, about 300 acres; and before <em>Purseram Bhow's</em> invasion extended to three times that size. In <em>Sira</em>, and the districts south from it, are many very valuable plantations of this kind, producing the <em>betel-nut</em> of the kind called <em>Wallagram</em>; but, until I get more into the heart of that part of the country, I shall defer giving an account of them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation of watered-land.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the accompanying table, as at <em>Madhu-giri</em>, I have given some of the particulars of the cultivation of watered-grounds, and shall afterwards proceed to detail the remainder.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-450"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="450" data-folio="402" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n449_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 402">[p. 402]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6. Rice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The observations made at <em>Madhu-giri</em> on the cultivation of rice apply here in every respect, except that at <em>Sira</em> there are no <em>Callivays</em>, or canals brought from rivers; but the whole is watered from reservoirs, or by machinery. Two <em>Capilies</em>, wrought by two men and four oxen, water a <em>Colaga</em> of land (1½ acre) that is cultivated with rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Transplanted Ragy, <em>Cynosurus Coracanus</em>, of the kind called <em>Doda.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The transplanted <em>Ragy</em> here is of two kinds: one is called <em>Doda Ragy</em>, and is that which at <em>Madhu-giri</em> is called <em>Gydda</em>, and which is there cultivated on dry-field. Here, on account of the different manner in which it is raised, it grows to a larger size. This is taken as a <em>Kártika</em> crop; but for this there is not time, if the supply of water has been sufficient for a <em>Vaisákha</em> crop of rice; and the crop of rice that follows it is but indifferent. The differences between the cultivation of this crop here, and at <em>Madhu-giri</em>, are as follow: The seedlings are watered twice a day, till they are two inches high; then only once a day. In 20 days they are fit for transplanting; and, before they are pulled, it is considered as necessary to loosen the soil by inundation. The field has five ploughings, and before the last is manured with dung. It is divided into plots by the same process as that which at <em>Madhu-giri</em> is used in the cultivation of <em>Jola</em>. The planted <em>Ragy</em> has water for the first time on the eighth day, and afterwards once only in 15 days. It is never weeded, but by occasionally plucking up with the hand any grass that may have grown.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Transplanted Ragy, of the kind called <em>Tripathi.</em></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The other kind of <em>Nat' Ragy</em> is called <em>Tripathi</em>, and grows in place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop; but it is evident, from the time required to bring this to maturity, that in one year these two crops can never be taken from the same field. The mode of cultivation is the same as for <em>Doda Ragy</em>; but the seed time is <em>Aswaja</em>, and the harvest <em>Vaisákha</em>. For a <em>Wocula-land</em> cultivated with <em>Ragy</em>, one <em>Capily</em>, with one man and two oxen, can raise a sufficient quantity of water.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Jola Holcus sorghum.</em></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">On rice land the farmers never willingly sow <em>Jola</em>; as, even should they have water, it totally prevents them from having rice <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-452" data-page="452" data-folio="404" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n451_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 404">[p. 404]</button> as the next crop; but, in order to prevent the lands from being waste, the renters frequently compel them to cultivate it. This is one great evil of the tenure that has here been adopted, of letting the villages to the annual renters who bid the highest price. These men must make up their rent in the best manner they can; but they care not what injury they do to the land; as, if it be spoiled, they will next year offer less rent. By this means, in the end, both the farmer and the revenue suffer.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Vaisákha</em> crop.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Jola</em> is most commonly taken in place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop, and, if there has been a preceding crop of <em>Doda Ragy</em>, cannot be cultivated; as the season is lost, before the <em>Ragy</em> is cut. After the <em>Jola</em>, if there be water, a <em>Kártika</em> crop of <em>Ragy</em>, and then a <em>Vaisákha</em> crop of rice, follow. In the course of the two months preceding and the one following the autumnal equinox, plough four times. In the course of the next month, after a rain, or after having watered the field, plough a fifth time, and drop the seed in the furrows, either with the hand or with the <em>Sudiky</em> (Figure 26) tied to the plough. Then form the field into plots, as described at <em>Madhu-giri</em>. At the end of six weeks after being sown, the <em>Jola</em> is allowed one watering, and another again in a month afterwards. A <em>Capily</em>, wrought by two men and two oxen, waters a <em>Colaga</em> of land, or about 1½ acre; but in the intermediate time the men and cattle do much business. In the same manner are cultivated all the three kinds of <em>Jola</em> that are mentioned in the table.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A <em>Kártika</em> crop of <em>Jola</em> is sometimes taken, from what is here called <em>Magay</em> land; this is that which, for want of rain or cultivators, has been lying fallow. On the same year no <em>Vaisákha</em> crop can follow. The next <em>Kártika</em> crop must be <em>Ragy</em>, and that may be followed by a <em>Vaisákha</em> crop of rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Navony</em>, or <em>Panicum italicum</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Kártika</em> crop, both <em>Bily</em> and <em>Mobu Navonies</em> are taken, and allow time for the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop of rice; but they injure it more than <em>Ragy</em> does. In the month preceding and that following the summer solstice, plough four or five times, and after the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-453" data-page="453" data-folio="405" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n452_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 405">[p. 405]</button> third manure with dung. In the following month, after a heavy rain, or after having watered the field, sow with the drill, and harrow with the rake drawn by oxen. It is then divided into plots like a field of <em>Jola</em>; and once a fortnight, when there is no rain, water is given.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wheats; <em>Triticum monococcum</em>, and <em>Triticum spelta</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In place of the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop, when there is a scarcity of water, wheat, both <em>Juvi</em> and <em>Hotay</em> are sown on rice-lands. These grains may be followed by a <em>Kártika</em> crop of <em>Ragy</em>; but by this process the ground is as much exhausted, as if it had been sown with <em>Navony</em>. If the <em>Kártika</em> crop be altogether left out, the <em>Vaisákha</em> crop of rice following wheat will be as good as if the ground had been regularly cultivated for rice alone; and in India it is a commonly received opinion, that, where a supply of water admits of it, ground can never be in such good heart as when regularly cultivated by a succession of rice crops. Wheat requires a clay soil, and the manner of cultivating both kinds is the same. In the two months preceding, and the one following the autumnal equinox, plough five times. In the following month, after a rain, or after having watered the field, plough again, and drop the seed into the furrows. Then divide it into squares, as for <em>Jola</em>, and water it once a month. The straw is only used for fire. If given to cattle for fodder, it is supposed capable of producing the <em>distemper</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sugar-cane.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">CHAPTER</span> VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The ground for cultivating sugar-cane is divided into two equal parts, which are alternately cultivated; one year with cane, and the other with rice. It is watered either from the reservoirs, or by the machine called <em>Capily</em>. In the last case, a field of two <em>Colagas</em>, or three acres, one half of which is in sugar-cane, and the other in rice, requires the constant labour of four men and eight oxen. Day-labourers must also be hired to rebuild the boiling-house, to tie up the cane, and to weed. When the field is watered from a reservoir, one man only is regularly employed; but to plough, to plant, to weed, and to tie up the cane, both men and cattle must be <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-454" data-page="454" data-folio="406" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n453_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 406">[p. 406]</button> hired in addition. Three kinds of cane are here cultivated. The most valued is the <em>Restalli</em>, which grows best on a black soil, in which there is much sand or gravel; a good crop of this, on a <em>Colaga-land</em>, produces 100 <em>Maunds</em> of <em>Jagory</em>; which is about 29½ hundred weight on an acre. The next in quality is the <em>Caricabo</em>, or <em>black-cane</em>. It requires a pure black mould, called <em>Eray bumi</em>; and, in a good crop, produces from a <em>Colaga-land</em>, 60 <em>Maunds</em> of <em>Jagory</em>, or from an acre nearly 17½ hundred weight. The poorest cane is the <em>Maracabo</em>, or <em>stick cane</em>. It is cultivated on the same kind of soil with the <em>Restalli</em>; but produces only half as much <em>Jagory</em> as the <em>Caricabo</em>, and that of a very bad quality, for it is quite black. The cultivation of the <em>Restalli</em>, however, is comparatively much more troublesome. In the course of the eight months following the summer solstice, the field must be ploughed eleven times; and once a month, during the whole of that time, 1000 sheep must be folded for one night on the field. It is then manured with mud from the bottoms of the reservoirs, and ploughed again twice. The channels are then formed, and in them the cuttings are laid down, two and two being always placed parallel. A <em>Colaga</em> of land requires 50,000. The channels are then filled with water, and the cuttings are trodden into the mud with the feet. The second watering is on the 4th day, the third watering on the 12th; afterwards the field, if the soil be good, must be watered once a fortnight; or once a week, if it part with its moisture quickly. On the 20th day the field is weeded with the small hoe called <em>Molu Potu</em>, which implies that the operation is done very superficially. On the 35th day the whole field is dug with the large hoe called <em>Yella Kudáli</em>; and, the earth being thrown up toward the canes in ridges, the channels for conveying the water run between the rows. About the 90th day the canes are tied up with a leaf of the plant in parcels of five or six, and once a month this is repeated. When the cane is ten months old, the crop begins, and in thirty days it must be finished. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-455" data-page="455" data-folio="407" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n454_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 407">[p. 407]</button> The farmers here say, that a fallow between the two crops of sugar-cane would not answer, and that the crop of rice gives strength to the ground.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cultivation of wheat, <em>Jola</em>, and <em>Carlay</em>, in the bottoms of dry reservoirs.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">No watered fields are here reserved for the cultivation of wheat, or of <em>Jola</em>; but when there is no rain, the bottoms of reservoirs are cultivated for these grains, and for <em>Carlay</em>. This kind of ground not being divided in <em>Colaga</em>-lands, no estimate can be formed of the produce; but both <em>Jola</em> and wheat thrive better on the rice-lands. The <em>Carlay</em> succeeds in the bottoms of reservoirs. The kind of <em>Jola</em> sown here is the <em>Agara</em>. The operation for all the three grains is the same, and is very simple. In the second month after the winter solstice, the ground is ploughed, and the seed is dropped into the furrow after the plough. It is then smoothed by drawing a plank over the ground, and no more trouble is required.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Reservoirs liable to be choked.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The soil in the bottom of the reservoirs is always a fine friable mud, being what is washed from the fields by the rains, and again deposited, when the water stagnates in the reservoirs. In all old reservoirs a great part is filled up by this soil, and thus their capacity is much diminished; and, when a village has been deserted for some time, unless the mound breaks down, its tanks in general become entirely obliterated. Nothing therefore can be more advantageous than the cultivators carrying away this mud as a manure for their land; and, as it is of an excellent quality, they would find their advantage in taking it, as fast as formed; at least in such parts of the country as afford them a permanent interest in the soil. In most places however, either from indolence or want of encouragement, or from both, the farmers neglect to remove the mud, and the public is put to a considerable expense in keeping the reservoirs clear.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Leaves used as manure.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The leaves that are here used as manure for rice lands are, the <em>Hoingay</em>, or <em>Robinia mitis</em>; the <em>Coghi</em>, or <em>Galega purpurea</em>; the <em>Yecada</em>, or <em>Asclepias gigantea</em>; the <em>Tumbay</em>, or <em>Phlomis esculenta</em>, Roxb; MSS.; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-456" data-page="456" data-folio="408" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n455_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 408">[p. 408]</button> the <em>Umuttay</em>, or <em>Datura metel</em>; the <em>Calli</em>, or <em>Euphorbium Tirucalli</em>; and the <em>Hughinay</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Aug. 1—6. Dry crops.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At <em>Sira</em> scarcely any <em>Ragy</em> is cultivated as a dry crop. Those which are raised, are <em>Suja</em>, <em>Harica</em>, <em>Huruli</em>, <em>Huts'-Ellu</em>, <em>Avaray</em>, <em>Tovary</em>, <em>Shamay</em>, <em>Navony</em>, <em>Harulu</em>, <em>Hessaru</em>, <em>Alasunda</em>, or <em>Tadaguny</em>, <em>Barugu</em>, and cotton. By far the most common are <em>Shamay</em>, <em>Suja</em>, and <em>Huruli</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Shamay</em>, or <em>Panicum miliare</em>, E. M.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Shamay</em> is of three kinds; <em>Bily</em>, <em>Cari</em>, and <em>Maliga</em>, or <em>Mujica</em>. The cultivation for the three kinds is the same, but the seeds are always kept separate. The soil that agrees with them is the <em>Marulu</em> and <em>Daray</em>, or poor sandy and stony lands. This soil, if it were dunged, would every year produce a crop of <em>Shamay</em>; but, as that can seldom be spared, the <em>Shamay</em> is always succeeded by a crop of <em>Huruli</em>, which restores the ground; and alternate crops of these grains may be continued, without any fallow, or without injury to the soil. In the first four months of the year, commencing about the vernal equinox, at any convenient opportunity plough four times. Then, after a good rain, harrow with the rake drawn by oxen, and sow with the drill; putting the seed of the <em>Shamay</em> into the <em>Curigay</em>; and that of the pulses called <em>Avaray</em>, <em>Tovary</em>, <em>Hessaru</em>, <em>Huruli</em>, or <em>Alasunda</em>, into the <em>Sudiky</em>. The first two are reckoned the best. Then harrow with the rake drawn by oxen. They have here no estimation for the extent of fields cultivated in this way; but for every 48 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Shamay</em> they sow 12 <em>Seers</em> of some one of the pulses. The produce in a good crop will be twenty seeds of the <em>Shamay</em>. Of the others no account is kept; for the legumes, as they ripen, are gathered for family use. <em>Bily Shamay</em> ripens in 3½, and <em>Kari</em> in 4 months; the <em>Maliga</em> requires only 3 months, and is therefore preferred when the rains begin late; but it gives little straw, and therefore in favourable seasons the others are more eligible. <em>Shamay</em> straw is here reckoned better fodder than that of rice; and, when mixed with the husks of <em>Huruli</em> or <em>Tovary</em>, is <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-457" data-page="457" data-folio="409" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n456_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 409">[p. 409]</button> preferred even to that of <em>Ragy</em>. Except in case of necessity, <em>Jola</em> straw is never used.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Aug. 1—6. <em>Suja</em>, or <em>Holcus spicatus</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only <em>Suja</em> or <em>Cambu</em> sown here is that called <em>Hulu</em>. It is sown on soils similar to what are used for <em>Shamay</em>, and which, on the dry-fields of <em>Sira</em>, are the most common. It is never sown two years successively on the same ground; this however is not owing to its exhausting the soil, but to its roots being troublesome to remove. Next year the ground may be easily ploughed sufficiently well for <em>Huruli</em>; and in the course of the second year the roots of the <em>Suja</em> rot, so as to allow the ground to be fully cultivated without trouble. In the month following the vernal equinox, plough four times; and after the first rain that happens in the course of the two following months, sow the seed with the drill; putting the seed of the <em>Suja</em> in the <em>Curigay</em>; and that of the pulses called <em>Huruli</em> or <em>Tovary</em> in the <em>Sudiky</em>. Then harrow with the rake drawn by oxen; or, if the field be clear of weeds, smooth it with the <em>Mara</em>, or plank. At the end of one month use, three times, the <em>Cuntay</em>, or hoe drawn by oxen. In four months it ripens without farther trouble. 20 <em>Colagas</em>, or 160 seeds, are reckoned a good crop from twelve <em>Seers</em> sowing. Cattle do not like the straw, but eat the smaller part of it when mixed with other fodder. The full grown straw, which is as thick as a man's thumb, and about ten feet high, is used for fewel.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pulse called <em>Huruli</em>, or <em>Horse-gram</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Huruli</em>, like that at <em>Seringapatam</em>, is black and white mixed. It grows better on stony than on sandy soils; and gives the greatest crops when cultivated on land that has been waste, and over-run with bushes; but it also thrives tolerably on land that is alternately cultivated with it and <em>Shamay</em>, or <em>Suja</em>. In the month which precedes and that which follows the autumnal equinox, sow the seed broad-cast, and then cover it with the plough. In four months it ripens without farther trouble, and in a good season produces ten seeds. Both straw and husks are reckoned good for labouring <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-458" data-page="458" data-folio="410" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n457_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 410">[p. 410]</button> cattle; but, owing probably to some idle prejudice, they are said to be bad for milch cows.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Aug. 1—6. A plant producing oil, and called <em>Huts'-Ellu</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Huts'-Ellu</em> is sown near villages, in places where rubbish and dirt are thrown. First, at the same season with the <em>Huruli</em>, sow the seed, and then plough the field twice. In three months it ripens without farther trouble, and in a good year produces sixteen seeds.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Navony</em>, or <em>Panicum italicum</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Navony</em> cultivated on dry-field is that called <em>Bily</em>, and is raised either on the two poorer soils, or on a black mould that has been prepared for it by a crop of the pulse called <em>Hessaru</em>. It is considered as exhausting the ground; but this is obviated by ploughing up the field immediately after the <em>Navony</em> has been cut, and thus exposing the soil to the air. In the two months following the vernal equinox, plough four times. With the next good rain, harrow with the rake drawn by oxen, and sow the seed with the drill; putting <em>Navony</em> in the <em>Curigay</em>, and the pulse called <em>Avaray</em> in the <em>Sudiky</em>. In three months it ripens without farther trouble, and in a good crop produces 96 seeds. For cattle, the straw is better than that of rice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Harulu</em>, or <em>Ricinus palma Christi</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Harulu</em>, of the kind called <em>Phola</em>, is here cultivated. For this a sandy soil is reckoned best; and as it is thought to improve the soil, the little <em>Ragy</em> that is sown on dry-field generally follows it. In the first month after the vernal equinox, plough twice; then, with the first rain in the next month, at every cubit's distance throughout the field, draw furrows intersecting each other at right angles. At every intersection drop a seed, and cover them with another furrow. After two months weed with the plough; and with the <em>Cuntay</em>, or hoe drawn by oxen, throw the earth in ridges toward the young plants. In six months it begins to give ripe fruit, which for three months is gathered once a week.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pulse called <em>Hessaru</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Hessaru</em> cultivated here is called <em>Cari</em>, or black, and requires a black soil, to which it is said to add much strength. It is therefore taken alternately with <em>Navony</em>, or with <em>Huts'-Ellu</em>, both of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-459" data-page="459" data-folio="411" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n458_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 411">[p. 411]</button> which are considered as exhausting crops. It is cultivated exactly in the same manner as <em>Huruli</em> is, ripens in three months, and in a good year produces sixteen seeds. Except for feeding camels, its straw or husks are of no use.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Grain called <em>Barugu</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><em>Barugu</em> is of two kinds; white, and black. A sandy soil of any kind agrees with this corn, which is also valuable as requiring very little rain. The straw is better fodder than that of rice. In the second month after the vernal equinox, plough three times. After the next rain, in the following month, either sow with the drill, and harrow with the rake drawn by oxen, or sow broad-cast, and plough in the seed. In three months it ripens without farther trouble, and in a favourable season produces sixteen seeds.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cotton.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Near <em>Sira</em> a very small quantity of cotton is raised; and, like that of <em>Madhu-giri</em>, it is of a quality inferior to what is brought from <em>Balahari</em>, and other places nearer the <em>Krishna</em> river. The soil on which it is sown is a black clay containing nodules of lime-stone. In the two months following the vernal equinox; plough three times. At any convenient time, in the two next months, mix the seed with dung, and drop it in the furrows after the plough, forming lines about nine inches apart. A month afterwards plough again between the lines; and in order to destroy the superfluous plants and weeds, use the hoe drawn by oxen three times, crossing these furrows at right angles. The second and third times that this hoe is used, it must follow the same track as at first; otherwise too many of the plants would be destroyed. Between each hoeing three or four days should intervene. In six months the cotton begins to produce ripe capsules, and continues in crop four more. The plants are then cut close to the ground; and after the next rainy season the field is ploughed twice in contrary directions. A month afterwards it is hoed once or twice with the same implement, and it produces a crop twice as great as it did in the first year. In the third year a crop of <em>Shamay</em> or <em>Navony</em> must be taken, and in the fourth year cotton is again sown, as at first.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-460"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="460" data-folio="412" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n459_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 412">[p. 412]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6. Increase on the seed no guide for judging of the fertility of a country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I have strong suspicions, that the chief of the village, or <em>Gauda</em>, from whom I had most of the above information, although a very sensible man, diminished in his accounts the produce of the different grains. There being no estimate of the extent of dry-lands here, it will be very difficult to form any comparison between the success of the operations of husbandry here and in other places. The increase on the seed is scarcely any guide; as in different places and countries the quantity sown on the same extent of ground is extremely various; and the only rule, on which we ought to depend, in judging of the success of agriculture, is the quantity of grain produced on the same extent of ground after deducting the seed. With the wretched climate and agriculture of the highlands of Scotland, four seeds of oats is reckoned a good crop; and at <em>Sira</em> the farmer expects 64 fold from his field of <em>Jola</em>; but after deducting seed, the highlander has 18 bushels from his acre, while the <em>Hindu</em> has only 17 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>2</sub>; for on the acre the former sows nearly 24 times as much seed as the latter does.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Actual produce, at <em>Sira</em>, of the dry-field.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I measured a field said to require 12 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Huruli</em>, or 2 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Suja</em>, or 12 <em>Seers</em> of <em>Shamay</em> for seed, and found it to contain 2 <sup>24</sup>/<sub>100</sub> acres. On these data the acre requires only about <sup>87</sup>/<sub>100</sub> parts of a peck for the seed of <em>Shamay</em> and <em>Huruli</em>; of the former, according to the <em>Gauda</em>'s rate of twenty seeds, it would produce only about 3 <sup>3</sup>/<sub>4</sub> bushels with a little pulse; and of the <em>Huruli</em> it would produce less than 2 bushels. An acre would sow about 0,124 parts of a peck of <em>Suja</em>, and produce something less than 5 bushels with a little pulse.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">I am farther confirmed in believing that the <em>Gauda</em> under-rated the produce, by having measured a field which had been last year sown with <em>Huruli</em>. It took 24 <em>Seers</em> of seed, and in a bad year produced 5 <em>Colagas</em>, or 20 seeds, which is double what he stated as the produce of a good year. The field contained 3 acres 9 chains. At this rate, the seed for an acre is about <sup>85</sup>/<sub>100</sub> parts of a peck, and the produce about four bushels. This last agrees with the account <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-461" data-page="461" data-folio="413" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n460_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 413">[p. 413]</button> obtained at <em>Madhu-giri</em> from <em>Trimula Nayaka</em>, which I consider as a fair one; and all the <em>Gauda's</em> statements will probably be found to require a similar amendment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the government of <em>Dilawur Khan</em> a measurement was made of all the <em>betel-nut</em> gardens in this district; an area of six poles in length by five in breadth being called a <em>Colaga</em>, or <em>Wocula-land</em>. About twenty years ago the <em>Sultan</em> extended this measurement to the whole district, and found that the pole was in length 13 <sup>1</sup>/<sub>3</sub> <em>Sultany Gujas</em>, each of which is equal to 37 <sup>5</sup>/<sub>8</sub> English inches. This would make the <em>Wocula-land</em> nearly 1 <sup>16</sup>/<sub>100</sub> acre; but during the <em>Marattah</em> invasion all the accompts of the measurement were lost, and the <em>Wocula-lands</em> now in use are the old computed ones which existed formerly in the public registers. Of course, from favour shown to individuals, they are of very different sizes. I measured two fields, and found that by the one the <em>Wocula-land</em> contained 1 <sup>397</sup>/<sub>1000</sub> acre, and by the other 1,<sup>5</sup>/<sub>10</sub>. In my account of the productions of watered-land, I have taken the last as the standard, as it agrees with the result of my inquiries at <em>Madhu-giri</em>. It is very probable, however, that <em>Dilawur Khan's</em> estimate is more accurate; for, by having long governed the country, he certainly had better opportunities of ascertaining the fact than I could possibly have. In this case, the seed and produce stated for an acre in the table must be increased in the proportion of 150 to 116.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Measures.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The measure of grain originally established here was 64 <em>Dudus</em>= 1 <em>Puddy</em>; 2 <em>Puddies</em>=1 <em>Seer</em>; 4 <em>Seers</em>=1 <em>Bulla</em>; 16 <em>Bullas</em>=1 <em>Wocula</em> or <em>Colaga</em>; and 20 <em>Colagas</em>=1 <em>Candaca</em>. <em>Purnea</em> has given orders, that the <em>Sultany Seer</em> should be used; and in fact it has been adopted, and 6 <em>Seers</em> are considered as equal to the <em>Bulla</em>; so that the <em>Candaca</em> and <em>Wocula</em> here are, at present, exactly the same with those of <em>Madhu-giri</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tenures.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. <em>Aug.</em> 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Except proprietors of gardens, none of the farmers here have any fixed property in their lands. The officers of government, or in their stead the renter, may give the land to any person who will <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-462" data-page="462" data-folio="414" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n461_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 414">[p. 414]</button> pay a higher rent than the former occupant. In some villages a new bargain is made once a year; in others, it is usual to make a bargain for each crop. In general, the bargain is made for a certain sum of money; at other times the farmers will only agree to cultivate the lands according to the usual division of crops. In every village the custom in doing this differs. At the <em>Kasba</em>, or chief town of the district, the following is the manner in which it is done. Aug. 1—6. Extent of land wrought by one plough; and stock.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A plough wrought by one man and two oxen, if the reservoir afford all the water that is necessary, can labour two <em>Wocula</em>-lands of irrigated ground; but, if the <em>Capily</em> be used, this stock can only cultivate one <em>Wocula</em>-land. The richest farmer in the place, who is the <em>Gauda</em>, or chief of a village, whom I have before mentioned, has ten ploughs; no other person has above six. About a fourth of the cultivators have one plough, a half two, and the remaining fourth from three to six. A farmer who has four ploughs, and who may be considered as a man in easy circumstances, ought to have four men and four women servants with eight oxen. In seed-time and harvest he will require eight additional labourers. Two men and four oxen are required to work a <em>Capily</em> from sun-rise to sun-set. Men servants hired as <em>Batigaru</em>, or by the year, get monthly 8 <em>Fanams</em>, about 5 <em>s.</em> 4 <em>d.</em>, and women get half as much. A man’s daily wages is ¼ of a <em>Fanam</em>, or about 2 <em>d.</em>; a woman’s ⅐ of a <em>Fanam</em>, or about 4½ farthings.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Weights.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The weights used here are, 22 <em>Rupees</em> = 1 <em>Seer</em>; 56 <em>Seers</em> = 1 <em>Maund</em>, or 31 <sup>14</sup>/<sub>100</sub> lb. By this every thing sold here is weighed; but goods sent to other countries, such as <em>Coco-nuts</em>, are weighed by a <em>Maund</em> of 48 <em>Seers</em>, or 26 <sup>7</sup>/<sub>10</sub> lb.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Exchange of money.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Twice a month the <em>Cutwal</em>, or officer of police for the chief town of the district, assembles the merchants, and settles the exchange of money. At present the <em>Fanam</em> exchanges for 16⅔ elephant <em>Dudus</em>, and is equal to <sup>1</sup>/<sub>13</sub> of a <em>Sultany Pagoda</em>. Small copper coins called <em>Casu</em>, and equal to ⅓ of the <em>Dudu</em>, are here in common currency; as are also the shells called <em>Cowries</em>, of which 16 are equal to one <em>Casu</em>. All accompts are kept in <em>Canter’-raya Pagodas</em> and <em>Fanams</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The merchants of <em>Sira</em> possess considerable enterprise, and carry goods to the countries ceded to the <em>Nizam</em> on the south of the <em>Krishna</em>, to the country near <em>Darwara</em> ceded to the <em>Marattahs</em>, to <em>Chatrakal</em>, to the vicinity of <em>Nagara</em>, to <em>Seringapatam</em>, and to <em>Bangalore</em>; and merchants from all these countries resort to this mart.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-464"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="464" data-folio="416" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n463_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 416">[p. 416]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6. Commerce With the <em>Nizam's</em> dominions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At present the trade with the <em>Nizam's</em> country is not safe; and the merchants, and other inhabitants, are rapidly emigrating into the <em>Rája's</em> dominions. The places, with which there was formerly an intercourse here, were <em>Ráya-durga</em>, <em>Kalyana-durga</em>, <em>Balahari</em>, <em>Gutti</em>, <em>Rajawully-Advany</em>, <em>Tadepatry</em>, <em>Pamudi</em>, <em>Dharmáwara</em>, <em>Nilomudodi</em>, <em>Penu-conda</em>, <em>Indu-pura</em>, <em>Modogusheria</em>, <em>Nedavena-hully</em>, <em>Cundurupi</em>, <em>Ratna-giri</em>, <em>Cumpuli</em>, <em>Hirialu</em>, <em>Cuddapa</em>, and <em>Condacundi</em>. The goods brought from thence are silk cloths, cotton cloths, plain and with silk borders, chintses, and coarse cotton cloths, all the manufactures of these places. The colours being better fixed, they sell higher than the goods of <em>Bangalore</em>. The returns from <em>Sira</em> are dried coco-nut kernels or <em>Copra</em>, <em>Betel-nut</em>, <em>Jagory</em>, <em>Popli</em> bark, <em>Lac</em>, and steel the manufacture of this neighbourhood. These are the staple articles; but occasionally oxen, buffaloes, boiled butter, or <em>Ghee</em>, oil, and tobacco have been sent. The merchants whom I here assembled at first asserted, that one half of the returns from hence were made in cash; but this they afterwards retracted, and alleged that the coco-nut kernels sent from hence greatly exceeds in value all the goods imported.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce with the <em>Marattah</em> country.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The intercourse with the <em>Marattah</em> country is perfectly undisturbed; and the places with which it is carried on, are <em>Darwara</em>, <em>Hubuli</em>, <em>Gudagu</em>, <em>Lechmeshura-Butcaray</em>, <em>Mulugunda</em>, <em>Catricay</em>, <em>Humsagara</em>, and <em>Havery</em>. The imports from thence are cotton wool, thread white and red, coarse and fine red cotton cloth, white cotton cloth with silk borders, dark blue cotton cloth, chints, sackcloth or <em>Goni</em>, tent cloth, matrasses, blankets, dates, raisins, almonds, walnuts, <em>Carthamus</em> flowers or <em>Cossumba</em>, asafœtida, sulphur, and redlead. The exports from <em>Sira</em> are oxen, buffaloes, <em>Popli</em> bark, a root called <em>Lavansa</em>, which, I believe, is that of the long pepper, <em>Lac</em>, and steel, with <sup>7</sup>/<sub>8</sub> of cash. Of this last, however, I am doubtful.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce with <em>Nagara</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The imports from the <em>Nagara</em> country to <em>Sira</em> are <em>Betel-nut</em>, black-pepper, cardamoms, <em>Lavanga patri</em> a leaf used in medicine, <em>Cabobchina</em> (the buds of the <em>Laurus Cassia</em>), bastard cinnamon (bark of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-465" data-page="465" data-folio="417" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n464_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 417">[p. 417]</button> the <em>Laurus Cassia</em>), and sandal-wood. The exports from <em>Sira</em> to <em>Nagara</em> are blankets, <em>Bangalore</em> cloths, country steel, tobacco, oil, boiled butter, or <em>Ghee</em>, buffaloes, and cash to the amount of one half of the imports.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce with <em>Chatrakal</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The imports from <em>Chatrakal</em> consist of ready money for the purchase of sugar.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce with <em>Seringapatam</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The country near <em>Seringapatam</em> supplies <em>Sira</em> with a great deal of grain, and receives back ready money, boiled butter, oil, dry ginger, limes, and coco-nuts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce with <em>Bangalore</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The black pepper and <em>Betel-nut</em> from <em>Nagara</em>, with some of the latter from this country, are sent to <em>Bangalore</em> for the manufactures of that place, and for the goods imported at <em>Madras</em> by sea.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Commerce with <em>Gubi</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Most of the <em>Betel-nut</em> is disposed of at <em>Gubi</em>, at which place there is annually sold about 15,000 <em>Maunds</em>, or about 3,575 hundred weight. All this is not the produce of the district of <em>Sira</em>; but is collected from several others in the neighbourhood. The <em>Gubi</em> merchants allege, that at their fairs more than double this quantity is sold.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Copra</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The grand article of produce here for exportation is the <em>Copra</em>, or dried kernel of the coco-nut. Many of the merchants make advances to the proprietors of gardens. At the time of advance the price is fixed, and the farmer has no right to sell his <em>Copra</em> to another, and to repay the merchant who made the advances. The average price is four <em>Fanams</em> a <em>Maund</em>, or 11<em>s</em>. 3¼<em>d</em>. a hundred weight.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Carriage cattle.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1–6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The cattle employed in this trade are buffaloes and oxen. The buffaloes of the <em>Nizam's</em> country are the best, and daily carry 12 <em>Maunds</em> or 320 lb. three cosses, or about nine miles. The oxen of this country-breed are the best, and daily carry 8 <em>Maunds</em>, or 213 lb. four cosses or twelve miles. In order to be able to do this, these cattle must be fed on oil-cake, or on cotton-seed and straw. The <em>Nizam's</em> country, abounding with cotton, wonderfully improves the oxen that are bred in this neighbourhood. An ox bought here <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-466" data-page="466" data-folio="418" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n465_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 418">[p. 418]</button> for four <em>Pagodas</em>, by keeping one year in the place, will be worth ten <em>Pagodas</em>. The native oxen of that country do not improve so fast, probably from having been always used to a stronger diet than those of this place have when young.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Manufactures; <em>Bily-Muggas</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The weavers in <em>Sira</em> are of two kinds; the <em>Bily Muggas</em>, and <em>Dévángas</em>. The former weave a coarse thin muslin called <em>Shilla</em>. That made for the dress of women, called <em>Shiray</em>, is the coarsest, and is called <em>Wuntacuddy</em>. It is in pieces 18 cubits long by 2 broad, and sells unbleached for 5½ <em>Fanams</em>, or about 3<em>s</em>. 8<em>d</em>. The <em>Erucudy Shilla</em>, intended for men’s dress, is finer; and is from 28 to 26 cubits long, by 1½ cubit broad. It sells from 6½ to 10 <em>Fanams</em> a piece (from 4<em>s</em>. 4<em>d</em>. to 6<em>s</em>. 8<em>d</em>.). These cloths are bought up by two sets of dyers; the <em>Niligaru</em>, who dye it blue; and the <em>Marattahs</em>, who dye with <em>Cossumba</em>, or flowers of the <em>Carthamus</em>. They advance money to the weavers for two or three pieces at a time.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Dévángas</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Dévángas</em> here make two thick coarse cloths; the one called <em>Cadi</em> is plain, and resembles what is made by the <em>Whalliaru</em> near <em>Bangalore</em>; and the other has red borders, like the cloth of the <em>Togotarus</em>. The whole of the cloth made here is used in the immediate neighbourhood.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Bily-Muggas</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1–6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Bily-Mugga</em> weavers consider this name as a term of reproach, and call themselves <em>Curivina Banijigaru</em>. They are an original tribe of <em>Karnata</em>. Some of them are dealers in cloth or grain, and a few are farmers. They have no hereditary chiefs; but infractions of the rules of cast are punished by their clergy or <em>Jangamas</em>; who are, however, bound to act by the advice of the elders of the tribe, should the fault be of such magnitude as to require excommunication. An assembly of the heads of families settles disputes. They pretend to be one of the tribes of pure <em>Banijigas</em>, and to be capable of being appointed to the priesthood. They say, that there are six tribes of proper <em>Banijigas</em>; the <em>Badagulu</em>, the <em>Pancham</em>, the <em>Stalada</em>, the <em>Turcana</em>, the <em>Jainu</em>, and the <em>Curivina</em>. All these can eat together; but cannot intermarry, unless they have been appointed <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-467" data-page="467" data-folio="419" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n466_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 419">[p. 419]</button> <em>Jangamas</em>; and the descendants of these never marry with the laity, although among themselves they lose all former distinctions. Each of these six tribes are again divided into <em>Gótrams</em>, and a man and woman of the same <em>Gótram</em> can never marry. The <em>Gótrams</em> of the <em>Curivina</em> are sixty-six in number. They may marry as many wives as they please; but cannot divorce them, except for adultery; and it is not unusual for a husband to keep his wife after she has been guilty of this crime. Women are marriageable even after the age of puberty; and widows may live with a man in a kind of left-hand marriage, and be called <em>Cutigas</em>, or concubines; but both the man with whom they live, and their children, are considered as legitimate. If a woman leaves her husband, and cohabits with another man of the same cast, she is called a <em>Hadra</em>; but her children are not disgraced. Any woman, even an unmarried one, who has connexion with a man of a strange cast, is excommunicated. A widow ought to bury herself alive in her husband’s grave; but the custom has become entirely obsolete. The people of this cast eat no animal food, nor drink any intoxicating liquor. They never take the vow of <em>Dáséri</em>. They are allowed to read all the books belonging to the sect, among which they do not reckon the <em>Védas</em>. They wear the <em>Linga</em>, and their adorations are principally directed to that emblem of <em>Siva</em>. Their women offer fruit and flowers to <em>Marima</em>, and the other <em>Saktis</em>; but this is not done by the men. They do not believe in the <em>Vírika</em>, or spirits of chaste men. Their <em>Gurus</em> are the same with those of the <em>Pancham Banijigaru</em>; the five chief thrones being called <em>Paravutta</em> at <em>Humpa</em>, <em>Verupacshy</em> near the <em>Tungabhadra</em> river, <em>Hujiny</em>, <em>Balahully</em>, and <em>Nidamavudy</em>. Their lay followers of this cast these <em>Gurus</em> make what is called <em>Detcha</em>. The <em>Detcha</em>, having shaved and washed his head, is instructed in some <em>Mantrams</em>, or forms of prayer, which are in the vulgar tongue, but which, like the <em>Upadésa</em> of the <em>Bráhmans</em>, are kept a profound secret. The <em>Guru</em> then bestows on the <em>Detcha</em> some consecrated herbs and water, and the <em>Detcha</em> in return gives him some money. This <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-468" data-page="468" data-folio="420" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n467_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 420">[p. 420]</button> ceremony is analagous to the <em>Dhana</em> of the <em>Bráhmans</em>. The <em>Gurus</em> on their circuits receive also from their followers <em>Dharma</em>, or charity, or rather duty, but have no fixed dues. The <em>Einaru</em> attend at marriages, births, and funerals, at <em>Mala-paksha</em>, as the <em>Tithi</em> of the <em>Súdras</em> is called, and at all great feasts. On these occasions they perform <em>Pujá</em> to the <em>Linga</em>, reading some <em>Mantrams</em>, in the vulgar tongue however, and pouring over it some water and flowers, which by this means are consecrated, and then are divided among the people whom the occasion has assembled. The <em>Einaru</em> then eats something that has been prepared for him, and at marriages receives a small sum of money. The <em>Panchánga</em>, or village astrologer, attends on similar occasions, and reads <em>Mantrams</em> in the vulgar language. He is of course paid for his trouble.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Karnata Dévángas</em> who do not wear the <em>Linga</em>.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Here, some <em>Dévángas</em> of the <em>Karnata</em> nation do not wear the <em>Linga</em>; but still they consider <em>Cari Baswa Uppa</em> as their <em>Guru</em>. They will eat in the house of a <em>Dévánga</em> who wears the <em>Linga</em>, but he will not return the compliment. They eat in common, but do not intermarry with the <em>Telinga Dévángas</em>, who, like themselves, worship <em>Siva</em>, without wearing his indecent badge. They eat animal food, an indulgence which has probably occasioned the separation. They ought not to drink spirituous liquors. As a kind of excuse, or pretence for eating the flesh, they offer bloody sacrifices to the <em>Saktis</em>. They take the vow of <em>Dáséri</em>, but do not pray to the <em>Vírika</em>, or spirits of men sainted for chastity. They acknowledge transmigration, as a future state of reward and punishment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Sadru Woculigas</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1–6.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Sadru Woculigas</em> are a cast of <em>Karnata</em> origin and <em>Súdra</em> birth; they are divided into two tribes that seem to have no communion; the <em>Cumblagataru Sadru</em>, and the <em>Sadru</em> simply so called. The <em>Sadru</em> proper are cultivators, both as masters and servants; they act as <em>Candachara</em>, or native militia, and sometimes trade in grain. They have no hereditary chiefs; but their disputes are settled by a council of four <em>Sadru Gaudas</em>, or chief farmers, who also punish all transgressions against the rules of cast, excommunicating licentious <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-469" data-page="469" data-folio="421" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n468_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 421">[p. 421]</button> women, and other heinous offenders, and reprimanding those who have been guilty of less enormous faults. By religion they are divided into three classes, those who worship <em>Jaina</em>, those who worship <em>Siva</em>, and those who worship <em>Vishnu</em> under the form of <em>Vencaty Rámana</em> ; but this does not prevent intermarriages, and the woman always adopts the religion of her husband. They are also divided into a number of families analagous to the <em>Gótrams</em> of the <em>Bráhmans</em> ; and a man never intermarries with a woman of the same family. They have among them a bastard race, descended from widows, who have become the kind of concubines called <em>Cutigas</em> ; but they are not numerous, and are held in great contempt by the others. The <em>Gauda</em> whom I have so often mentioned is the person that gives me the information concerning the cast. He is a worshipper of <em>Vencaty Rámana</em>, and denies any belief in a future state ; his worship of the gods being performed with a view of obtaining temporal blessings. This sect take the vow of <em>Dáséri</em>, and bury the dead. They can write accompts, but have no books nor science. They eat no animal food, and ought not to drink spirituous liquors. They are allowed as many wives as they can obtain ; but do not divorce them for any cause except adultery. Girls continue to be marriageable even after the age of puberty ; and widows are not expected to bury themselves with their husbands bodies ; but their becoming concubines of the kind called <em>Cutigas</em> is considered as very disgraceful to all their connections. Their <em>Guru</em> is <em>Tata Achárya</em>, an hereditary chief of the <em>Sri Vaishnavam Bráhmans</em>. He bestows on his followers holy-water and consecrated victuals, and accepts their charity. The <em>Panchánga</em>, or village astrologer, is their <em>Puróhita</em>, and attends at marriages, births, the building of a new house, and at <em>Mala-paksha</em>, the ceremony which the <em>Súdras</em> annually perform in commemoration of their deceased parents. The <em>Sadru</em> who worship <em>Siva</em> are but few in number, and wear the <em>Linga</em>. The third sect of <em>Sadru</em> worship only the god <em>Jaina</em>, but do not intermarry with the true <em>Jainaru</em>. These burn the dead. The <em>Gauda</em> says, that <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-470" data-page="470" data-folio="422" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n469_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 422">[p. 422]</button> formerly all the <em>Sadru</em> were <em>Jainu</em> ; but that his ancestors, disliking that religion, betook themselves to worship <em>Vishnu</em>. They have not adopted the worship of the <em>Saktis</em>, of <em>Dharma Rája</em>, nor of the <em>Vírika</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Customs of the <em>Ladaru</em>.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">CHAPTER VI. Aug. 1—6.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The <em>Ladas</em>, or <em>Ladaru</em>, have a language quite different from all the others that are spoken south of the <em>Krishna</em> river. This language they call <em>Chaurasi</em> ; and say, that it is spoken at a city called <em>Caranza</em>, which is near the river <em>Gódávari</em>. In fact, it is a dialect of that spoken near <em>Benares</em>, to which the others have much less resemblance. The <em>Ladaru</em> say, that, in consequence of a famine in their own country, about five hundred years ago, they came to this neighbourhood. They serve as cavalry ; trade, especially in horses ; and farm lands, but never cultivate them with their own hands. They assume the title of <em>Kshatryas</em> of the family of the sun, and wear a string like the <em>Bráhmans</em>. They will not intermarry with the <em>Rajputs</em>, or other pretenders to a royal descent ; but they are treated by the <em>Bráhmans</em> merely as <em>Súdras</em>, and in fact seem to be the highest rank of <em>Súdras</em> in their native country, like the <em>Kayashthas</em> of Bengal, or the <em>Kerit Nairs</em> of <em>Malyala</em>. They are of 14 different families, like the <em>Gótrams</em> of the <em>Bráhmans</em> ; and some are followers of the <em>Siva Bráhmans</em>, and some of the <em>Sri Vaishnavam</em>: but this does not produce a separation of cast ; for the woman always adopts the religion of her husband. They have no hereditary chiefs; but the affairs of the tribe are managed by an assembly of the heads of families. For small faults these assemblies reprimand ; for adultery, or for eating forbidden food, or with forbidden persons, they excommunicate. Many of them read <em>Sanskrit</em>, and study every kind of book except the <em>Védas</em>, which they never presume to inspect. My informers are worshippers of <em>Vishnu</em> ; yet their <em>Guru</em> is a <em>Smartal Bráhman</em>, who bestows consecrated victuals and holy-water, and receives their <em>Dharma</em>. When they are 6 or 7 years of age, they receive from the <em>Panchánga</em> their first thread, and <em>Upadésa</em>, at a ceremony called <em>Upanéna</em>. At this the <em>Panchánga</em> reads <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-471" data-page="471" data-folio="423" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n470_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 423">[p. 423]</button> <em>Mantrams</em>, as also at births, marriages, full and new moons, at <em>Sankrántis</em>, or the first days of the solar months, at funerals, and at the <em>Mala-paksha</em> lately mentioned. These <em>Ladas</em> sacrifice to the <em>Saktis</em>, especially to the goddess <em>Bhawání</em>. The <em>Pujáris</em> or priests in the temples of this idol are called <em>Bombolas</em>, who observe the rules of <em>Sannyási</em>, especially celibacy, and yet go absolutely naked. They have disciples, who are also <em>Sannyásis</em>, but who are not considered as sufficiently holy to be allowed to show their nudities. Part of the sacrifices are eaten by the votary, and part by the <em>Bombola</em>; but the animal is sometimes made a burnt offering to the idol, which in this country is done by no other cast. This burnt-offering is by the <em>Ladas</em> called <em>Hómam</em>, which is the same name that the <em>Bráhmans</em> use for their burnt-offerings ; but these always consist of flour, or other vegetable matter. It is true, that the <em>Bráhmans</em> have a burnt sacrifice of animals, which they call <em>Yagam</em> ; but it must be preceded by such a severity of penance, and is attended with such enormous expense, that no one in these degenerate days is either willing or able to undertake such an offering. The proper <em>Sakti Pújá</em>, that ought to be performed to <em>Bhawání</em>, has also fallen into disuse here among the <em>Ladas</em> ; but my interpreter says, that at <em>Madras</em> it is very common. The votary takes an animal, and offers it as a sacrifice to the idol in presence of a beautiful young woman who is perfectly naked. It is supposed, that any person who, while in the performance of this sacred ceremony, should even look with desire at the charms exposed to his view, would be instantly struck dead ; no one, therefore, undertakes it who has not great confidence in the power which he has over his passions. By the <em>Bráhmans</em> this ceremony is much condemned, and ought to deprive any one of his cast that attempted its performance ; yet some of them are said in a private manner to have recourse to this superstition, as it is supposed to have wonderful efficacy in procuring temporal success and felicity. Some of the <em>Ladaru</em> take the vow of <em>Dáséri</em>, and at the same time receive <em>Chakrántikam</em>. These beg only one day in the week, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-472" data-page="472" data-folio="424" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/journeyfrommadra01hami_2/page/n471_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 424">[p. 424]</button> following on the other six their usual professions; and they never travel about as vagabonds making a noise with bells and conchs. The <em>Ladas</em> burn their dead, who ought to be accompanied on the pile by their widows; but this custom has become obsolete. Widows are not permitted to become concubines of the kind called <em>Cutigas</em>, nor are the men allowed to keep those called <em>Hadras</em>. A girl, after ten years of age, is no longer marriageable. The men may take as many wives as they can procure, but can only divorce them for adultery. Persons of this cast drink no spirituous liquors; and, as is usual in Bengal, eat no animal food, except that which has been offered as a sacrifice.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">END OF VOL.
