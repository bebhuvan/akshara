<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-311"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="311" data-folio="289" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n310_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 289">[p. 289]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">NOTE H, p. 194.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">ON PAR<em>g</em>ANYA IN GERMAN.</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I am afraid that Slavonic scholars may think that I have represented the identity of Par<em>g</em>anya and the Lithuanian Perkuna as more certain than it really is. Though I have pointed out one difficulty, namely, the Lithuanian guttural tenuis k taking the place of a Sanskrit palatal media, I ought perhaps to have added that the transition of Perkuna into the Old Slav. Perunŭ is not free from difficulties either. G. Krek (Einleitung in die Slavische Literaturgeschichte, Gratz, 1874, p. 101) still keeps to the old derivation of Perunŭ (thunder) from a root pr, ferire, and looks upon the k as a phonetic intrusion, as in Lit. arklas = Old Slav. oralo. The name Perkuna, however, seems older than the forms without the k, for it occurs in the Lithuanian Dainos (Schleicher, Handbuch der Litauischen Sprache, vol. ii. p. 1 seq.). In Russian the name of Perun is mentioned by Nestor (about 1100 <span class="sc">A.D.</span>), while Perkunŭ still occurs in old Russian documents of the thirteenth century (Kerk, l. c., p. 101, n. 3). All this is difficult to explain; yet Slavonic scholars would hardly feel inclined to admit two different deities, one Perkunŭ, the other Perun. Here we must wait for further researches, particularly with reference to the phonetic laws of the Slavonic languages.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But if the identification of Par<em>g</em>anya with Perkuna is not quite free from doubt, this is much more the case with another identification of Par<em>g</em>anya with the Gothic faírguni, first suggested by Grimm in his Teutonic Mythology, and supported <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-312" data-page="312" data-folio="290" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n311_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 290">[p. 290]</button> by him, as may be expected, with very powerful arguments.  Faírguni in Gothic means mountain, and Grimm thinks that the chief mountains, being considered originally as the seat of the thunder-god, may after a time have been called by his name, as we speak of the St. Bernard, instead of the Mount of St. Bernard, and that, still later, the name of the chief mountain may have become the name for mountain in general.  As relics of the proper name he points out <em>Fergunna</em>, an old name of the Erzgebirge, and <em>Virgunia</em>, the tract of wooded mountains between Ansbach and Ellwangen, &amp;c. The name of the god, if it had been preserved in Gothic, would have been Faírguneis, and the existence of that name is confirmed by the Old Norse Fiörgyn, fem., gen. Fiörgynior, the goddess of the Earth, the mother of Thor, and by Fiörgynn, masc., gen. Fiörgyns, the father of Frigg, the wife of Oðin.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A young and talented scholar, Professor Zimmer, has lately supported the same view by some very ingenious arguments, in the Zeitschrift für Deutsches Alterthum, Neue Folge, vol. ii. p. 163 seq.  According to him, the Northern nations formed a feminine deity <em>Fiörgyn</em> by the side of the masculine <em>Fiörgynn</em>.  This <em>Fiörgyn</em>, as a feminine, was meant for the Earth, just as Par<em>g</em>anya's wife was P<em>ri</em>thivî, the Earth. <em>Ôðinn</em>, who took the place of <em>Tŷr</em> (Dyaus), and of the male <em>Fiörgynn</em> (Par<em>g</em>anya), was the husband of <em>Iörð</em>, the Earth, and became naturally the husband also of <em>Fiörgyn</em>, the Earth, while <em>Fiörgynn</em> himself became absorbed in <em>Thôrr</em>.  If therefore <em>Thôrr</em> is called the first son of <em>Ôðinn</em>, this is the same as Par<em>g</em>anya being called the son of <em>Dyaus</em>, and if <em>Thôrr</em> is called <em>Iarðar burr</em> and <em>Fiörgynjar burr</em>, this is the same as Par<em>g</em>anya being called the son of P<em>ri</em>thivî, though being her husband also.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Grimm in his German Dictionary, vol. i. p. 1052, thinks that Greeks and Romans, changing <em>f</em> into h, represented Fergunna or Fergunnia by Hercynia, and he traces in the end both <em>berg</em> and <em>burg</em> back to Par<em>g</em>anya.</p></div>
