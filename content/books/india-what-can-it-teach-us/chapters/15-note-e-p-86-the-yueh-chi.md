<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-300"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="300" data-folio="278" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n299_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 278">[p. 278]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">NOTE E, p. 86.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">THE YUEH-CHI (YUEH-<em>K’</em>).</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The conquests of Alexander, though they seem to have left a very slight impression in India, so much so that the very name of Alexander is never mentioned in Sanskrit literature, supplied the first impulse to great commotions in Asia, which at last reacted most powerfully and fatally on India. The kingdoms of Bactria, Syria, and Egypt were essentially the outcome of Alexander’s Oriental policy. Egypt and Syria, we know, fell after a time a prey to Roman conquest. But the Greek kingdom of Bactria came in contact with a different class of enemies, and was destroyed by the Tochâri (the Ta-hia in Chinese<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-001" href="#sec-15-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>), a Turanian race, who, after having made themselves masters of that position, advanced westward against the kingdom of Parthia, founded 250 <span class="sc">B.C.</span> by Arsaces I. Artabanus, the king of Parthia, fell fighting against the Tochâri, but his son Mithradates II (124 <span class="sc">B.C.</span>) repelled their inroads, and thereby drove an enormous wave of half-nomad warriors towards Kábul, and thence to India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Chang Kien, who was sent by the Emperor Wu-ti as ambassador to the Yueh-chi, tells us that these Yueh-chi (also called Yueh-ti, the <em>Ἐφθαλῖται</em> of Greeks) had been driven at that time out of their old seats by the Hiung-nu, and had <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-301" data-page="301" data-folio="279" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n300_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 279">[p. 279]</button> poured into Bactria, then occupied by the Tochâri (Τόχαροι of Strabo), and called Ta-hia, or Tocharia (now Tokhâristân).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This may account for the fact that Yueh-chi and Tochâri are used synonymously by later writers. Richthofen declares that the Yueh-chi of the Han dynasty correspond to the Tochâri, and that they are the Yeta of the Wei dynasty, called Hayathalah or Haithal (really Habathilah) by the Persians, and Ἐφθαλῖται or White Huns by the Byzantine historians. Colonel Yule (J. R. A. S. vi. pp. 92–120) likewise pronounces in favour of the identity of Tocharoi (Strabo), or Tu-ho-lo, or Tokhares (Arabian) with the Yueh-chi.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The beginning of the migration of the Yueh-chi from the Eastern portion of the basin of the Tarym is fixed at 157 <span class="sc">B. C.</span>, their arrival in Transoxiana and their conquest of Bactria at about 128 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> Strabo speaks of them as Ἄσιοι, Πασιανοί, Τόχαροι, and Σακάραυλοι, and as coming from beyond the Yaxartes. Ptolemy speaks of the Tokhari as holding Bactria. He also calls them Thagori, which Richthofen identifies with Hiouen-thsang’s Tu-ho-lo, which is the Sanskrit Tukhâra.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Chang Kien, who was sent by the Emperor Wu-ti to induce the Yueh-chi to make war against the Hiung-nu, met with them on the banks of the Tu-kwai-shui (Surkhâb), their northern boundary being the Oxus (Kwai-shui). This must have been between the years 139–126 <span class="sc">B. C.</span>, though rather towards the end of that time. The Yueh-chi are described as of a pink and white complexion, and as accustomed to shoot from horseback. They were then 7000 li north of India. Their country was bounded on the South by the districts lately conquered by the Ta-hia (Tochâri) and on the West by Ansik, i. e. Parthia. They were herdsmen and nomads, and resembled the Hiung-nu in manners and customs. Driven out of their seats by the Hiung-nu, they fell on the Tochâri from the West, and defeated them<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-002" href="#sec-15-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-302" data-page="302" data-folio="280" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n301_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 280">[p. 280]</button> They then followed the course of the Surkhâb, and founded a royal residence on its northern bank. Some of them took refuge in Little Tibet (Khiang or Kanka), and were called the Lesser Yueh-chi.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To the South-east of the Tochâri lay Shen-tuh, i. e. India, and when Chang Kien was with the Tochâri, he saw articles of trade brought to their country from India. India was reckoned to be some thousand li to the South-east of Ta-hia (Bactria). The country was said to be cultivated, and the manners and customs of its inhabitants were very similar to those of the Tochâri. The climate was damp and hot, and the people made use of elephants in war. It lay near a great river<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-003" href="#sec-15-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">So far our information about the Yueh-chi and their distant relation to India rests on Sze-ma Tsien, who was born in 163 <span class="sc">b. c.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-004" href="#sec-15-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">If now we proceed to the Annals of the After (or Eastern) Han Dynasty (<span class="sc">a. d.</span> 25–220), or to the Annals of the Sui Dynasty (<span class="sc">a. d.</span> 589–618), we find some more information about the same subject, for which I am chiefly indebted to Professor Legge<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-005" href="#sec-15-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Annals of the After Han Dynasty were written down by Han Yô, who was killed in 445 <span class="sc">a. d.</span>, and we there find the following account of Tien-chu, that is, India. It is said to be called also 身毒, Ken-toku or Shin-doku. Its situation is described as many thousand li South-east of the Yueh-chi. The customs of the people are said to be the same as those of the Yueh-chi. Its climate is damp and hot. The country is near to the great rivers. The people fight riding on elephants, and they are weaker than the Yueh-chi. They practise the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-303" data-page="303" data-folio="281" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n302_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 281">[p. 281]</button> religion of Futo, i. e. Buddha, and refrain from killing, and this forms their custom.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The whole region extends from a state of the Yueh-chi called Kôfu, i. e. Kábul, to the West Sea in a South-western direction, and it reaches Eastward another state called Han-ki.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Then the Han annalist, speaking of the time of that Dynasty, 25–220 <span class="sc">A. D.</span>, continues:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are in Ken-toku separate castles which are counted by hundreds, and in each castle there is a chief<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-006" href="#sec-15-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are also separate states which are counted by tens<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-007" href="#sec-15-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>; and in each state there is a king. Although there is a little difference, yet all of them are called Ken-toku or Shin-doku.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At that time (under the Eastern Han Dynasty) they all belonged to the Yueh-chi, who had killed the kings, and appointed generals to govern the people.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This seems to have happened about one hundred years after Chang Kien’s embassy, or 20 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> At that time the five tribes of the Yueh-chi were united under Kieou-tsieu-kio, who then assumed the title of Kouei-shuang (it may be Gushan or <em>Κοῤῥανος</em> of the coins<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-008" href="#sec-15-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>). He conquered the Kings of Pota and Kipin, and then invaded Tien-chu or India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The products of the country are elephants, rhinoceros, tortoise-shell, gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, and tin. The people have rare things, which are found in the country of Tai Chin or Great Chin, because they have communication with those of the Great Chin westward. There are also among the products of India fine linen, good rugs or mats made of wool <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-304" data-page="304" data-folio="282" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n303_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 282">[p. 282]</button> and fur, several kinds of incense, stone honey, black pepper, ginger, and black salt.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the time of the Emperor Hwa (89–105) they often sent messengers to China and presented something, as if it were their tribute. But afterwards those of the Western regions rebelled (against the Emperor of China), and interrupted their communication, until the second year of the period Yen-hsi (159) in the reign of the Emperor Kwan (147–167)<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-009" href="#sec-15-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the fourth year of the same period (161) the foreign people incessantly came from outside of the wall of a castle on the border at a place called Jitsu-nan.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This is an independent and, if we make allowance for Chinese modes of thought and expression, a perfectly trustworthy account of the state of things in India from the first century before to about the third century after Christ.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-15-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-15-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">The <em>Δάαι</em> are supposed to appear again as Dacians, and Grimm would have wished to connect them with Dânavas, evil spirits, and in the end with the Danes.
