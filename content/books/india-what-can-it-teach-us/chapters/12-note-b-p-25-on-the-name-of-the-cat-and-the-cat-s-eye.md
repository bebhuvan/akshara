<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-283"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="283" data-folio="261" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n282_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 261">[p. 261]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">NOTE B, p. 25.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">ON THE NAME OF THE CAT AND THE CAT’S EYE.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Our domestic cat came to us from Egypt<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-001" href="#sec-12-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, where it had been tamed by a long process of kindness, or, it may be, of worship<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-002" href="#sec-12-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>. In no classical writer, Greek or Roman, do we find the cat as a domestic animal before the third century <span class="sc">A.D.</span> It is first mentioned by Caesarius, the physician, brother of Gregory, the theologian of Nazianzus, who died 369 <span class="sc">A.D.</span> He speaks of <em>κάτται ἔνδρυμοι</em>. About the same time Palladius (De re rustica, IV. 9, 4) writes: ‘Contra talpas prodest catos (cattos) frequenter habere in mediis carduetis (artichoke-gardens). Mustelas habent plerique mansuetas; aliqui foramina earum rubrica et succo agrestis cucumeris impleverunt. Nonnulli juxta cubilia talparum plures cavernas aperiunt, ut illae territae fugiant solis admissu. Plerique laqueos in aditu earum setis pendentibus ponunt.’ Hehn supposes that <em>talpa</em> here means mouse. But whether it means mouse or mole<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-003" href="#sec-12-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>, it is clear that when <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-284" data-page="284" data-folio="262" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n283_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 262">[p. 262]</button> Palladius wrote (fourth century <span class="sc">A. D.</span>), tame mustelae were still more common than cats, whether called <em>cati</em> or <em>catti</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Evagrius scholasticus (Hist. Eccl. 17, 23), about 600 <span class="sc">A. D.</span>, speaks of <em>κάττα</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-004" href="#sec-12-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> as the common name of <em>αἴλουρος</em>, here meant, therefore, for cat. He says: <em>αἴλουρον ἣν κάτταν ἡ συνήθεια λέγει.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">And Isidorus, his contemporary, expresses himself in the same sense when saying (12, 2, 38), ‘hunc (murionem) vulgus <em>catum</em> a captura vocant.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">If we admit, in the absence of evidence to the contrary effect, that the tame cat came from Egypt to Greece and Italy in the fourth century <span class="sc">A. D.</span>, and that the shrewd little animal was called by the Romans <em>cătus</em>, everything else becomes intelligible.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the ruins of Pompeii, where the bones of horses, dogs, and goats have been found, no bones of cats have hitherto been discovered, and the pictures there which were supposed to be intended for cats, are now proved to be at all events not pictures of the tame cat<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-005" href="#sec-12-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the language of Roumania no traces exist of the word <em>catus</em>, probably because at the time when that Romanic dialect became settled in Dacia, <em>cătus</em> did not yet exist as a Latin name for cat<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-006" href="#sec-12-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Mice were very troublesome, no doubt, to Greeks and Romans, but they fought against them, and against lizards and snakes also, not by cats, but by the <em>γαλέη</em> or <em>γαλῆ</em>, the <em>ἴκτις</em>, and the <em>αἰέλουρος</em> or <em>αἴλουρος</em>. We must not suppose that the names of these animals were used by the ancients with anything like zoological accuracy. So much only is certain that, before the fourth century <span class="sc">B. C.</span>, none of them, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-285" data-page="285" data-folio="263" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n284_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 263">[p. 263]</button> when applied to animals outside Egypt<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-007" href="#sec-12-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, should be taken for our <em>Felis domesticus</em>, while Cuvier<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-008" href="#sec-12-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> maintains that in Egypt the cat-mummies, from the most ancient times, are anatomically the same as our tame cat.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">My excellent friend, the late Professor Rolleston, whom I miss more than I can say, in a paper ‘On Domestic Cats, ancient and modern,’ published in the Journal of Anatomy and Physiology, 1868, pp. 47–61, came to the conclusion that when the ancient Greeks, such as Aristotle, Aristophanes, &amp;c., spoke of γαλῆ, except in Egypt, they generally meant the <em>Mustela foina</em>, the white-breasted marten, called also beech-marten or stone-marten, sometimes the <em>Mustela furo</em>, the ferret, and the <em>Viverra genneta</em>, the genet, but never the polecat, <em>Mustela putorius</em>. What distinguishes the γαλῆ is that it destroys mice, snakes, and birds, that it steals the eggs of birds, and is offensive by its smell. The γαλῆ ἀγρία or ἴκτις shares these qualities, but it is larger and, what is important, fond of honey. Neither ferret (<em>Mustela furo</em>) nor weasel (<em>Mustela vulgaris</em>) will touch honey.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The cat, our <em>Felis domesticus</em>, may in extreme cases be brought to kill snakes, but it will not steal eggs, nor eat honey, nor go into holes, like the γαλῆ or the <em>Mustela</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The most useful talent of killing mice was shared by γαλῆ, <em>mustela</em>, weasel, and cat; and when we say, cat and mouse, the Greeks said Μῦς καὶ γαλῆ, the Romans <em>Mus et mustela</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">When the Greeks came to know the tame Egyptian cat, they transferred to it the old names of γαλῆ and αἴλουρος. Most likely κάττος was the imitation of an Egyptian word, and when Kallimachos, writing in Egypt (third century <span class="sc">b.c.</span>), speaks of the αἴλουρος, his commentator was no doubt right in saying, τὸν ἰδίως λεγόμενον κάττον.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-286"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="286" data-folio="264" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n285_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 264">[p. 264]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the Greek fables, down to Babrius, <em>αἴλουρος</em> need never be taken for a tame cat, but for a weasel, marten, or possibly a wild cat<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-009" href="#sec-12-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Romans did not transfer the name of <em>Mustela</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-010" href="#sec-12-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> to the cat, but by a kind of popular etymology, changed <em>cattus</em> into <em>cătus</em>, and these two names, <em>κάττα</em> and <em>catus</em>, found their way afterwards into nearly all the languages of Europe<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-011" href="#sec-12-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In Germany the arrival of the cat<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-012" href="#sec-12-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> must have been sufficiently early to account for the adoption of cats, instead of weasels (O.H.G. <em>wisula</em>, or <em>wisale</em>), as drawing the chariot of the goddess Freya<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-013" href="#sec-12-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup>, unless we admit that here too the cat intended was originally the wild-cat, particularly as its place is often taken by the gold-bristled boar and the falcon<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-014" href="#sec-12-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We now come to the question, whether the cat was known at an early time in India. The two principal words in Sanskrit for cat are mâ<em>rg</em>âra and vi<em>d</em>âla.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Mâ<em>rg</em>âra means the cleaner, the cat being well known for its cleanliness. The wild-cat is called ara<em>n</em>ya-mâ<em>rg</em>âra, the forest-cat (Pañ<em>k</em>atantra, p. 165, l. 14).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Manu (XI. 131) places mâ<em>rg</em>âra by the side of the nakula, the ichneumon, and in the Pañ<em>k</em>atantra (p. 110, l. 23) we <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-287" data-page="287" data-folio="265" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n286_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 265">[p. 265]</button> read that there is a natural enmity between cats and dogs (sârameya-mâr<em>g</em>ârâ<em>n</em>âm) and between ichneumons and serpents (nakula-sarpâ<em>n</em>âm). This instinctive enmity between certain animals was so well known that Pâ<em>n</em>ini gave a rule (II. 4, 9) according to which compounds may be formed of the names of such animals. But among these compounds we find in Pâ<em>n</em>ini neither cat and dog, nor cat and mouse. Pâ<em>n</em>ini knew the wild-cat, the vi<em>d</em>âla (VI. 2, 72), but not the tame cat, the enemy of dogs and mice. Nay, even Pata<em>ñg</em>ali, the author of the Mahâbhâshya, does not yet mention the cat among the animals exhibiting an instinctive hatred of other animals (II. 4, 9). He gives in the Mahâbhâshya instances (II. 4, 12, 2) of such instinctive enmities, as kâkolûkam, crows and owls, <em>s</em>vas<em>ri</em>gâlam, dogs and jackals (even <em>s</em>rama<em>n</em>a-brâh-ma<em>n</em>am, mendicants and Brâhmans), but not cat and dog, or cat and mouse. The later Kâ<em>s</em>ikâ, on the contrary, gives mâr<em>g</em>âra-mûshakam, cat and mouse, as the very first instance of II. 4, 9.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Again (IV. 2, 104), the animals mentioned by Pata<em>ñg</em>ali for a similar purpose are ahi-nakulikâ<em>h</em>, serpents and ichneumons, and <em>s</em>vâvarâhikâ<em>h</em>, dogs and boars, but not cat and dog, nor cat and mouse.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the Chinese translation of the story of Bedd Gelert, made by Fa Hian about 412 <span class="sc">a. d.</span>, the animals that hate each other instinctively are the snake and the nakula, the little bird and the hawk, the <em>S</em>rama<em>n</em>a and Brâhma<em>n</em>a, the stepmother and the child of another wife<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-015" href="#sec-12-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A strong confirmation of the comparatively late date of the cat, as the enemy of mice, in India is furnished by the Pa<em>ñk</em>atantra. Here we read (V. 109)<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-016" href="#sec-12-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>, ‘A mouse, though born in the house, must be killed, because it does mischief. A cat is asked for from elsewhere, and paid for, because it is useful.’ <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-288" data-page="288" data-folio="266" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n287_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 266">[p. 266]</button> But in the Arabic translation (Guidi’s Codd. F. and V.), instead of the cat we find the falcon; in the <em>Directorium</em> the nisus, or sparrow-hawk; in the Stephanites the <em>ἱέραξ</em>, and in the old Spanish translation, the <em>azor</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It might have been supposed at first that as the cat occurred in the Pañ<em>k</em>atantra, the Arabic translation had changed the cat into a falcon. But no. The old Syriac version, which is older than our Pañ<em>k</em>atantra, has: ‘Mice, though bred in the house, are killed on account of their mischievousness, but falcons are caught on account of their usefulness, and carried on the hand<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-017" href="#sec-12-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This leaves no doubt that in the original the simile was taken from the mice and the falcon, and that the somewhat lame simile of the cat and the mice is of later date.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The second name for cat in Sanskrit is vi<em>d</em>âla or bi<em>d</em>âla. In the Vâ<em>g</em>asaneyi-sa<em>m</em>hitâ (XXIV. 31) vrisha<em>d</em>a<em>m</em>sa is explained by vi<em>d</em>âla, and kept quite distinct from nakula (XXIV. 32), which occurs in the Atharva-veda (IV. 139, 5) as an animal hostile to serpents. Manu also (XI. 159) clearly distinguishes vi<em>d</em>âla from nakula, and his vana-vi<em>d</em>âla is most likely meant for the wild-cat. Pâ<em>n</em>ini must have known the word, for in VI. 2, 72 he gives a rule for the accent of the compound bhikshâ-vi<em>d</em>âla. Professor Bühler has called my attention to a vârtika, mentioned by Patañ<em>g</em>ali (Mahâbh. III. 2, 84, 4; ed. Kielhorn, ii. p. 555), where the vi<em>d</em>âla is mentioned as akhu-han, mouse-killer.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is difficult to analyse this word. I thought at first that it might be connected with vidala (bidala, in the Ait. Âr. III. 1, 2, 6) which means cut in half, split in the middle, which would be a very appropriate term for a cat’s eye. But this would leave the lingual <em>d</em> unaccounted for. In the U<em>n</em>âdi-sûtras (I. 117) it is derived from vi<em>d</em>, to shout, with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-289" data-page="289" data-folio="267" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n288_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 267">[p. 267]</button> the suffix âla. This suffix shows a certain analogy with âlîya in mârg<em>âlî</em>ya, another name for cat.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The question then arises, whether from vid<em>â</em>la a derivative vaid<em>â</em>lya might have been formed, and whether this word could have given rise to the Greek αἴλουρος or αἰέλουρος, instead of αἰόλος and οὐρά, as commonly supposed. We should have to admit a parallel form vaid<em>â</em>rya, and then a transition of ορρος into ουρος, allowing at the same time the possibility that the word came into Greek, not as a common Aryan word, but as a foreign name for a foreign animal.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">And this suggests a new question. Vaid<em>û</em>rya and vaid<em>û</em>rya, the very form that would best correspond to the Greek αἴλουρος, means in Sanskrit the cat’s eye. The cat is called manivaid<em>û</em>r-yalokana, i. e. having eyes like the Vaid<em>û</em>rya jewel. It is true that so ancient a grammarian as Pânini (IV. 3, 84) derives vaid<em>û</em>rya from vidûra, ‘very distant,’ and that accordingly it is often spelt with a dental d. But this seems an after-thought. The transition of vaid<em>â</em>rya into vaid<em>û</em>rya is not impossible, even in Sanskrit, if we remember such parallel forms as dûra and davîyas, sthûla, sthavîyas, &amp;c. If then vaid<em>û</em>rya was connected with vid<em>â</em>la, cat, and meant originally a cat’s eye, it is strange, to say no more, that the Prakrit form veluriya should, as Pott pointed out, appear in Greek as βήρυλλος, again a foreign name for a foreign jewel, i. e. for the beryl. It is true no doubt that, scientifically speaking, the cat’s eye and the beryl differ, but in some cases, as Professor Fischer informs me, the colour of the beryl is like that of the eyes of a cat, though it never has that peculiar waving lustre which is perceived in all real cat’s-eye minerals, when they have been cut convexly<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-018" href="#sec-12-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Vaid<em>û</em>rya is also used as the name of the country or the mountain where the vaid<em>û</em>rya mineral is found<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-019" href="#sec-12-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>. At the time <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-290" data-page="290" data-folio="268" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n289_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 268">[p. 268]</button> of Varâhamihira (Brihat-sa<em>m</em>hitâ XIV. 14), in the sixth century, the mines of beryl stone were said to be in the south of India.  But in the commentary on the U<em>n</em>âdi-sûtras (II. 20) we hear of Vidûra as the name of Bâlavâya, being either a mountain or a town, from whence the best Vai<em>d</em>ûrya stones are said to come.  In the commentary on Pâ<em>n</em>ini also (VI. 2, 77) this Bâlavâya is mentioned as the name of a mountain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It was objected by Kâtyâyana that Pâ<em>n</em>ini’s rule (Pâ<em>n</em>. IV. 3, 84), according to which vai<em>d</em>ûrya is formed from Vidûra, must be wrong, because the Vai<em>d</em>ûrya jewel does not come from Vidûra, but from Bâlavâya, and is only cut or polished at Vidûra.  We are not concerned here with the manner in which Pata<em>ñ</em>gali tries to solve this dilemma, but with the dilemma itself, that is, with the fact that in Kâtyâyana’s, or, at all events, in Pata<em>ñ</em>gali’s time Vai<em>d</em>ûrya stones were known to come from the mountain Bâlavâya, not from Vidûra.  We know nothing else about this Bâlavâya mountain, but Burnouf, by a very bold combination, tried many years ago to identify the name of the Bolor or Balur-tâgh<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-020" href="#sec-12-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> with the Vai-<em>d</em>ûrya mountain, the mountain supplying the Vai<em>d</em>ûrya jewels.  This would indicate new points of contact between the East and the West, which however it seems premature to follow up.  Even the coincidences and similarities touched upon in this note are by no means firmly established, and I have only put them together because, if we should come to the conclusion that there is no historical relationship between vi<em>d</em>âla, vai<em>d</em>ûrya, <em>αἴλουρος</em>, <em>βήρυλλος</em>, and Belur-tâgh<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-021" href="#sec-12-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>, we <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-291" data-page="291" data-folio="269" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n290_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 269">[p. 269]</button> should, at all events, have learnt the useful lesson that the chapter of accidents is sometimes larger than we suppose.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A competent critic, reviewing Professor Rolleston’s ‘Scientific Papers and Addresses,’ 2 vols., published after his death by the Clarendon Press, has the following remarks:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Professor Rolleston’s favourite studies were many; but he had a decided preference for what may be termed either archæological zoology or zoological archæology. The opening essays of the second volume, “On the Domestic Cat, of Ancient and Modern Times,” and “On the Cat of the Ancient Greeks,” are interesting in themselves, and well exemplify his mode of treatment, while they abundantly display his immense erudition, both classical and scientific. Their object is to show that the white-breasted or stone marten, <em>Mustela foina</em>, was “functionally” the “cat” of the ancients; and though the two papers occupy only seventeen pages, they bring together a mass of evidence that betokens an astounding width of reading in ancient and in mediæval literature as well as in modern science. It may be doubted, however, whether more is proved than that γαλῆ was some kind of <em>Mustela</em> which was kept as a house-pet. To the numerous quotations from Greek writers might have been added a somewhat Rabelaisian line from the <em>Clouds. Catus</em> and <em>catta</em>, the etymological ancestors of our “cat,” are Low-Latin words, though the latter is found in Martial (XIII. 69)—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">“Pannonicas nobis nunquam dedit Umbria cattas;”</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">denoting, however, not the cat, but some sort of weazel (?). Professor Rolleston does not cite the passage, neither does he tell us that the cat is not mentioned in the Bible, a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-292" data-page="292" data-folio="270" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n291_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 270">[p. 270]</button> singular fact, seeing that it was domesticated in Egypt in very early times. In the apocryphal Book of Baruch, however, the cat is once mentioned. Of <em>catus</em>, the etymology is as uncertain as the origin of puss herself.
