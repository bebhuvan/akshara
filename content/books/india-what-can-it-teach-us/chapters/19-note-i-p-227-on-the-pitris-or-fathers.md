<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-313"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="313" data-folio="291" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n312_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 291">[p. 291]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">NOTE I, p. 227.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">ON THE PIT<em>RI</em>S OR FATHERS.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In Manu the belief in the Pit<em>ri</em>s or Fathers and the rules for their worship have assumed a most complicated character, and there are many passages that might be quoted by those who hold that in India also a belief in the Fathers came first, and a belief in the Devas followed afterwards. There are other arguments too that might be used in support of such a theory, and I wonder they have not been used, though I do not think they can be upheld against the mass of evidence on the other side. The name of the oldest and greatest among the Devas, for instance, is not simply Dyaus, but Dyaush-pitâ, Heaven-Father, and there are several other names of the same character, not only in Sanskrit, but in Greek and Latin also. Does it not look as if Dyaus, the sky, had become personal and worshipful, only after he had been raised to the category of a Pit<em>ri</em>, a father, and that this predicate of Father must have been elaborated first, before it could have been used to comprehend Dyaus, the sky, Varu<em>n</em>a, and other Devas? This sounds plausible, nor do I deny that there may be some truth in it. But it is not the whole truth, and nothing, I believe, is so constant a source of error as this mistaking of some truth for the whole truth. The Vedic poets believed in Devas, gods, if we must so call them, literally, the bright ones; Pit<em>ri</em>s, fathers; and Manushyas, men, mortals<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-001" href="#sec-19-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. Who came first and who came after is difficult to say, but as soon as the three were placed side by side, the Devas certainly <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-314" data-page="314" data-folio="292" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n313_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 292">[p. 292]</button> stood highest, then followed the <em>Pitris</em>, and last came the mortals. Ancient thought did not go so far as to comprehend the three under one common concept, but it paved the way to it. The mortals, after passing through death, became Fathers, and the Fathers became the companions of the Devas. This answered for a time—it was some truth, but not the whole truth.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In Manu there is a decided advance beyond this point. The world, all that moves and rests, we are told (Manu III. 201), has been made by the Devas, but the Devas and Dânavas were born of the <em>Pitris</em>, and the <em>Pitris</em> of the <em>Rishis</em>. The <em>Rishis</em> were originally the poets of the Veda, where their number is given as seven, the Sapta <em>Rishayah</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-002" href="#sec-19-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. How they came to be placed above the Devas, and above the <em>Pitris</em>, is difficult to understand; still so they are, at least at the time of Manu. He gives even their names and genealogy<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-003" href="#sec-19-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="ledger-table-block" id="tbl-001"><div class="table-toolbar"><span class="table-id-badge">TBL-001 · FOLIO 314</span><span class="table-csv-links"><a href="/downloads/india-what-can-it-teach-us/datasets/tables/tbl-001_p0314.csv" download>Diplomatic CSV</a> · <a href="/downloads/india-what-can-it-teach-us/datasets/tidy/tbl-001_p0314.tidy.csv" download>Tidy CSV</a></span></div><div class="ledger-table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Statistical table" tabindex="0"><table class="ledger-table"><thead><tr><th scope="col" colspan="7">Manu Haira<em>ny</em>agarbha</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr class="row-subheader"><td colspan="7">His sons, the seven <em>Rishis</em>.</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>Virâ<em>g</em></td><td>Marî<em>k</em>i</td><td>Atri</td><td>Kavi (Bh<em>ri</em>gu)</td><td>Aṅgiras</td><td>Pulastya</td><td>Vasish<em>th</em>a</td></tr><tr class="row-subheader"><td colspan="7">Their sons, the <em>Pitris</em>.</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>Somasads</td><td>Agnishvâttas</td><td>Barhishads</td><td>Somapas</td><td>Havishmats</td><td>Â<em>g</em>yapas</td><td>Sukalin</td></tr><tr class="row-subheader"><td colspan="7">Their descendants.</td></tr><tr class="row-data"><td>Sâdhyas</td><td>Devas</td><td>Daityas</td><td>Brâhma<em>n</em>as</td><td>Kshatriyas</td><td>Vaisyas</td><td>Sûdras</td></tr></tbody></table></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He then mentions the <em>Pitris</em> who belong exclusively to the Brâhma<em>n</em>as:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Agnidagdhas, Anagnidagdhas, Kâvyas, Barhishads, Agnishvâttas, Saumyas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The first book of Manu tells us of seven Manus (I. 61).
