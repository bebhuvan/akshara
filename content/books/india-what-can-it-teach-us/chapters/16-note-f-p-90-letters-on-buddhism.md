<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-305"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="305" data-folio="283" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n304_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 283">[p. 283]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">NOTE F, p. 90.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">LETTERS ON BUDDHISM.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A Conference on Buddhism was held in June, 1882, at Sion College, to discuss the real or apparent coincidences between the religions of Buddha and Christ. Being unable to assist in person, I addressed the following letters to the Secretary, which were read at the meeting and published afterwards.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">I.</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘I regret that it is quite out of my power to be present at the discussion on Thursday. May I venture, however, to say that a discussion on Buddhism in general seems to me almost an impossibility. The name of Buddhism is applied to religious opinions not only of the most varying, but of a decidedly opposite character held by people on the highest and the lowest stages of civilisation, divided into endless sects, nay, founded on two distinct codes of canonical writings. I hardly know any proposition that could be made with regard to Buddhism in general. <em>Divide et impera !</em> is the only way that can lead to a mutual understanding on the fundamental principles of Buddha’s doctrine, and considering the special qualifications of those who will address your meeting, I should think that an account of what Buddhism is at the present moment in Ceylon, both with the learned and unlearned classes, would be far more interesting and useful than a general discussion on Buddhism. I shall mention the subject to two Buddhist priests who have been reading Sanskrit with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-306" data-page="306" data-folio="284" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n305_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 284">[p. 284]</button> me for several years, but their Buddhism is so different from the Buddhism now practised in Ceylon that they would hardly recognise it as their own religion.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Excuse these hurried remarks, and believe me, ‘Yours faithfully, ‘<span class="sc">F. Max Müller.</span>’</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">II.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘I can have no objection to your reading my letter at your conference, and after receiving your second letter, I feel all the more sorry that I am unable to attend in person, not that I have much faith in <em>public</em> discussions, it being so very difficult to be quite frank and truthful when you are listened to by hundreds of people, and when success and applause seem for the moment more important than the establishment of facts and the recognition of truth. But I admire the fearless spirit in which you invite public discussion on a subject which has become a kind of bugbear to so many people. I fully sympathise with you, and I think I can say of myself that I have all my life worked in the same spirit that speaks from your letter, so much so that if any of your friends could prove to me what they seem to have said to you, namely, “that Christianity was but an inferior copy of a greater original,” I should bow and accept the greater original. That there are startling coincidences between Buddhism and Christianity cannot be denied, and it must likewise be admitted that Buddhism existed at least 400 years before Christianity. I go even further, and should feel extremely grateful if anybody would point out to me the historical channels through which Buddhism had influenced early Christianity. I have been looking for such channels all my life, but hitherto I have found none. What I have found is that for some of the most startling coincidences there are historical antecedents on both sides, and if we once know those antecedents, the coincidences become far less startling. If I do find in certain Buddhist <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-307" data-page="307" data-folio="285" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n306_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 285">[p. 285]</button> works doctrines identically the same as in Christianity, so far from being frightened, I feel delighted, for surely truth is not the less true because it is believed by the majority of the human race<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-16-fn-001" href="#sec-16-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-16-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘I believe we have made some progress during the last thirty years. I still remember the time when all heathen religions were looked upon as the work of the Devil. We know now that they are stages in a growth, and in a growth not determined by an accidental environment only, but by an original purpose, a purpose to be realised in the history of the human race as a whole. Even missionaries have begun to approach the heathen in a new and better spirit. They look for what may safely be preserved in the religion of their pupils, and on that common ground they try to erect a purer faith and a better worship, instead of attempting to destroy the sacred foundations of religion which, I believe, exist, or at least existed, in every human heart. See on this subject the wise remarks of the Bishop of Lahore (French), as quoted in the <em>Liberal</em>, July 23, 1882.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘I send you a report which I have just issued on The Sacred Books of the East, translated by various Oriental scholars, and edited by myself. My object in publishing these translations is exactly the same as yours, namely, to give to those who are interested in the history of religion, facts, instead of theories.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘I had spent nearly the whole of my life in publishing the text and commentary of <em>one</em> of the Sacred Books of the East, the Veda, or more correctly the Rig-veda, the most ancient monument of Eastern religion, the root of all the later religious growth of India, in a certain sense, the key also to Buddhism, inasmuch as that religion starts with a denial of the sacred authority of the Veda. The publication of that work has produced a complete revolution, not only in our own <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-308" data-page="308" data-folio="286" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/indiawhatcanitte00mluoft/page/n307_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 286">[p. 286]</button> views of the origin and growth of ancient religion, but in the religious life of the Hindus themselves, and this not so much on the surface as in its deepest foundations.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘When I saw how little there was left to me of active life, I invited the co-operation of my friends and colleagues to make, at all events, a beginning in the publication of trustworthy translations of all the more important among the Sacred Books of the East. From the enclosed report you will see that Buddhism in its various phases has received its full share of attention, and that some of its canonical books may now be studied by those who do not read Sanskrit, Pâli, or Chinese.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Yours very faithfully, ‘F. <span class="sc">Max Müller.</span>’</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-16-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-16-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">See now ‘Similarities between Christianity and Buddhism,’ in Physical Religion, Appendix XV. p. 390.</span></li></ol></section>
