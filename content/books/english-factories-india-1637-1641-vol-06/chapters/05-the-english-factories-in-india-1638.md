Sailed [from Swally?] on December 10, and three days later anchored at Chaul, where they delivered the present sent to the Captain, and obtained from him a letter of recommendation to the Portuguese factor at Dābhol. He also gave them a letter to the Viceroy, ‘expressing thanckfullnesse to Your Worships for giving passadge to the Portugall captives.’ Reached Dābhol December 15 at night, and landed next morning. Found that Pitt was ‘above in the cuntry’,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-002" href="#sec-05-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> but expected to be at Rājāpur within ten days with 500 bags of pepper for the <em>Francis</em> to embark; Downham had in addition 240 bags at Dābhol. It was difficult to decide how best to dispose of the pinnace <em>Eagle</em> on her arrival at Goa; so Downham was asked to write to Pitt at ‘Rabagg’, desiring him to send them instructions at Goa. Quitting Dābhol on December 17, they soon after met the <em>Christopher</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-003" href="#sec-05-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> and the ‘taurime’ [<em>see</em> p. 42], and learned that the <em>Francis</em> had left Rājāpur for Surat, and that these two vessels were bound for Dābhol to take in pepper, &amp;c., for the same place. Have therefore decided to send the <em>Eagle</em> from Goa to Rājāpur to embark any goods Pitt may bring thither, unless the latter orders otherwise, or the Viceroy agrees to lade her for Surat on account of the 67,865 xerafins [<em>see previous volume</em>, p. 160], ‘of which wee much doubt.’ On arriving off Goa on December 19 they found twelve Dutch men-of-war at anchor there.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-004" href="#sec-05-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Mr. Carter and the purser went on board, and delivered letters from Methwold and from the Dutch chief at Surat; ‘and so soone as they had read them, they sent for the butt of beere.’ Then the <em>Eagle</em> stood in for Goa harbour, where they found two carracks and six galleons, the latter being ready to put to sea and encounter the Hollanders. The same night <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-89" data-page="89" data-folio="35" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n88_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 35">[p. 35]</button> a number of frigates from Cochin brought a strong reinforcement of soldiers for the same purpose, besides a lading of silk, cinnamon, &amp;c. Hearing that the Viceroy was 'theire belowe in the castle' and the Vedor 'above in the citty of Goa', they landed and delivered their letters to the former, who gave them leave to go up to the city and unlade the pinnace. They accordingly proceeded thither; and on the day following their arrival they visited the Vedor. He, however, was obliged to go down to 'Bardas' [Bardes] to see the Viceroy and was absent for ten days, thus preventing the unlading of the <em>Eagle</em>. Meanwhile they hired a house in a convenient position. Weddell has settled a factory at Bhatkal, leaving there fifteen persons, of whom only seven survive. 'What is become of theare shipps wee cannot heare certaynely. Capt. Weddall dyed at Achyne<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-005" href="#sec-05-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and his fleete (as the Portugalls report) is gone for the coast of Chyna.' The wheat and the three bales of goods have been landed from the <em>Eagle</em> and are now in the customhouse. 'The 25th, beeing our Christmas Day, early in the morning the Portugalls went oute to the Hollanders only with theare six galleoons against theare twelve shippes.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-006" href="#sec-05-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The fight continued aboute seven howres, and was performed on both sydes with as much vallour as could bee expected. At last it so fortuned that the smallest of the Portugalls galleoons fyred, the which caused the firing of the vice-amerall of the Hollanders, with another of theare greatest shippes; besydes which the Portugalls suncke one of the smallest of the Hollanders vessells; soe that the Hollanders lost three shippes for theare one. Besydes, the amerall of the Hollanders was most pittifully toorne by the admirall of the Portugalls. But the Portugalls lost all theire men in their galleoon but 30, and those the Hollanders tooke upp; the Hollanders lost but very fewe men. The Generall of the Portugalls did confesse at his comming in that, had not two of the Hollanders (the vice-amerall and another) fyred, hee did beleeve they would scarce have lett him come in againe that night; or if hee did, it would have bine with much losse. The Vizroy, the Generall, and Veedor de <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-90" data-page="90" data-folio="36" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n89_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 36">[p. 36]</button> Fazenda, with the rest of the Councell, hath bine below at Bardas sitting in councell till the 30th of December concerning their goeing forth to the Hollanders to fight againe; but for what wee can heare they intend it not; in respect they gott the victory by this fight, they resolve to keepe it this yeare.' On December 31 the Vedor returned to Goa; whereupon they inquired of him what the Viceroy had decided concerning the Company's money deposited in his hands. He replied that the President had written to the Viceroy about this, and he himself had spoken to the latter on the subject, but had been unable to obtain a reply. ‘Our oppinion is (by what wee heare) hee dares not bee seene to speake in our behalfe; or, yf hee dares, hee will not.' Then they asked whether the Viceroy would lade the <em>Eagle</em> with pepper ‘uppon the afforesaid money imbarged in his hands’; to which reply was made that they had not pepper enough for their own turns. Their next inquiry was whether a vessel should be sent from Surat to the Malabar coast to fetch pepper on the same account; but he answered that this would be useless, as the Portuguese had not had enough pepper from those settlements these two years to lade their own carracks. Being questioned whether the Viceroy intended to pay the debt or not, he said they had better go down and ask him themselves. Have heard nothing of the lawsuit, but will inquire; in any case they will abstain from meddling therein without orders. Wheat sold to the wife of the Captain of Damān. Have now (January 1) interviewed the Viceroy, who gave the same replies as the Vedor about the pepper; with regard to the debt, he said that he could do nothing, because the money was dispersed in the hands of several men and must be recovered ‘by course of lawe’. He promised to write to the President by the <em>Eagle</em>. About five weeks ago a letter arrived from Bhatkal [<em>see</em> p. 33], inquiring for news from England. Nothing has been heard from Pitt at Rājāpur or from Downham at Dābhol. Intend to lade the <em>Eagle</em> with a certain amount of freight for Surat, and then dispatch her to Rājāpur to fill up with pepper. Enclose a list of prices at Goa. Have just heard that the result of the lawsuit was that ‘there was a judgement past in Court of Justice against the Bannians that they had noe right nor interest in the saide debt, but did belong absolutely to the proprietors the English’. Will procure a copy of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-91" data-page="91" data-folio="37" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n90_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 37">[p. 37]</button> this decree and send it by the <em>Eagle</em>. Should the <em>Mary</em> put in here on her way from Masulipatam, they will not fail to solicit the Viceroy and the Vedor for the discharge of the debt. Will hasten the sale of their wheat, as a consignment is expected from up country. Vernworthy, one of Courteen's factors, is dead [<em>see</em> p. 6]. Hope to dispatch the <em>Eagle</em> within four days. (<em>Copy.</em> 4½ <em>pp. Received January</em> 25.)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">William Bayley's Account of the Voyage of the</span> <em>Mary</em> <span class="sc">from Armagon to Surat</span> (<em>Marine Records</em>, vol. lxii. p. 74).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1638, <em>January</em> 3. Sailed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-007" href="#sec-05-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> <em>January</em> 14. Sighted Ceylon. <em>January</em> 25. Robert Aldridge punished for insubordination.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-008" href="#sec-05-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> <em>January</em> 30. Saw the coast near Calicut. <em>February</em> 3. Passed Mangalore. <em>February</em> 13. Saw the Dutch fleet blockading Goa. <em>February</em> 19. Anchored at Dābhol. <em>February</em> 25. Sailed. <em>March</em> 1. Passed Bombay. <em>March</em> 5. Anchored in Swally Hole, where the <em>Blessing</em> and five Dutch ships were riding. (11 <em>pp. Damaged.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">William Fremlen, John Willoughby, and Henry Chapman at 'Bunder Abassee' [Gombroon] to [President Methwold and Council at Surat], January</span> 10, 1638 (<em>O.C.</em> 1613).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Fremlen reached this place on December 31, and found in the road two Dutch ships and two Surat junks. . . . Broadcloth sent to Surat in the <em>Swan</em>. . . . Also fruit, rosewater, &amp;c. . . . Disposal of the cinnamon belonging to the Vedor. . . . Rūnās and freight goods put aboard the ship. The enclosed list of prices will show how plentiful provisions are. The false abbāsīs brought in the <em>Swan</em> have been exchanged for good ones. The Dutch on their way attacked a Portuguese galliot, but their boats were beaten off and the skipper of the <em>Maestricht</em> was killed. 'Signor Speering coming afterwards into this roade tooke upon him the command of the shipps, but Signor Nicholas<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-009" href="#sec-05-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> beat better manneres into him; so that now he submits and Nicholas is commander.' Two more <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-92" data-page="92" data-folio="38" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n91_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 38">[p. 38]</button> Dutch ships expected from Surat. Price paid by the Hollanders for their silk. ‘Yesterday a collaatt [Hind. <em>khilat</em>, a dress of honour] or vest arrived hether from the King for Nicholas’; its reception described. ‘Theire expence of all sorts are infinite, and indeed (as sometime our people have writ of them) beseeming rather theire prince then merchants, or rather servants of merchants.’ Arrival of the rest of the Dutch silk. ‘Never in one yeare was so much silke bought by them, halfe so much dreamt of by us, nor ever any so extreamely dearely paid for.’ Six horses sent in a native junk, with two ‘murderers’ and six Englishmen to defend her against the Malabars. Money and pearls forwarded by the <em>Swan</em>. (<em>Copy.</em> 6½ <em>pp. Received February</em> 14. <em>The omitted portions relate solely to Persian affairs.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-010" href="#sec-05-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">William Fremlen at Gombroon to the Company, January</span> 13, 1638 (<em>O.C.</em> 1614).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Events in India since the dispatch of the <em>William</em>. John Drake slain near Dholpur. The <em>Francis</em> reached Surat from Persia on April 16, 1637, bringing 1,000 tūmāns, three horses, and some freight goods. Ten days later the <em>Mary</em>, also from Gombroon, anchored at the river’s mouth, laden with passengers and freight goods, besides three horses, 373 bales of silk, and 1,000 tūmāns in abbāsīs on account of the Company. The passengers and 276 bales of silk were landed; the rest had to be left on board, owing to bad weather. ‘The second of May, time of shipping being past, your President, William Methwold, myselfe, with divers others your servants, departed Surrat, intended for Ahmudabad, accompan[y]ing your caphila, consisting of all that years quicksilver, elephants teeth, and 110,000 rupees, resolved to settle and visit the factories of Buroach and Burrowda; of whose Governour accompt and satisfaction for your lost goods and servants lives [<em>see</em> p. 2] was required, yet little received other then promises of restauration of the former and revenge of the latter in kinde. Thence wee proceeded to Ahmudabad, and remayned there till the 28th August, and arived againe to Surratt the 8th September. But not long after our coming to Ahmudabad newes allso came of the <em>Shaheis</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-93" data-page="93" data-folio="39" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n92_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 39">[p. 39]</button> losse upon Danda Rajapore, a port of the Decanees; who being late dispeeded from Surratt for Mocha, yet favoured some few daies with faire winds, was (when shee had well nigh atteyned her port) incountred with so violent a storme that, being forced to beare up before it, fell with and on the forementioned place and was there wrackt; the people in generall saved, but little or no goods; so that 1,100,000 rupees are computated and reported to bee lost with her.’ On May 10 [1637] the <em>Mary</em>, after riding out such bad weather that the boats could not get near her with provisions, set sail again; but, the monsoon continuing violent, she was forced to winter at ‘Bombaien’. Letters were received from Masulipatam and Bantam on June 29, announcing the death of President Willoughby, the dispatch of the <em>London</em> and <em>Jewel</em> from Bantam to England with cargoes worth 180,000 rials of eight, the total wreck of the Dutch <em>Prins Willem</em>, and the narrow escape of the <em>Zutphen</em>; and one on July 17 from Abraham Aldington, who had been left by the <em>Blessing</em> at Mozambique and had thence proceeded in a Portuguese vessel to Goa. He wrote that the <em>Blessing</em> reached Mohilla on December 21, 1636, and, learning that Cobb was at Johanna, endeavoured next day to make that island, but was retarded by contrary winds and currents. Cobb, on sighting the <em>Blessing</em>, put to sea in a hurry and came within a league, ‘yet allwaies kept the winde of her,’ and at last sailed away. The <em>Blessing</em>, finding it impossible to overtake him, proceeded to Mozambique to refit. She arrived there on January 9 [1637] and stayed till March 1, in which time no less than fourteen of her crew died, leaving only about fifty sound men aboard. The Portuguese would not permit them to buy any slaves, but at Mohilla (which was reached on April 7) they procured eighteen from a Portuguese skipper in exchange for three of Cobb’s guns which the <em>Blessing</em> had recovered. They next went to Johanna, whence they sailed on May 6 and reached Masulipatam on June 12. A junk which arrived at Cambay from Mokha on August 30 brought intelligence of good markets there; also that the <em>Swan</em> and <em>Michael</em> were safe at Aden, but could get no further. On September 3 letters arrived from John Wylde and his companions at Aden, relating that they had been surprised by the storm in which the <em>Shāhī</em> was lost, but had managed to reach Aden on June 2; they were at first kindly used <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-94" data-page="94" data-folio="40" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n93_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 40">[p. 40]</button> and invited on shore, but were then detained till all their goods were landed. They sold these (except the gum-lac and indigo) at a profit of from 60 to 100 per cent., and got back to Swally on September 27. On the 9th of that month the <em>Francis</em> returned from Bombay, whither she had been sent on August 25 with provisions for the <em>Mary</em>; she brought the rest of that vessel's silk. The <em>Mary</em> departed on September 12 for Dābhol, to land freight goods and passengers and then to proceed to Masulipatam to fetch 400 bales of piece-goods, &amp;c., for transmission to England. On October 5 the pinnace <em>Eagle</em> arrived at Swally, having on her way touched only at Johanna, where she waited for the <em>Jonas</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-011" href="#sec-05-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> from August 22 to September 4. She brought word that the <em>Supply</em>, in doubling the Cape, had crossed a small vessel supposed to be the <em>Roebuck</em> making for England, and had also met the <em>Planter</em> at St. Augustine's Bay. News came on October 8 that a carrack<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-012" href="#sec-05-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> which had sailed from Lisbon in company with another carrack and a 'pataxo' [Port. <em>patacho</em>, a pinnace] reached Goa on September 19. Letters received from Masulipatam on October 15 announced the arrival of four Dutch ships, two of which were to go into the Bay to surprise Portuguese vessels, or at least to hinder them from visiting Hūglī and Pipplī, 'ports frequented by them on that coast': that the Dutch had made peace with Macassar: that eight great ships had reached Batavia from Holland, and four more were expected: that the commander of the fleet which had besieged Goa the previous year 'was publiquely disgraced for performing no better against the Portugalls': and that orders had been received that no ships were to be sent to Holland except from Batavia. The Agent at Masulipatam also wrote that the <em>Blessing</em> would be dispatched to Surat within five days, carrying calicoes, saltpetre, sappan wood, and some freight goods for transmission to Persia: that the pinnace <em>Advice</em> arrived [from England] on August 8, and the <em>Coaster</em> from Bantam on the [17]th: that they had in hand 400 bales of calico, which would be sent to Surat by the <em>Mary</em>, if she came, and if not, by the <em>Thomas</em>: that President Coulson had died at Bantam in August: that John White, George Peirson, and Edward Falconer <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-95" data-page="95" data-folio="41" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n94_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 41">[p. 41]</button> were dead [at Masulipatam]: and that ‘Jacob Cottanceaw’, chief mate, had taken charge of the <em>Blessing</em>. On October 18 the <em>Jonas</em> arrived at Swally under Captain Pynn, with her crew in good health. Her master, John Shilling, had died a few days before, and by his special request the body was buried on the shore at Swally; he contracted his illness by lying ashore at Mohilla. The Persian, ‘Allee Ballee<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-013" href="#sec-05-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>’, buried one of his company on the voyage, but he himself arrived in good health. A vessel belonging to the Governor of Surat brought news of the death of Agent Gibson, leaving affairs in Persia in a distracted state. A consultation was held at Surat, in which Fremlen was elected to succeed as President when Methwold should depart; this post Fremlen accepted, though he was desirous of returning home, and had received but ‘meane incouragements’ from the Company. Thereupon he was deputed to Persia in the <em>Swan</em> to settle affairs in that country. On November 14 [Willem Jacobszoon] Koster arrived at Swally with two Dutch ships and three ‘pramms<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-014" href="#sec-05-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>’, having seventeen days before left eleven vessels in Goa Road, where they are to remain all the monsoon, to prevent the sailing of the carracks, and, if possible, to make an attack on the city in conjunction with the King of Bījāpur. This, however, is improbable, for the King is not likely to leave his dominions while the Mogul's son with a powerful army remains at Daulatābād. The Dutch landed 28 chests of rials and four of gold, besides spices, chinaware, and other goods. On November 19 they dispeeded their vice-admiral and one 'pramm'<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-015" href="#sec-05-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> for Persia, and five days later the <em>Swan</em> sailed for the same destination, carrying rice, tobacco, and sundry other commodities, besides freight goods bringing in 11,424 mahmūdīs. At Damān she <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-96" data-page="96" data-folio="42" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n95_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 42">[p. 42]</button> met the <em>Blessing</em>, which had left Masulipatam on September 27. The President intended to repair her on arrival and then dispatch her [to England]. The <em>Francis</em> was sent to Damān to transport to Goa the family of the Captain. It is hoped that she will bring back the money there detained at the suit of the Diu merchants. With her sailed a ‘towryn<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-016" href="#sec-05-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> or ship of this country built, bought this last yeare, of burthen 80 tonns’, laden with rūnās and freight goods for Rājāpur, whence she is to bring pepper for the <em>Blessing</em>. The <em>Francis</em> is next to be sent to Persia with freight goods, and on her return voyage to call at Lārībandar to bring away the factors and their goods. When the <em>Swan</em> left Swally, John Spiller and John Stamford were about to sail for Lārībandar in the <em>Michael</em>, carrying 10,000 rupees and two chests of rials, to pay for what the broker had already bought and to commence the new investment. From that port the <em>Michael</em> is to proceed to Persia with butter and oil. ‘Being thither come, shee againe returnes for Scinda to lade freight goods provided for Bussora by the Mogols embassador to the Great Turke, who allso accompanieth them, not daring to goe by land for feare of the Persian. This imployment proceeds from Asaph Ckauns intimation and intreaty. Somewhat of each severall sort of vendible comodities at Bacora are enordered to bee provided in (and laden from) Tuttha, that so a seasonable and hoped for proffitable trade might by this meanes bee discovered.’ After the <em>Blessing</em> is prepared [for England], the <em>Jonas</em> is to be sent to Gombroon, carrying ‘Allee Ballee’ and freight goods. She will remain there until Fremlen is ready to return in her to Surat, which will be about the end of February. The <em>Swan</em> was much hindered by contrary winds, and did not reach Gombroon until December 31. . . . The ill-success of trade between this place and the Coromandel coast is entirely the fault of the factors, for Persian merchants, who pay in freight and customs at least 15 per cent. more, ‘thrive and throng yearly twixt Gombroone and Messulapatam.’ The trade with Sind ought to be equally profitable; while, as for European commodities, the industrious Hollanders yearly land and sell great quantities. . . . The <em>Swan</em> sailed for Surat on January 10, accompanied by a junk of that place. . . . Two Dutch ships reached <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-97" data-page="97" data-folio="43" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n96_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 43">[p. 43]</button> Gombroon on December 7. Their boats attacked a Portuguese galliot near 'Goadell' [Gwādar], but were repulsed with loss. . . . (7½ <em>pp. The omitted portions relate to Persia only. Received overland, July 19, 1638.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Thomas Clark and Richard Hudson at Masulipatam to the President and Council at Surat, January</span> 24, 1638 (<em>Factory Records, Masulipatam</em>, vol. v. p. 37).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Are sensible that the Company disapprove the dispatch of ships from this port to Persia, but fear that, if they do not fulfil their promises in that respect, the immunities they now enjoy will be withdrawn. Beg that the <em>Jonas</em> may be sent hither, and hope by her to give better tidings of success in these parts. Of the horses that came in the <em>Mary</em>, the best died on the way; the other two will be sent to the court. Complain of the unfriendliness of the Portuguese. The <em>Mary</em> left Armagon on the 3rd current; refer to the enclosed invoices for particulars of her lading. If Khairāt Khān offers to return the jewel and the greyhound, Rogers should not hesitate to receive them. (<em>Copy.</em> 1½ <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Thomas Clark and Richard Hudson at Masulipatam to Gilbert Gardner [at Narsapur], March</span> 10, 1638 (<em>Ibid.</em>, p. 15).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Will supply him with iron. Any of Malik Mahmūd's trees may be used for a bowsprit, if necessary; but he should remember the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-110" data-page="110" data-folio="56" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n109_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 56">[p. 56]</button> old proverb, ‘ not to ride a free horse to death,’ and they think he should be content to fish his present bowsprit. A deck should be built on the <em>Darling</em>. Couch’s boat is to be returned, if not wanted there. (<em>Copy.</em> ½ <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">Thomas Clark</span> at <span class="sc">Masulipatam to Thomas Grove</span> [at <span class="sc">Petapoli</span>], <span class="sc">March</span> 12, 1638 (<em>Factory Records, Masulipatam</em>, vol. v. p. 33).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Hopes to see him shortly and therefore a brief reply will suffice. As regards the merchants who have made the offers he reports, he is to ‘ feede them awhiles with dellassa and reape<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-033" href="#sec-05-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>’, and accept their goods, but make no terms with them for the present. (<em>Copy.</em> ½ <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">The Company to the President and Council at Surat, March</span> 16, 1638 (<em>Letter Books</em>, vol. i. p. 142).</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Have received their letter of November 21, 1636, brought by the Dutch <em>Emilia</em>, and that of March 31, 1637,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-034" href="#sec-05-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> by the <em>William</em>, which arrived on December 10. The <em>London</em> and <em>Jewel</em> have also got home from Bantam. The latter sailed on January 24, 1637, and when departing met the <em>Crispiana</em> entering theroad from Masulipatam with a cargo valued at 35,224 rials of eight. Of the goods received in the <em>William</em>, the Persia silk was both deficient in weight and coarse in quality; the factors should be informed accordingly. Many of the China stuffs were found to be ‘ rotten and damnified ’; and, as they were not advised as defective goods, some trickery is suspected. The white twisted China silk produced little profit, and no more is desired. ‘ Callicoes in generall (howsoever by severall names distinguished) were in tymes paste a maine support of that India trade, and were here in good use and well requested and bin sold to som proffitt while their makeing was answerable to their first cost; but, since they have declyned in goodnes from their former manufacture and increased so much in their prizes, they are nowe become here att a stand in their use, and other countrie cloathing, beeing better made and cheaper, succeeds in callicoes roome.’ Those bought at Surat were well made; and, though the prices were high, a greater quantity would have been welcome; cannot <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-111" data-page="111" data-folio="57" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n110_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 57">[p. 57]</button> understand why so small a proportion of the investment was made there. Complain of the 'multiplicity of sorts' sent home, which 'makes the commodity like a pedlers packe, not fitt for a merchants forren exporte but for a towne retayle trade'; in future the buyers should confine themselves to fewer sorts, and those of the cheaper rates, not exceeding 100 mahmūdīs the corge, charges included. 'All sorts of callicoes are of late very much disesteemed here; and would be more, were they not principally used in this place for dyeing into colloures.' Directions for the better packing of piece-goods. The calico bought at Agra was better, and on the whole cheaper, than that obtained in Gujarāt. Of the three sorts the 'Merculees or broad baftes' were the best made but were not well 'bleaked' [i. e. bleached]; of the other two the 'Derebauds' were preferred to the 'Kerebauds' [<em>see the previous volume</em>, p. 146], which are rather too broad. The calicoes from Baroda were very poor, and were evidently bought by unskilled persons; while their packing was exceedingly bad. The Guinea stuffs have been sold to the Guinea Company, though not to great profit; the same quantity as ordered by the <em>Jonas</em> may be sent. The Sinda factorie, for ought wee yet perceive, hath most cause to be cherished and persued then anie of your other callico factories, for the goods received from thence this shipping are the flower of the whole parcell and are preferred here before all others for their makeing and prizes. . . . Wee shall therefore desire that you doe not neglect that place (if a factorie may there bee permitted to bee established), but see the same stocked sufficiently for the tymely procureing of callicoes and indico.' Of the former, the 'joorees [<em>see the previous volume</em>, p. 133] Nursapoore' are preferred. The sample bale of Sind indigo sent by the <em>Discovery</em> could not be found. The calicoes from Ahmadābād were exceedingly dear. Those from Broach, though high in price, were good and well conditioned; would be glad of a larger supply from that factory. Any cotton yarn sent home should be of good quality, as it will then sell readily; and besides both coarse and fine pay the same customs duties. Malabar pepper is in demand here; and it would be well to send some vessels along the coast to collect a quantity, following the example of Weddell, who has laden home the <em>Planter</em> with 150,000 lb. of pepper, 17,453 lb. of benzoin, 7,349 lb. of cinnamon, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-112" data-page="112" data-folio="58" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n111_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 58">[p. 58]</button> and 3,166 lb. of myrrh, besides gum-lac, cassia fistula, and some samples of calicoes. He has also made a contract with the King of ‘Balligatte’ [<em>see note on p.</em> 5] for pepper, and has settled a factory at Bhatkal. The <em>Planter</em> is preparing for a new voyage thither with a cargo of lead, coral, &amp;c. It is said, however, that Mr. Courteen disapproves of the new factory and will order its dissolution. Deficiency in the weight of the pepper. Saltpetre is expensive to buy and troublesome to bring home, as it infects and spoils other goods; therefore preference should be given to sugar (white and dry), preserved ginger, cinnamon, &amp;c. Two bales of the flat indigo were found to contain nothing but black earth; and it is feared that more will be discovered when the rest of the bales are examined; Benjamin Robinson and the other buyers must give satisfaction for their negligence. A good supply of this sort of indigo should be sent home, with a third of the like quantity in Biāna indigo, as the dyers find that a pound of the latter sort is as good as a pound and a half of the former. The gum-lac was poor and not in accordance with the instructions sent, being nothing but lumps and clods. Double the quantity would vend if the quality were good. Fifty-eight pieces of calico are wanting in the bales already opened. The missing bale of Tatta indigo was invoiced at 70 rupees the maund; but some of the officers of the <em>Discovery</em> declare that they brought from thence a consignment for the Surat factors which had cost only 43 rupees the maund, and so it is suspected that this was afterwards put upon the Company at the higher rate. A bale of calico samples is also missing. The news received from Persia shows that the Company has been much wronged by the late Agent Gibson, especially in lending money to the Dutch. Hope that a trustworthy man has been dispatched from Surat to take up that post and investigate past transactions. Now reply to the letter of November 21, 1636. Note the arrival of the <em>Mary</em>, and the fruitless search of the <em>Blessing</em> for Cobb, who arrived near Falmouth at the beginning of May last. Have complained of the damage he has caused, but see little prospect of satisfaction. Had the <em>Swan</em> carried the pirates along with her to India, much trouble might have been saved. Grieved to hear of the sufferings of the factors at Surat, but can suggest no remedy but patience. Approve their care and industry in the disposal of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-113" data-page="113" data-folio="59" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n112_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 59">[p. 59]</button> the ships. Censure the Agent at Masulipatam for taking so much money out of the <em>Crispiana</em>. Agent Joyce and his successor promised plentiful returns from that coast for England; but the goods hitherto received (apart from some lost in the <em>Palsgrave</em>) cannot amount to much above 9,000<em>l.</em>, although (besides the supplies from Surat and Bantam) the factors have had nearly 100,000<em>l.</em> direct from England in the five or six preceding years. They are still craving for more, alleging that forty or fifty thousand pounds may yearly be invested there for England; but no more money will be sent till some return is made. The Masulipatam factors might have afforded some help to those at Surat, since there will be no need for them to make so large an investment as usual to provide cloves at Macassar, 'for that commodity is here so debased by the Hollanders that they are nowe worth scarce 4<em>s.</em> the pound monie, and therefore that trade is not worth the following.' Note that, although it was intended that the <em>Swan</em> should return from Surat to Masulipatam and there lade for England, the President and Council sent her instead to Mokha, to seek trade there and protect the Indian junks against pirates. Have never yet found trade in the Red Sea worth the employment of a ship to those parts; and can see no reason why the English should defend native shipping without reward. If there be any rovers in those seas, they are probably 'the French pirates whoe have followed the stepps of Regemont<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-035" href="#sec-05-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, the man of warr of Deipe'; and surely the English would not be held responsible for their depredations. One thing is certain—that the diversion of this ship has endangered the goods for England being left behind at the Coast. 'You cannot be ignorant of our desires and howe often wee have written you that all freighting viages and other desig[n]ments of our shipps to anie newe trades or by-imployment should cease and that all should give place to the seasonable and tymely makeing home of our retournes for Europe. This hath bin allwaies our requiryes, and wee doe still remayne of the same minds and commend the same unto your performaunces.' As it is evident that there is some ill feeling between the Presidencies of Surat and Bantam, they suspect that the former has thus disposed of the <em>Swan</em> to spite the latter; but, if so, the loss will fall upon the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-114" data-page="114" data-folio="60" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n113_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 60">[p. 60]</button> Company. Trust that the Surat factors have been able to send home the <em>Blessing</em> at the beginning of last December and that the <em>Mary</em> will follow her speedily. Note the want of money at Surat ; but it seems strange that the factors should send so much gold to Goa to be changed and should leave it there so long ; the result is seen in the action brought there by the merchants of Diu, which may entail the loss of the whole sum. Can see no way of recovering the losses caused by Cobb's piracies and by Weddell's proceedings on the Malabar coast. Cannot learn anything of the latter's subsequent movements, but no doubt they are known at Surat. Condemn the ingratitude of Weddell and his associates, and approve the President's attitude towards them. Nevertheless, should Weddell and the rest have more cash than they can use, the factors may accept it and give bills on the Company in exchange, reckoning the rial of eight at 5<em>s.</em> or 5<em>s.</em> 6<em>d.</em> ; and they may also buy their commodities from them at a reasonable rate in exchange for bills on London. Advised in former letters that there had been French pirates in the parts about the Red Sea, one of whom returned to Dieppe last April with booty to the value of 30,000<em>l.</em>;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-036" href="#sec-05-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> now perceive that the factors were informed by the Dutch of such a rover, who had exhibited to them a commission from the King of France.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-037" href="#sec-05-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> ‘You knowe out of your owne experience that the French nation hath nothinge to doe in those seas by waie of trade, and therefore their intentions must be to robb, whatsoever they otherwise make shew of.’ Trust that they have warned the Moors and others of this, for it ‘is neither equity nor conscience’ that the English should be held responsible for any losses caused by pirates of other nations. Detention of the <em>William</em> at Masulipatam ; such delays should be avoided in future. Care must also be taken to guard against the embezzlement of freight goods and consequent claims against the factors ; ‘as those Moores of Coromondell did unto Thomas Rogers, our factour in Gulcundah, they complaining against Thomas Wilbram, late purser of the <em>William</em>, that he should have purloined some of the Moores goods.’ Have examined the master, &amp;c., but can discover nothing of moment. Freighters should be warned that the Company accept no liability. ‘But this <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-115" data-page="115" data-folio="61" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n114_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 61">[p. 61]</button> same freighting trade is an excellent cover and a cleanly conveighance for that which hath bin called heretofore private trade; for under the name of Banians goods anie thing may passe without controle or the least suspect, unto whomsoever it doth apperteyne. For nowe there is noe more newes of private trade; the words and thing are both out of date, for custome hath taken awaie that denomination and made it soe publique that it needeth noe vizard to cover the abuse thereof. Wee heare of it daylie, howesoever the cheife actours will take no notice therof.’ The result is seen in damage to the Company’s goods thus transported from place to place. If this abuse be not remedied, they must take drastic steps at home against those responsible. The ships’ officers cannot be relied upon, for they are the chief agents in this private trading, and naturally shield those who employ them in this way. Complain that the accounts sent home by the <em>William</em> were brought down only to September, 1636; in future they must be completed to near the time of the ship’s departure. A passage was given in that ship to a Portuguese Don named Simaon de Tavor, a kinsman of Father Paulo. He was well treated on board; yet he gave much trouble on the way, threatening to stab the master and ‘giveing evill and disdainefull language’, and since his arrival he has spoken ill of the English nation and has departed for Portugal without a word of thanks for his entertainment. In future such favours should be sparingly granted. The letters from ‘Edwarte Fernando Corren’ [Correa], ‘your Portugall scribe’, have been transmitted to Venice, as the enclosed answer will show; and the parcel of tokens will be sent thither by the first ship. Trust that compensation has been obtained for the goods lost when the caravan was robbed by the Rājpūts, for as the Company has been forced to make good the robberies of the <em>Roebuck</em>, it is only fair that the Indians should in like manner pay for the losses sustained at the hands of their fellow-countrymen. Find from the accounts that at the departure of the <em>William</em> they had at Dābhol 51,340 mahmūdīs, at Goa 176,190 mahmūdīs, and at Surat 264,663 mahmūdīs, making a total of 492,193 mahmūdīs, besides certain goods and the money received for freight. Against this must be set 200,000 mahmūdīs borrowed at Ahmadābād, and 25,000 borrowed at Agra; but the balance should be sufficient to lade home the <em>Blessing</em> and <em>Mary</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-116" data-page="116" data-folio="62" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n115_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 62">[p. 62]</button> without incurring fresh indebtedness. Last year the <em>Jonas</em> and <em>Eagle</em> carried out goods and money to the value of 30,719<em>l.</em> 6<em>s.</em> 8<em>d.</em> Have now sent them in the <em>Discovery</em> 20,000<em>l.</em> in rials, and a cargo of broadcloth, lead, quicksilver, &amp;c., to the value of 1,847<em>l.</em> 18<em>s.</em> 10<em>d.</em>; besides certain goods for Francisco de Castro and Father Paulo. This is the only supply to be expected this year; for until they find greater encouragement they will ‘pause a litle (and yet not totally declyne the trade).’ For factors, the following have been sent: (1) Andrew Cogan, engaged for five years at 200<em>l.</em> per annum. He has served under the Bantam Presidency for eight or ten years, and is now to be a member of the council at Surat, should there be a vacancy; if not, he is to be sent to the coast of Coromandel as Agent, should that post be unoccupied. (2) William Taylor, who is apprenticed to Cogan until November 4, 1639, when he is to be employed by the Company at 30<em>l.</em> per annum, with an increase of 10<em>l.</em> yearly for five years. (3) Thomas Wheeler, engaged at the same rate. (4) Thomas Morley, at 40<em>l.</em> for the first year, and 10<em>l.</em> increment for seven years. (5) Daniel Elder, at 30<em>l.</em> the first year, and 10<em>l.</em> increment for five years. (6) William Janson, a youth who has been in Spain and Barbary and knows Spanish, engaged at 20<em>l.</em> the first year, and 5<em>l.</em> increment for seven years. The burden of the <em>Discovery</em> is about 500 tons, and her return cargo should include 600 churls of indigo at about 14<em>l.</em> each, 300 bales of calico at 30<em>l.</em> each, and 400 bales of cotton yarn at 5<em>l.</em> each; the rest should be Malabar pepper, gum-lac, sugar, and other ‘groff’ [coarse] commodities. But she is not to be sent home if this would necessitate the borrowing of money for the purpose, for they ‘knowe nott howe to send meanes to discharge the same presently, as things nowe stand here with us, by reason of those pirates and other distractions here att home’. In such case the <em>Discovery</em> may be dispatched to Persia and then, after her return, to the coast and Bantam, proceeding thence to England with a cargo gathered at these various places. Swanley's protest against them for not supplying him with stores. After obtaining provisions, the <em>Discovery</em> sailed on July 30 and reached Johanna eight days later; there further supplies were obtained and they departed on August 17. Swally was reached on September 23, only two men having died during the whole voyage. Moreover, there would be less tendency for the Presidents (as recently at Bantam) to 'comport themselves like pettie kings, which gives occation to theire subordinate factoryes to live at that hight of expence as lately they have done att Maccassar and Mesulapatnam, which (yf reports be true) is beyond all modestie. Then, secondly, the Company must give order for the makeinge good a place for the securitie of there estate in India; which how much prejudiz the want hath ben is very visible, for are wee not continually subject, and have not your estates made good all demaunds from tyme to tyme, be it right or wronge? And, lastly, are wee sertayne of continuall peace with the Portugalls and Dutch? Noe; for the peace wee injoy by them is but for theire owne ends. All which beinge duely considred, 'twer very requisitt that the makeinge good a place were first put in practiz; from which will arrise these benifitts: you shalbe ever secur'd of the most part of your estate, and yf any affront offer'd be enabled to doe [y]ourselves right.'<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-062" href="#sec-05-fn-062" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-062" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Were there but one President, every ship going home from Surat might be provided with sufficient pepper to discharge all the expenses of the voyage. (4 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Benjamin Robinson at Surat to [the Company], December</span> 26, 1638 (<em>O.C.</em> 1655).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Regrets the censure they have passed upon his administration at Ahmadābād. If they could begin in the spring, the cloth would be cheaper and more abundant. Further, the goods from Ahmadābād are made to appear dearer than they really are, because in the invoices the rupee is valued at 2½ mahmūdīs, whereas it should be 2¼. As regards gum-lac, it is impossible to procure any here in the state described by the Company, as the lac gets caked by being carried so far in the heats; but he does not believe that this injures its value for dyeing. By their orders the investment in that commodity was entrusted to a Banyan called ‘Defgee Saw’ [Devajī Sāh], who bought it in his own name. Took great pains over the Surat accounts for 1634, but Mountney got the credit. [<em>Part missing.</em>] The price of indigo was forced up by the competition of the Dutch, while the orders from Surat to secure the full quantity required necessitated the purchase of some of poorer quality than usual. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-146" data-page="146" data-folio="92" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n145_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 92">[p. 92]</button> Indigo this year is both good and plentiful.
