The <em>Francis</em> arrived. She left Gombroon on November 20, and ‘fell with Bumbay’ on December 23. She brought letters from the Company, dated May 25, 1636, and sent overland; also letters from the Agent in Persia and six horses. <em>January</em> 12. A caravan from Ahmadābād, &amp;c., arrived at Rānder, where the goods were stored pending the coming of the <em>William</em>. The usual warehouse being full, one next door was hired at four rupees per month. <em>January</em> 18. Richard Warburton, one of the <em>Swan’s</em> carpenters left here ‘to build up the <em>Bacaine</em>, which wee now call the <em>Convoy</em>’, fell overboard and was drowned. <em>January</em> 27. The <em>Mary</em> came in. She left Gombroon on January 7, and on the same day the <em>William</em> arrived there from Masulipatam. The long-expected caravan from Agra reached Surat. The Portuguese ‘caphila’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-002" href="#sec-04-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> from Goa for Cambay anchored at the mouth of the river, bringing with them from Chaul the Governor’s junk which was sent to Basrā last March. The merchants complained of bad markets at that place, having lost on the average 20 per cent. of their capital; so after all it was fortunate that the <em>Michael</em> was disappointed of her voyage thither. With the ‘caphila’ returned the <em>Kit</em> from Goa, laden with 1,100 corge of ‘chavenes’ [<em>see previous volume</em>, p. 275], taken in payment in freight of ‘that sorte of goodes’ brought by the <em>London</em> from China. <em>January</em> 28. The President and Council went to Swally to see to the supply of provisions for the <em>William</em>. <em>January</em> 29. The <em>Convoy</em> (‘which wee <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-56" data-page="56" data-folio="2" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n55_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 2">[p. 2]</button> now have made a handsome pinnace’) sailed for ‘Sinda<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-003" href="#sec-04-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>’. <em>February</em> 2. The <em>Francis</em> and <em>Kit</em> went down the river with goods to be shipped on the <em>Mary</em>. <em>February</em> 3. Two Surat junks arrived from Gombroon. <em>February</em> 8. Methwold repaired to Swally to superintend the lading of freight goods on the <em>Mary</em>. At the request of the merchants the <em>Francis</em> was filled as well, the two together realizing about 36,000 mahmūdīs for freight and passagemoney. They were both ready to sail on the 18th, when Methwold returned to Surat. <em>February</em> 19. Two English deserters from the <em>Mary</em> presented themselves at the factory, and were put in irons. <em>February</em> 20. William Farr died. He had come out as surgeon of the <em>Mary</em>, and had been left on shore to recover his health. <em>February</em> 22. News came of the arrival of the <em>William</em>. She had left Gombroon on January 30, on which day the <em>Michael</em> reached that port from ‘Sinda’. The <em>William</em> brought some rūnās [Indian madder] and money, but not the promised silk. The King’s junk returned from Mokha, having seen nothing of the pirates. ‘I am heartely joyfull thereof, for upon the safety of this junke depended all the hopes wee had of liberty in India.’ <em>February</em> 27. Two Dutch ships returned from Gombroon, having departed thence on the 3rd. The <em>Michael</em> was to sail two or three days later; but there was no news from the Agent of silk or anything else. ‘God amend the trade or acquitt us fairely of it, for those great charges and no retournes are insupportable.’ <em>February</em> 28. A letter from ‘Tulcidas’ [Tulsī Dās], the broker at Broach, advised that the preceding day the English caravan was attacked and plundered by 500 Rājpūts, and Abel Druce and Walter Kidlie were killed. <em>March</em> 1. A letter from John Turner gave the following account of the disaster: ‘Wee departed from Brodera [Baroda] the 26th present, not much after midnight, and made our manzeele [halt: Arabic <em>manzil</em>] the day followeinge at Carwann [Kārvān]. The next day very earely wee departinge thence arrived very well at Herondale aboute ten of the clocke next morninge, where making little stay wee proceeded, intending to have made our manzeele at Serran.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-004" href="#sec-04-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> But so it happned that the caphila, beinge putt in very good order, cart after carte, and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-57" data-page="57" data-folio="3" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n56_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 3">[p. 3]</button> 120 paeons [men on foot] or theareabouts attending it, was, not above a mile and a halfe from the same place, most furiously sett upon (as it is gennerally reported) by 400 Rashpootes, horse and foote, which makeing to the further end of the caphila,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-005" href="#sec-04-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> leaveing me to guard the van (which we thought was likewise in daunger), and their the shouldiers (according to their generall costome in any eminent perrill) for the most part forsaking them, left both of them to confront the enimie and to spend their last breath, which was there expired through multiplicity of mortall wounds received. The caphila in the meane time I lead forward with all convenient hast, but could not with all meanes used perswade the souldiers that were with me to bring up the reare; but they with divers of the carters and oxen forsooke the caphila, who fledd leaveinge att Sarra[n?] 61 or 62 carrts of goodes (of what sorte wee yet knowe not) to the mercy of the inymy, who hath taken a great part thereof away, leaveing diverse cartes of goodes without oxen [           ]; which hath made mee, not without much trouble and charge, to hire [      ] souldiers at Samblott,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-006" href="#sec-04-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> where wee now are, to goe with oxen and ca[rts to] fetch away what possibly may bee saved. This night they have promised [to set out?] but I much doubt of their performance, because they are very feare- [full].’ Will do his best to recover the goods and documents, but begs that some one of more experience may be sent to see to the payment of customs at Broach. ‘Mr. Druce and Mr. Kidlye by two of our house peons are brought hether and interred as the present time would affoard.’ (<em>Signed copy.</em> 3 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><span class="sc">Note by Ralph Cartwright</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-007" href="#sec-04-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> <span class="sc">of Papers delivered to the Agent at Masulipatam</span>, <span class="sc">January</span> 13, 1637 (<em>O.C.</em> 1585).</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Three journals and ledgers of Bay of Bengal accounts, April 6, 1633, to December 17, 1635. Four journals and ledgers for ‘Harrarpore’, June 12, 1633, to December 17, 1635. One journal and ledger for Balasore, August 15, 1633, to May 31, 1634; Thomas Clark is to account from the latter date to December 17, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-58" data-page="58" data-folio="4" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n57_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 4">[p. 4]</button> 1635. A book of wills and inventories kept in the Bay. One journal and ledger continued on December 17, 1635, ‘and concluded the same day.’ Papers, &amp;c., belonging to the estates of Thomas Colley, Jonathan Mountney, John Powell, and Robert Littler. <em>PS.</em>—Also delivered to the Agent on January 16 duplicates of the said accounts and two books of expenses. (1 <em>p.</em>)</p></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><span class="sc">The Voyage of Captain Weddell’s Fleet</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-008" href="#sec-04-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> (<em>Public Record Office: Dom. Chas. I</em>, vol. cccli. no. 30).</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1637, <em>January</em> 11. Early in the morning the six Portuguese galleons stood out to the Dutch, and got so near before they were discovered that the Dutch rear-admiral had to slip his cable to escape. ‘They continued a slight buckering for the space of five houres in sight of the road, and then by a warninge piece from the castle were called in.’ The Dutch ships were not much injured and their casualties were only six; the galleons were more damaged and they had eleven men slain and ten wounded. ‘All those who were spectators constantlie affirme that there were but three shipps on either side that performed anie thing to purpose, and yet even the best of those also not meriting much comendacion.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-009" href="#sec-04-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> <em>January</em> 15. After much dispute with the Vedor [<em>see the previous volume</em>, p. 90], Weddell received what he would allow for the cordage, &amp;c., sold to him, and next day took leave. <em>January</em> 17. The fleet sailed. Exchanged visits with the Dutch, to whom they showed King Charles’s letter. <em>January</em> 19. It was decided to range the coast to the southwards. <em>January</em> 24. Anchored at ‘Batacalla’.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-010" href="#sec-04-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The Shāhbandar and the King’s Merchant came on board and assured them of a welcome, offering to provide pepper enough to lade one of the smaller vessels. <em>January</em> 27. An agreement was made with them to barter lead for pepper. A letter and a present were sent up country to the King, who replied promising all reasonable concessions. <em>February</em> 10. Having decided to dispatch <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-59" data-page="59" data-folio="5" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n58_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 5">[p. 5]</button> the <em>Planter</em> to England, they began to land their lead. Found that pepper came down very slowly, partly because recent disputes with the Portuguese had diverted the trade into other channels. No stock is ever kept at the port, as the latter has no defences to secure goods from the Malabars and others. It was determined to send an embassy to court to treat for a settled residence; also to give the King a piece of ordnance. <em>February</em> 21. Robinson and Mundy, with two English attendants, started on this mission. From the account given by them on their return, it appears that they reached ‘Icary<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-011" href="#sec-04-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>’ on the 24th and were welcomed by the Master of the King’s Horse, who provided them with supper and lodging. The King was in mourning for the death of his nurse, who was buried that day, and so he could not receive them. Next morning they moved to a house appointed for them, and the King sent a supply of provisions. After dinner they went to the palace, ‘where was an extraordinary shewe purposely prepared for our enterteynement, with a tragicall representacion of some antient historys of those parts, as also sundrie dances both of men and women.’ They were then presented to the King, and kissed his hand. He ‘satt in very great state with most of his nobilitie about him in a most grave and reverent manner, farr beyond the ordinary port of these heathen princes’. The present of cloth, &amp;c., was next laid before him, together with a drawing of the piece of ordnance. He was much pleased, promised to grant all their demands, and vested the factors with robes of honour. On the 27th they were summoned to court and entertained in a private chamber by the King, he himself ‘with a longe wand in his hand reachinge from dishe to dish and envitinge us to tast <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-60" data-page="60" data-folio="6" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n59_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 6">[p. 6]</button> thereof. After supper he firmed his grant to our demands and sent it to the Governour of Baticalla by ourselves.’ In this ‘he granted us by his pattent a large plott of ground,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-012" href="#sec-04-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> lyinge very comodiouslie by the river side, to build us a house, the ruynd parts whereof will aford us squared stone enuffe for that purpose; and moreover in the same patent he hath given us timber (of which there is no want) and all materialls, we paying onlie for laborers hire, which in these parts is very cheape’. He promised to bring weavers, &amp;c., into his country to provide cloth for trade. As regards pepper, he assured them that they could procure from 1,500 to 2,000 ‘candills’ yearly, each ‘candill’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-013" href="#sec-04-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> being about four Portuguese quintals; and he undertook to give them the exclusive right to export that commodity, either by sea or land. He promised to protect them, and offered no objection to their fortifying their factory. He desired them to procure him rarities from England, China, &amp;c., but especially horses from Persia and Arabia.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-014" href="#sec-04-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> <em>March 2.</em> The embassy returned to Bhatkal. <em>March 3.</em> It was decided by a consultation to leave a factory at that place. Anthony Vernworthy was appointed chief, with John Fortune, George Wye, and Peter Vandam as assistants; also an apothecary, a joiner, and a smith. <em>March 6.</em> The three first named went up to court, and returned on the 15th with a supplementary agreement. <em>March 19.</em> The ships sailed, standing along the coast to pick up the <em>Sun</em> and the <em>Anne</em>, which had been sent to ‘Mount Dilley’ to refresh the sick men<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-015" href="#sec-04-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> and look for pepper. <em>March 24.</em> Anchored at that <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-61" data-page="61" data-folio="7" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n60_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 7">[p. 7]</button> place, but found from letters that the two vessels had gone on to Cannanore. A quantity of pepper was bought from the Malabars. <em>March 26.</em> Anchored two leagues off Cannanore and sent Robinson thither with letters to the Captain. He procured some provisions and a quantity of cinnamon. <em>March 28.</em> Sailed for Malacca. At Cochin heard that the King of Achin and his son had both been murdered.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-016" href="#sec-04-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> (6¼ <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">John Weddell off Honāwar to Dom Filippe Mascarenhas</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-017" href="#sec-04-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> [AT GOA], JANUARY 23, 1637 (<em>O.C.</em> 1587).</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘The 17th setting saile from your fort of the Aquida<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-018" href="#sec-04-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>, wee stood off to the Dutch, by reason wee well knewe that they would chase us, and soe report that feare made us runne away from them, as they doe by your gallions. Before my comming to them wee sent our barge, with Mr. Robinson and Captain Moulton, with the King of Englands letter directed to the Governor of Batavia and likewise to the commander of aney fleete; which when they sawe they received with great reverence and respect, and were very unwilling to open it; but by the perswasion of those that carried it hee sent for all his Councell and in publique opened it, Mr. Robinson interpreting into Latin and theire Domine [preacher] into Dutch; and their Admirall<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-019" href="#sec-04-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> signed the acknowledgement thereof and returned the letter againe with its coppy in Dutch. These affaires lasted till night, and then their Admirall came on board to see mee, and theare was not any word of discontentment used amongst us, the Admirall striking his flagge when wee came within shott, in honor of the Kings coulours. All this night wee anchored off the Ilands, and the next day wee had diverse of their captains aboard us; when <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-62" data-page="62" data-folio="8" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n61_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 8">[p. 8]</button> I demaunded of them howe many men they had loste, and they swore very deeply but five men slaine in the whole fleete and one hurt, and that in all that tyme they rode theare they lost but one man, and hee was the Admiralls brother; all their men beeing in very good health, but for the most part they are youths. Their shippes are not of the force I tooke them to bee, nor their ordinance very great; some fewe brasse peeces they have very good. The Admiralls masts were shott, and the Vice Admiralls and Reare Admiralls foremasts; but the fitches<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-020" href="#sec-04-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> they tooke in the timber prize supplyed their wants. They have a great project upon the citty of Goa by an army which is provided to bee in readynes to beleager Goa the next yeare or two yeares hence without all faile, whether from the Mogull or neighbour people, but I feare from the Moores which are about Goa. They are resolved to stay without soe long as weather will give them leave; and yf the gallions doe not come forth to them in that tyme, then, when they perceave them to unrigge the gallions, then they will come in directly upon them into the road and clappe the Admirall on board with one of their old shippes which hath a cutt nose and very old sailes, and to fire them both together yf they can; for backe they dare not goe without doeing somethinge. They toold mee that they had order from their employers in Europe to lye before Goa these three yeares; and the next yeare doe purpose to have 16 shippes to lye before it. More, Gasper Gomes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-021" href="#sec-04-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> hath (as they say) given them notice of 40,000 shott and cordage of all sorts which wee broaght you; which much discontented them. However, they tould mee that had wee beene laden with Portugalls goods that they had no commission to medle with us. They have lying before Malacca eight saile of shippes; on the coast of China four saile; before the Ilands of the Moluccaes four saile. Their losse of anchors and cables was but one, and that from the Reare Admirall, with 12 pipes. They have diverse tymes beene under your Admiralls sterne in seven fathomes, and have heard the tingling of your bells; and intend to woorke some stratageme upon her, yf not prevented by some of your friggotts.’ (<em>Copy.</em> 2 <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-63"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="63" data-folio="9" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n62_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 9">[p. 9]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><span class="sc">Andrew Swan, Purser of the</span> <em>Crispiana</em>, <span class="sc">to the Company, January</span> 24, 1637 (<em>O.C.</em> 1586).</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The ship left Masulipatam on December 3, 1636; called at Petapoli for 72 bales of piece-goods; and off Pulicat took in more goods from the boat <em>John</em>, which had been sent after her from Masulipatam.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-022" href="#sec-04-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Met the <em>London</em>, homeward bound, in the Straits of Sunda on January 18. They are now within three leagues of Bantam. Richard Fitch, late purser of the <em>Crispiana</em>, has been detained on the coast of Coromandel by the Agent; he has left his accounts very imperfect. This letter is sent by the <em>Jewel</em>. (<em>Damaged. Seal. Received December</em> 20. 2¾ <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><span class="sc">President Coulson and Thomas Ivy at Bantam to the Company, January</span> 24, 1637 (<em>O.C.</em> 1588).</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Dispatch of the <em>London</em> and <em>Jewel</em> to England. The <em>Crispiana</em> arrived on the 22nd from the Coast with a cargo invoiced at 35,224 rials of eight. She brought news that Weddell had reached Goa and had sent the King’s letter to the President at Surat. The Dutch <em>Zutphen</em> took off at the Cape four survivors of the <em>Mary’s</em> boat which was wrecked there [<em>see the previous volume</em>, p. 305]. Lee, the commander of the <em>Crispiana</em>, is dead, and William Bradbent has been appointed in his place. ‘So that of all your shipping in India there is scarce one comander hath seene forty yeares; and the most of them scarse thirty yeares (Master White, master of the <em>Blessing</em>, excepted).’ The <em>Pearl</em> must be laid up for want of goods and men. Disposal of the cargo of the <em>Crispiana</em>. (<em>Received December</em> 20. 1¾ <em>pp.</em>)</p></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><span class="sc">William Slade</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-023" href="#sec-04-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> <span class="sc">at Swally to the Company [February]</span> 5, 1637 (<em>O.C.</em> 1589).</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Mary</em> left the Downs on April 14, 1636; lost sight of England on the 18th; passed the Canaries on the 27th; and a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-64" data-page="64" data-folio="10" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories06fost/page/n63_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 10">[p. 10]</button> month later crossed the Line. Their captain, James Slade, died on June 2, and was succeeded by William Bayley, who has now been confirmed in the post by the President and Council. On July 12 they anchored in Table Bay. The loss of their boat described. Sailed on July 14, and on August 6 reached St. Augustine’s Bay, where they obtained plenty of provisions on giving presents to the native chiefs. Went next to Johanna, where they found letters left by the <em>Blessing</em>. On August 28 Captain Weddell came in with the <em>Dragon</em>, <em>Sun</em>, and <em>Planter</em>. The <em>Mary</em> sailed on the 30th, sighted the coast of India on September 27, and anchored at Swally on October [2]. Twenty men died during the voyage, and since then they have lost three more, including Abraham Bryer, who was hanged for an infamous crime. Has prepared six galleons, including a new one of seventy guns, in addition to the two ships from Europe; and with this fleet it is intended to give battle. Received his letter from Ahmadābād of July 25, but hitherto has been unable to find time to reply. Is convinced of Methwold’s freedom from complicity in the robbery of the Diu ships; nevertheless a certain responsibility must rest upon him as head of the English. Moreover, the satisfaction given to the Surat merchants strengthens the claim of those of Diu to be treated in the same way. Here the law must take its course; but if it be found that the claimants have already been compensated by the servants of the Mogul, the money under embargo will at once be released. Intends to maintain the truce in all sincerity, but he cannot permit any further commerce on account of the bad return the English have made for the courtesies they have received. For instance, Weddell has defamed the Portuguese both to the Dutch and to Vīra Bhadra Nāyak, from whom he has obtained permission to build a factory at Bhatkal. That Nāyak declares that he only admitted the English under the impression that they were the friends of the Portuguese, and that he will expel them if the Viceroy desires it.
