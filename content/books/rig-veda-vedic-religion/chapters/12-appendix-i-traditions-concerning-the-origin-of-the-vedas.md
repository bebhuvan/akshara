<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-256"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="256" data-folio="244" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n255_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 244">[p. 244]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX I</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Traditions concerning the Origin of the Vedas</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> painstaking research of Dr. John Muir has made the theories of the origin of the Vedas contained in the Sacred Books of the Hinduism accessible to every student. They are set forth with the Sanskrit passages on which they are founded or in which they are expressed in the third volume of his <em>Original Sanskrit Texts</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is impossible to discuss all these theories here, or to quote all the passage bearing on them. A careful selection of the most important is all that can be attempted here.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Traditions in the Hymns</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are comparatively few statements in the Vedas that give any hint of the history of the writers of the Vedic Hymns or of the conditions in which their ancestors had entered into and settled in north-west India. But the name of the author of each Hymn is preserved in the <em>Anukramaṇī</em> or index to the contents of each Veda which has been handed down from very ancient times. The defenders of the eternity of the Vedic Hymns argue that these ṛishis were not and do not really claim to be the authors of the Hymns which are said to be their words (Muir, <em>Sanskrit Texts</em>, III. 85) <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-257" data-page="257" data-folio="245" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n256_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 245">[p. 245]</button> but that they merely repeated the Hymns and other parts of the Vedas that they had ‘seen’.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Consideration of the words of the ṛishis will, however, show that they distinctly speak of themselves as the authors of the Hymns, without any reference to any supernatural inspiration; and that they uttered the Hymns before an artificial dogma had as yet begun to assign a mysterious divinity to the Hymns to secure sanctions for the elaborate system of priestcraft described in the Brāhmaṇas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Dr. Muir has arranged the sayings of the ṛishis in which they claim to be actual authors of the Hymns in three classes, according to the Sanskrit verb which used to express the idea. These verbs are three:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li><em>Kṛi</em>, to make</li><li><em>Taksh</em>, to fabricate</li><li><em>Jan</em>, to beget, generate or produce.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The verb <em>Kṛi</em> is very frequently used. Its meaning is to do, to make, to manufacture, prepare, work at, build, construct or compose, and the ṛishis used it in this sense of the Hymns that they had composed. There is no suggestion that they considered that they were merely repeating what had existed from eternity in the minds of the Gods.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Four examples may be given.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Kaṇvāso vām brahma kriṇvanti adhvare teshām</em><br>
<em>su śriṇutam havam |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘The Kanvas make a prayer to you: hear well their invocation.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> i. 47, 2.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Evā te hariyojanā suvṛikti Indra brahmāṇi Gotamāsaḥ akran |</em></div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-258"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="258" data-folio="246" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n257_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 246">[p. 246]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Thus O Indra, yoker of steeds, have the Gotamas made hymns for thee efficaciously.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> i. 61. 16.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Etāni vām Aśvinā vardhanāni brahma stomam Gṛitsamadāsaḥ akran</em> | ‘These magnifying prayers, [this] hymn, O Aśvins, the Gritsamadas have <em>made</em> for you.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> ii. 39. 8.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Adha priyam śūsham Indrāya manma brahmakṛito Vṛihadukthād avāchi</em> | ‘An acceptable and powerful hymn has been uttered to Indra by Vrihaduktha, maker of hymns.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> x. 54. 6.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The verb <em>Taksh</em> is less common. It means to form by cutting, or by the plane or chisel ; to chop, slice, fashion out of wood as a carpenter does, and so to form in the mind or invent. <em>Takshaka</em> and <em>Takshan</em> (<em>Tassan</em> in Tamil) are two Sanskrit names for a carpenter. From this it will be seen that by using this verb the ṛishi thinks of himself as the inventor or maker of the Hymns that he utters just as the carpenter is the maker of a cart or a plough. There is here again no suggestion of the later tradition.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Two passages will be sufficient as examples. <em>Sanāyate Gotamaḥ Indra navyam atakshad brahma hariyojanāya ityādi</em> | ‘Nodhas, descendant of Gotama, fashioned this new hymn for [thee] Indra.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> i. 62. 13.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Etam te stomam tuvi-jāta vipro ratham na dhīraḥ svapāḥ ataksham</em> |</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-259"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="259" data-folio="247" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n258_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 247">[p. 247]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘I, a sage, have fabricated this hymn for thee, O powerful [deity], as a skilful workman fashions a car.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> v. 2. 11.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The verb <em>Jan</em> means to generate or beget and there are many passages in which the ṛishis used this simile. However later tradition may interpret the phrase it indicates that the vedic poets fully believed that they themselves were the authors of the Hymns that they sung.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There is no need to quote many passages.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Navam nu stomam Agnaye divaḥ śyenāya jījanam</em><br>
<em>vasvaḥ</em> | <em>kuvid vanāti naḥ</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘I have generated a new hymn to Agni, the falcon of the sky ; will he not bestow on us wealth in abundance ?’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> vii. 15. 4.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Suvṛiktim Indrāya brahma janayanta viprāḥ</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘The sages generated an efficacious production and a prayer for Indra.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> vii. 31. 11.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Asmai te pratiharyate Jātavedo vicharshaṇe Agne</em><br>
<em>janāmi sushṭutim</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘Wise Agni Jatavedas, I generate a hymn for thee who receivest it with favour.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> viii. 43. 2.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Other verbs are used with similar import to convey the idea that the rishi is the maker of the Hymn.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Asmai id u stomam samhinomi ratham na tashṭā iva</em><br>
<em>ityādi</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘To him (Indra) I send forth a hymn, as a carpenter a car.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> i. 61. 4.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-260"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="260" data-folio="248" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n259_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 248">[p. 248]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The following passage shows most clearly that the authors of the Hymns recognized the part that their own minds played in the composition of Hymns.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Imam stomam arhate Jātavedase ratham iva sam</em><br>
<em>mahema manīshayā | bhadrā hi naḥ pramatir asya</em><br>
<em>samsadi Agne sakhye mā rishāma vayam tava |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘Let us with our intellect construct (or, send forth) like a car, this hymn for the adorable Jātavedas, for his wisdom is favourable to us in the assembly. Agni in thy friendship may we never suffer.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> i. 94. 1.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The idea that the hymns were altogether their own work seems however always foreign to the ṛishis.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Some hymns ask for or acknowledge divine assistance just as poets of all nations often do. One poet says:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Indra mṛiḷa mahyam jīvātum ichcha chodāya</em><br>
<em>dhiyam ayaso na dhārām | Yat kincha aham tvāyur</em><br>
<em>idam vadāmi taj jushasva kṛidhi mā devavantam |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘O God (Indra), have mercy, give me my daily bread ; sharpen my mind, like the edge of an iron instrument. Whatever I now may utter, longing for thee, do thou accept it ; give me divine protection.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> vi. 47. 10.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Direct divine inspiration is asserted.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Sa pratnathā Kavi-vṛidhaḥ Indro vākasya vakshaṇiḥ |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘Indra was of old the promoter of the poet, and the augmenter of the song.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> viii. 52. 4.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">From these, and from many other passages it may fairly be reasoned that at the time when the Hymns were <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-261" data-page="261" data-folio="249" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n260_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 249">[p. 249]</button> composed their authors while considering themselves as rendering service specially pleasing to the Gods by composing Hymns, certainly considered those Hymns to be their own work.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">One notable text in the Ṛig-veda which refers to the creation of the Vedas occurs in the Tenth Book in the well known Ninetieth Hymn called the Puruṣa Sūkta. The Tenth Book is generally believed to contain Hymns of much later date than the preceding Books so that there is reason to doubt whether a tradition in the Puruṣa Sūkta is to be taken as of very ancient authority. Professor Macdonell says it is one of the very latest poems of the Ṛigvedic age ; for it presupposes a knowledge of the three Vedas, to which it refers by their names, and it also mentions the four castes. It describes the creation of the universe by the gods out of the body of a primeval giant with a thousand heads. ‘The act of creation is treated as a sacrificial rite, the original man being conceived as a victim, the parts of which when cut up become portions of the universe. His head, we are told, became the sky, his navel the air, his feet the earth, while from his mind sprang the Moon, from his eye the Sun, from his breath the wind ’.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-001" href="#sec-12-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Hymn has been quoted in full on pages 165–6. The verse that relates to the origin of the Vedas states that the Rig, Sāma and Yajur Vedas were born from the sacrifice. It does not mention the Atharva-veda.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tasmād yajnāt sarvā-hataḥ ṛichaḥ sāmāni jajnire |</em><br>
<em>chandāmsi jajnire tasmād yajus tasmād ajayata |</em></div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-262"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="262" data-folio="250" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n261_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 250">[p. 250]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘From that universal sacrifice were born the Rich and Saman verses : the metres were born from it : the Yajush was created.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> x. 90. 9.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><strong>New and Old Hymns</strong></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Dr. Muir quotes more than fifty passages to show that the authors of the Vedic hymns themselves recognized that some hymns were new and some were old. This alone proves that the tradition of the eternity of these hymns had no place in the thought of the Vedic poets themselves. It will be sufficient to quote one or two of these verses.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The ṛishis believed that the Gods would be better pleased if their praises were celebrated in new, and perhaps more elaborate compositions, than if older and possibly ruder, prayers had been repeated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Sa naḥ stavānaḥ ābhara gāyatreṇa navīyasā |</em><br>
<em>rayim vīravatīm isham |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Glorified by our newest hymn, do thou bring to us wealth and food with progeny.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> i. 12. 11.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tān pūrvayā nividā hūmahe vayam Bhagam Mitran</em><br>
<em>Aditim Dakshan Asridham ityādi |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘We invoke with an ancient hymn Bhaga Mitra, Aditi, Daksha, Asridh.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> i. 89. 3.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Yaḥ pūrvyābhir uta nūtanābhir gīrbhir vāvṛidhe</em><br>
<em>gṛiṇatām rishīnām |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘He (Indra) who grew though the ancient and modern hymns of lauding ṛishis.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> vi. 44. 13.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-263"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="263" data-folio="251" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n262_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 251">[p. 251]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Ā sakhāyaḥ subardughāṁ dhenum ajadhvam upa</em><br>
<em>navyasā vachaḥ</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Friends, drive hither the milch cow with a new hymn.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> vi. 48. 11.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Nu navyase navīyase sūktāya sādhaya pathāḥ |</em><br>
<em>pratna-vad rochaya ruchaḥ</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Prepare (O Soma) the paths for our newest, most recent hymn ; and, as of old, cause the lights to shine.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> ix. 9. 8.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Traditions in the Atharva-veda</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Several traditions find expression in the hymns of the Atharva-veda.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">i. The seventh hymn of the tenth maṇḍala of the Atharva-veda in a somewhat similar way to the Puruṣa Sūkta of the Rig-veda identifies Skambha with the universe and describes all things as derived from him. All four vedas are mentioned in it.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Yasmād ṛicho apātakshan yajur yasmād apākashan | sāmāni yasya lomāni atharvāngiraso mukham | Skambham tam brūhi katamaḥ svid eva saḥ</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Declare who is that Skambha from whom they cut off the Ṛich verses, from whom they scraped off the Yajush, of whom the Sāman verses are the hairs, and the verses of the Atharvan and the Angiras the mouth.’ <em>Atharva-veda</em> x. 7. 20.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">ii. The seventh hymn of the eleventh maṇḍala of the Atharva-veda is a glorification of Uchchhishṭa, the ‘residue of sacrifice.’ It states that the Vedas sprung from the ‘Leavings of Sacrifice.’</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-264"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="264" data-folio="252" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n263_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 252">[p. 252]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Ṛichaḥ sāmāni chhandāmsi purāṇam yajushā saha |</em><br>
<em>Uchchhishṭāj jajnire sarva divi devāḥ diviśritaḥ |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘From the leavings of the sacrifice sprung the Rich and Sāman verses, the metres, the Purana with the Yajush, and all the gods who dwell in the sky.’ <em>Atharva-veda</em> xi. 7. 24.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">iii. Another tradition in the same Veda says that the Vedas sprung from Indra, and he sprung from them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Sa vai ṛigbhyo ajāyata tasmād ṛicho ajāyanta |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Indra sprung from the Rich verses; the Rich verses sprung from him.’ <em>Atharva-veda</em> xiii. 4. 38.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">iv. A verse in a later book states that the Vedas sprung from Time.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Kālād ṛichaḥ sambhavan yajuḥ kālād ajayata |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘From Time the Rich verses sprung; the Yajush sprung from Time.’ <em>Atharva-veda</em> xix. 54. 3.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Traditions in the Brahmanas</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is impossible to say definitely at what dates the various Brāhmaṇas were composed. Both they and the Atharva-veda belong to the same period and both are much later than the Rig-veda. The period when the Brāhmaṇas were composed may however with some reason be said to end about 600 <span class="sc">B. C.</span>, but, of course, they contain traditions earlier than that date.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">i. The Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa closely connected with the text of the Black (Kṛishṇa) Yajur-veda, is one of the earliest Brāhmaṇas, while the Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa is of later date, perhaps one of the latest of them.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-12-fn-002" href="#sec-12-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-12-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> One or <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-265" data-page="265" data-folio="253" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n264_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 253">[p. 253]</button> two quotations from them will show the character of the beliefs concerning the origin of the Vedas that had become current during the Brāhmaṇic period and that were embodied in and endorsed by them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">ii. Two passages may be cited from the Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa. The first is a eulogy of Vāch, the goddess of speech. She is declared to be the mother of the Vedas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Vāg aksharam prathamajā ṛitasya vedānām mātā</em><br>
<em>amṛitasya nābhiḥ</em> | <em>sā no jushāṇā upa yajnam āgād</em><br>
<em>avantī devī suhavā me astu</em> | <em>yām ṛishayo mantra-kṛito</em><br>
<em>manishiṇaḥ anvaichhan devās tapasā śrameṇa</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Vāch is an imperishable thing and the first-born of the ceremonial, the mother of the Vedas, and the centrepoint of immortality. Delighting in us, she came to the sacrifice. May the protecting goddess be ready to listen to my invocation—she whom the wise rishis, the composers of hymns, the gods, sought by austere fervour and by laborious devotion.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa</em> ii. 8. 8. 5.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">iii. The other passage is an example of the grotesque symbolism that surprises the reader of the Sacred Books. It states that the Vedas are the hair of Prajapati’s beard.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Prājāpater vai etāni śmaśrūṇi yad vedaḥ</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa</em> iii. 3. 9. 1.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">iv. Of the two passages quoted here from the later Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa, the first is interesting for the figure of speech and for its assertion that the Vedas were dug from the mind-ocean by the labour of the gods, the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-266" data-page="266" data-folio="254" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n265_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 254">[p. 254]</button> <em>devas</em>, who are not of supreme rank. In the other passage the Vedas are called the breathings of the Great Being and classed with works like the <em>sūtras</em> which are generally considered to be merely human compositions (<em>paurusheya</em>) without independent authority.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Mano vai samudraḥ</em> | <em>manaso vai samudrād vāchā ’bhryā devās trayīm vidhyām nirakhanan</em>. | ‘Mind is the Ocean. From the mind-ocean, with speech for a shovel, the gods dug out the triple Vedic science.’ <em>Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa</em> vii. 5. 2. 52.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>Sa yathā ārdredhāgner abhyāhitāt pṛithag dhūmāḥ viniścharanti evam vai are ’sya mahato bhūtasya niśvasitam etad yad ṛigvedo yajurvedaḥ sāmavedo ’tharvāṅgirasaḥ itihāsaḥ purāṇam vidyā upanishadaḥ ślokāḥ sūtrāṇy anuvyākhyānāni vyākhyānāni asyaiva etāni sarvāṇi niśvasitāni</em> | ‘As from a fire made of moist wood various modifications of smoke proceed, so is the breathing of this Great Being the Rig-veda, the Yajur-veda, the Sāma-veda, the Atharvāṅgirasas, the Itihāsas, Purāṇas, Science, the Upaniṣhads, Slokas, aphorisms, comments of different kinds—all these are his breathings.’ <em>Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa</em> xiv. 5. 4. 10.</p></blockquote></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Traditions in the Upanishads</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The earlier Upanishads were composed approximately between 600 and 480 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> The teaching in them is generally philosophic. The traditions of the past are used to illustrate metaphysical ideas, and are repeated as <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-267" data-page="267" data-folio="255" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n266_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 255">[p. 255]</button> symbolic of esoteric truth. Thus in the Chhāndogya Upanishad Prajāpati is said to have produced the three Vedas through the fire, the wind and the light of the sun.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Prajāpatir lokān abhyatapat | teshām tapyamānānām rasān prābṛihad agnim pṛithivyāḥ vāyum anta rikshād ādityam divaḥ | sa etās tisro devatāḥ abhyatapat | tāsām tapyamānānām rasān prābṛihad agner ṛicho vāyor yajumshi sāma ādityāt | sa etām trayīm vidyām abhyatapat | tasyās tapyamānāyāḥ rasān prābṛihad bhūr iti ṛigbhyo bhuvar iti yajurbhyaḥ svar iti sāmabhyaḥ |</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Prajāpati infused warmth into the worlds, and from them so heated he drew forth their essences, viz. Agni (fire) from the earth, Vāyu (wind) from the air, and Sūrya (the sun) from the sky. He infused warmth into these three deities, and from them so heated he drew forth their essences,—from Agni the Rich verses, from Vāyu the Yajush verses, and from Sūrya the Sāman verses. He then infused heat into this triple science and from it so heated he drew forth its essences,—from Rich verses the syllable <em>bhūḥ</em>, from Yajush verses, <em>bhuvaḥ</em>, and from Sāman verses <em>svar</em>.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Chhāndogya Upanishad</em> iv. 17. 1, 3.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Manu’s account</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Manu assigns the same origin to the Vedas in his account of creation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Sarveshām tu sa nāmāni karmāṇi cha pṛithak pṛithak | Veda-śabdebhya evādau pṛithak samsthāś cha nirmame | karmātmanam cha devānām so ’sṛijat prāṇinām prabhuḥ | sādhyānām cha gaṇam sūkshmam</em></div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-268"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="268" data-folio="256" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n267_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 256">[p. 256]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>yajnam chaiva sanātanam | Agni-vāyu-ravibhyas tu</em><br>
<em>trayam brahma sanātanam | dudoha yajna-siddhyar-tham ṛig-yajuḥ-sāma-lakshaṇam |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘He (Brahmā) in the beginning fashioned from the words of the Veda the several names, functions and separate conditions of all [creatures]. That Lord also created the subtile host of active and living deities, and of Sādhyas, and eternal sacrifice. And, in order to the performance of sacrifice, he drew forth from Agni, from Vāyu, and from Sūrya, the triple eternal Veda, distinguished as Ṛich, Yajush and Sāman.’</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Mānava dharma-sāstra</em> i. 26, 23.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>The Mahabharata</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sarasvati may be said to have taken the place of Vāch in later Hindu mythology, and the Mahābhārata calls <em>Sarasvati the mother of the Vedas.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Vedānām mātaram paśya mat-sthām devīm Sarasvatīm |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Behold Sarasvati, mother of the Vedas, abiding in me.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Mahābhārata, Śānti-parva</em> 12. 920.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>The Harivamsa</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Harivamsa, the nineteenth or supplementary book of the Mahābhārata, gives a different tradition :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Gāyatrī, quoted on page 186, is the most famous of Hindu prayers. It is repeated to this day by every Brahman in India in his morning prayers. All kinds of mystic properties are ascribed to the Gāyatrī, and the Harivamsa contains a verse which states that the Vedas were produced from the Gāyatrī.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-269"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="269" data-folio="257" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n268_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 257">[p. 257]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Tato ’sṛijad vai tripadām gāyatrīm veda-mātaram</em> |<br>
<em>Akaroch chaiva chaturo vedān gāyatri-sambhavān</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">After framing the world, Brahmā ‘next created the Gāyatrī of three lines, mother of the Vedas, and also the four Vedas which sprung from the Gāyatrī.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Harivamsa</em> 11, 516.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>Statements in the Puranas</strong></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Puranas give different accounts of the origin of the Vedas, in harmony with later developments of Hinduism. For instance, in one passage the Vishṇu Purāṇa identifies the three Vedas, omitting the Atharva-veda, with Vishṇu.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Sa riñ-mayaḥ sa sāmamayaḥ sa chātmā sa</em><br>
<em>yajurmayaḥ</em> | <em>ṛig-yajuḥ-sāma-sārātmā sa evātmā śarīriṇām</em> |</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘He is composed of the Ṛich, of the Sāman, of the Yajush ; he is the soul, consisting of the essence of the Ṛich, Yajush and Sāman, he is the soul of embodied spirits.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Vishṇu Purāṇa</em> iii. 3. 19.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On the other hand, the Bhāgavata Purāṇa says in one place that the Vedas issued from the mouth of Brahmā the Creator.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Kadāchid dhyāyataḥ srashṭur vedāḥ āsamś chaturmukhāt</em> | <em>katham srakshyāmy aham lokān samavetān</em><br>
<em>yathā purā</em> | . . . <em>Ṛig-yajuḥ-sāmā-tharvākhyān vedān</em><br>
<em>pūrvādibhir mukhaiḥ</em> | <em>śastram ijyām stuti-stomam</em><br>
<em>prāyaśchittam vyadhāt kramāt.</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Once the Vedas sprung from the four-faced Creator, as he was meditating “how shall I create the aggregate <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-270" data-page="270" data-folio="258" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n269_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 258">[p. 258]</button> worlds as before ?”. . . He formed from his eastern and other mouths the Vedas called Ṛich, Yajush, Saman and Atharvan, together with praise, sacrifice, hymns, and expiation.’ <em>Bhāgavata Purāṇa</em> iii. 12, 34 and 37.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Vishṇu Purāṇa</em> (i. 5, 48ff) gives the same explanation, with details as to the particular mouth of Brahmā by which each Veda was uttered.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-12-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-12-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body"><span class="sc">Macdonell</span>, <em>Sanskrit Literature</em>, p. 133.</span></li><li id="sec-12-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-12-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body"><span class="sc">Macdonell</span>, <em>Sanskrit Literature</em>, p. 203.</span></li></ol></section>
