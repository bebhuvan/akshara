<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-15"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="15" data-folio="3" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n14_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 3">[p. 3]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>The early Aryans</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">In</span> the dawning time of history, somewhere in the lands beyond Afghanistan and north of Persia roamed bold tribes of fair-complexioned men and women with their horses and cattle. From stories that have come down to us about them, from words that they used which have still place in our speech, and from rites of worship still observed by many of their descendants to-day something can be known of their life and thoughts. They were a rough, brave, hardy, adventurous race, of honest and simple soul.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Some of them gradually limited their wanderings to Irān, the land of Persia. There they settled and there they stayed, becoming the ancestors of the Parsis now in India; and their speech became the old Zend language and their religion, with its sacred literature, the <em>Avesta</em> of Zoroaster, developed into Zoroastrianism.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Others of these tribes, more daring, by long marches, and through many generations approached and entered India from the north-west. These were the men who called themselves ‘Áryas’, Aryans, a word meaning ‘kinsmen’ as distinct from the aboriginal tribes already dwelling in the land.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-001" href="#sec-04-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> And though much concerning them is obscure, not a little is evident, and to-day it is <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-16" data-page="16" data-folio="4" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n15_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 4">[p. 4]</button> possible to look back across the many centuries that separate us from those nomads and learn what manner of men they were. Having some conception of the men themselves, remembering that they were once alive, and vastly concerned with matters that fill men’s lives, even to-day, it will be possible to go on to some survey of their hymns and their beliefs, their hopes and their fears, their gods and their demons, not as to a dry study of abstractions but in hope of finding the vital beginnings of faiths that still sway the hearts of millions in India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>The home of the Aryans</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the names of the rivers mentioned in their hymns it is clear that about the time that they were composed, the chief settlements of the Aryans in India were in the neighbourhood of the Sindhu, the modern Indus, a river which after receiving the waters of its tributaries is so wide that people on one bank cannot see the opposite bank. The Himalayas would prevent them from turning towards the north, and it is thus clear that before they crossed the Yamuna, the modern Jumna, and made their way to the Ganges, the Aryans dwelt mainly in East Kabulistan and the Panjab. The Saraswati was their southern boundary during that period.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>The Dasyus</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">As to other races that were in the land there is little information in the hymns of the Aryans and the science of ethnology has to help us. From it we learn that before the Aryans came India was sparsely peopled by some of the races that are now often called ‘jungle tribes’. Among them the majority would be Dravidians. The Gonds, Bhīls and Santāls are modern <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-17" data-page="17" data-folio="5" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n16_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 5">[p. 5]</button> survivors of those races. Some were very humble in the scale of humanity burying their dead in the cells made of stone-slabs, called <em>Kistvaens</em> by scientists and ‘Pāṇḍava vīdu’—the houses of the Pāṇḍava brothers when in exile—by the Tamil people of the south to this day. Those in the south were never so subject to the Aryans as the tribes of the north and grew into the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-18" data-page="18" data-folio="6" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n17_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 6">[p. 6]</button> powerful and civilized Tamil and Telugu nations, but there is no hint of this in the Rig-veda. There these aborigines are named ‘Dasyu’ ‘destroyers’ or ‘Dāsa’, or ‘injurious’. Their skins were much darker than those of the Aryans and so they were stigmatized as ‘black’ or ‘black-skins’. From the shape of their broad noses they were called ‘goat-nosed’. They possessed herds, they had strongholds called ‘pur’. Those who were captured were made slaves, and the very word ‘<em>dāsa</em>’ came to mean ‘slave’ in later Sanskrit and cognate languages. The references to their religion describe them as offering no sacrifices, being unbelievers in the gods of the Aryans, and thus grievously impious. There are two passages in which they are called <em>śiśna-devah</em>, ‘whose god is the phallus’ (<em>Rig-veda</em> vii. 21. 5; x. 99. 3), and it will be remembered that the phallus, as the linga, came to be the chief symbol of the god Siva in later Hinduism.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-002" href="#sec-04-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-001"><img src="/figures/rig-veda-vedic-religion/fig-001_p0017.webp" alt="Map of India in the Vedic and Epic Age, about B.C. 1500–1000, showing rivers, regions, and Lanka, published by the Christian Literature Society." loading="lazy"></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">From the first these Dasyus were the enemies of the Aryans, but their demon worship greatly affected the Aryans, especially in the simpler domestic religion that has always been that of the ordinary folk.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Aryan houses</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Probably the conditions of climate and soil were then much as they are now and the Aryans who had lived in tents while they were nomads became dwellers in houses. The roofs of these houses were of long bamboos laid on rafters supported by pillars or corner posts. The spaces between the pillars were filled in with straw or reeds, tied in bundles. In places where stone was <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-19" data-page="19" data-folio="7" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n18_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 7">[p. 7]</button> readily obtainable it was sometimes used, and Indra is said to have destroyed a hundred cities of stone. The roof would be of thatch, and the various timbers were fastened together with bars, pegs, ropes and thongs. The house had a door fastened by a strap. A number of these houses made a village. Such villages were near to streams or rivers for the sake of crops and cattle. There were ramparts and ditches to protect the village from enemies or from flood. But there were no cities, if by city is meant a collection of houses near to each other surrounded by a wall.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Food</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Bread, milk and products from milk, such as butter, cakes of flour and butter, vegetables and fruits were the usual articles of food. But meat, roasted or boiled, was eaten, though probably only at great feasts and family gatherings. The late Dr. Rajendralala Mitra occupied the highest rank among Indian scholars, and in his <em>Indo-Aryans</em>, he has a chapter headed, ‘Beef in Ancient India.’ It begins as follows:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The title of this paper will, doubtless, prove highly offensive to most of my countrymen; but the interest attached to the inquiry in connexion with the early social history of the Aryan race on this side of the Himalaya, will, I trust, plead my excuse. The idea of beef—the flesh of the earthly representative of the divine Bhagavati—as an article of food is so shocking to the Hindus, that thousands over thousands of the more orthodox among them never repeat the counterpart of the word in their vernaculars, and many and dire have been the sanguinary conflicts which the shedding of the blood of cows has caused in this country. And yet it would seem that there was a time when <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-20" data-page="20" data-folio="8" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n19_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 8">[p. 8]</button> not only no compunctions, visitings of conscience, had a place in the mind of the people in slaughtering cattle—when not only the meat of that animal was actually esteemed a valuable aliment—when not only was it a mark of generous hospitality, as among the ancient Jews, to slaughter the ‘fatted calf’ in honour of respected guests—but when a supply of beef was deemed an absolute necessity by pious Hindus in their journey from this to another world, and a cow was invariably killed to be burnt with the dead.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Dr. Rajendralala Mitra quotes Colebrooke’s opinion as follows:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>It seems to have been anciently the custom to slay a cow on that occasion (the reception of a guest) and a guest was therefore called a <em>goghna</em>, or ‘cow killer’. In the <em>Uttara Rāma Charitra</em> the venerable old poet and hermit Valmiki, when preparing to receive his brother sage Vasiṣṭa, the author of one of the original law books (<em>Smṛitis</em>) which regulates the religious life of the people, and a prominent character even in the Vedas, slaughtered a lot of calves expressly for the entertainment of his guests. Vasiṣṭa, in his turn, likewise slaughtered the ‘fatted calf’ when entertaining Viśvamitra, Janaka, Satānanda, Jamadagnya, and other sages and friends.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-003" href="#sec-04-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Cows and oxen were sacrificed on certain occasions even in later Vedic times and on such occasions priest and sacrificer would eat part of the flesh of the victim.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-004" href="#sec-04-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Salt is not mentioned in the hymns, though it abounds in the Northern Panjab.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For drink the Aryans used <em>surā</em>, a brandy made from corn or barley, and <em>soma</em>, the sap of a herb of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-21" data-page="21" data-folio="9" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n20_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 9">[p. 9]</button> Sarcostemma species, which on account of its stimulating and exciting character was deified.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Aryans kept herds of cattle and horses. Goats, buffaloes and camels are mentioned. There are many prayers for these in the hymns, especially for cows that yield the white milk from which mead and butter, ‘the favourite food of gods and men’, were prepared. An Aryan called his daughter <em>duhitṛi</em> or milkmaid, and the word <em>gopa</em>, cowkeeper, came to mean any protector.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Agriculture</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Agriculture was the principal industry. Plough and harrow, mattock and hoe were used, and sometimes water was conducted in irrigation channels. There were two harvests in the year, especially for barley. The grain was threshed; the chaff was winnowed away; the corn was ground in a mill; and bread was made from the flour. Agriculture was more important than hunting with the bow, or capturing game with snares, or than fishing.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Artisans</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Wood-workers, who had tools such as hatchets and planes, built wagons and war-chariots, or carved wooden cups. There were tanners who made leather from the hide of slaughtered cattle, and manufactured it into waterbottles, bow-strings, slings and the like. There were workers in metal, smiths who made weapons and rims for the wheels of carts, and potters. Women spun and wove wool and made garments. There were even barbers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Trade existed as barter, and the value of goods was calculated at so many cows. Golden ornaments were beginning to be used. Goldsmiths are spoken of and usurers are mentioned. The Babylonian <em>minā</em> of gold seems to have been called <em>manā</em> in Sanskrit which may <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-22" data-page="22" data-folio="10" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n21_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 10">[p. 10]</button> be an indication of very early intercourse between India and the western Semitic races. Ships are mentioned.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>The father</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The family was the unit and the father of the family was its high-priest and head, and controlled the worship of the ancestors of the family in all details. He knew the peculiar ritual which was traditional in his family, and which had to be maintained unchanged, if the favour of the dead was to be retained. He alone had the power to pass on the rites to his son. The reverence and the power which his priestly position brought him made him supreme in the household. He had full power over his wife and his young children, and his grown-up sons were under his authority. The property of the family was altogether in his hands. This was the source of the paternal authority (<em>patria potestas</em>) of the Roman father, and of the prominent place held by the father in Greece, Persia, India, and among Teutonic and Slavonic peoples as well. This type of family, which is known as the patriarchal, succeeded an earlier and less developed type.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-005" href="#sec-04-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Marriage</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Youths and maidens saw each other at festivals. Should the youth be attracted he generally but not always asked for the maiden in marriage from her father through a friend. It was considered improper for the marriages of younger sons or daughters to be arranged before those of their elder brothers and sisters. If the suitor was approved he occasionally had to purchase his bride by giving gifts to her father. A dowry was sometimes given with the bride. The marriage was performed in the presence of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-23" data-page="23" data-folio="11" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n22_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 11">[p. 11]</button> both families and their friends in the house of the bride’s parents. The fire was kindled on the domestic altar and the bride was handed by her father to her husband. The bridegroom took hold of the right-hand of the bride with his right-hand repeating the formula:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">By thy right-hand for happiness I take thee,</span><br>
<span class="line">That thou may’st reach old age with me, thy husband.</span><br>
<span class="line">Aryaman, Bhaga, Savitar, Puramdhi,</span><br>
<span class="line">Gave thee to me to rule our home together.</span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Rig-veda</em> x. 85. 36</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">After repeating other verses he led his bride round the altar, from left to right (<em>pradakshina</em>), and she was then his wife, and he her husband. Then followed a feast and the wife was taken to her husband’s house on a wagon decked with flowers and drawn by white cattle. Here hymns or parts of hymns were chanted, full of goodwill to the wife, and her authority was solemnly declared.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">Here now remain, nor ever part;</span><br>
<span class="line">Enjoy the whole expanse of life,</span><br>
<span class="line">With son and grandson joyous sport</span><br>
<span class="line">Be glad in heart within your house.</span><br>
<span class="line">So rule and govern in thy home</span><br>
<span class="line">Over thy husband’s parents both;</span><br>
<span class="line">His brother and his sister, too,</span><br>
<span class="line">Are subject likewise there to thee.</span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Rig-veda</em> x. 85. 42, 46.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Clearly the Aryan bride was of an age fitting her to be wife and mother and mistress of a home when she was married. In the home the wife took part with her husband in the daily sacrifice.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Some kings and nobles might have more than one wife. The two Aśvins had together one wife, Sūryā, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-24" data-page="24" data-folio="12" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n23_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 12">[p. 12]</button> the daughter of the sun-god. But the ordinary condition was ‘a united pair, with one heart and one mind, free from discord’. The marriage of blood relations was thought wrong. The birth of a daughter is nowhere sought, but sons are earnestly desired.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Widows</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">There is no evidence in the Rig-veda that when her husband died the Aryan widow had to burn herself on his funeral pyre. The eighteenth hymn of the tenth Book of Rig-veda refers to the death of a husband, to the vicissitude of life and to the funeral ceremonies. The seventh verse runs as follows in Kaegi’s translation.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-006" href="#sec-04-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The women here, still happy wives, not widowed, shall come and bring rich oil and precious ointment; and tearless, blooming, rich adorned, may they first approach the resting-place of the departed. <em>Rig-veda</em> x. 18. 7.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The words ‘may they <em>first</em> approach the place’ are a translation of the Sanskrit words</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>ā rohantu yonim agre.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">By the most awful crime in the history of literature this phrase was altered in later times. It then read</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p><em>ā rohantu yonim agneh</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">meaning ‘let them enter the place of fire’, and by this terrible falsification the verse was made to justify the burning of widows.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The exact opposite was the fact. Among the early Aryans the widow might marry again. The very next verse of the same hymn calls on the widow to rise from beside the bier or pyre and take the hand of her new <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-25" data-page="25" data-folio="13" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n24_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 13">[p. 13]</button> husband, ‘doubtless’, says Prof. Macdonnell (<em>Sanskrit Literature</em>, p. 126) ‘a brother of the deceased, in accordance with an ancient marriage custom’ of which a trace remained among the Hebrews. I quote his translation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">Rise up; come to the world of life, O woman;</span><br>
<span class="line">Thou liest here by one whose soul has left him.</span><br>
<span class="line">Come: thou hast now entered upon the wifehood</span><br>
<span class="line">Of this thy lord who takes thy hand and woos thee.</span><br>
<span class="line"><em>Rig-veda</em> x. 18. 8.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There may have been instances of widow burning in early Aryan times but it was during a much later period (A.D. 650–1200) that the custom of burning a widow with her husband’s body came gradually into force. Such a widow is highly praised in the <em>Garuḍa Purāṇa</em>; she was called a <em>satī</em> (pronounced <em>suttee</em>) emphatically a ‘good’ woman. Hence the modern name of the custom. At the same time it became customary for a widow who did not ascend her husband’s pyre to live a life of asceticism and privation, and precepts sanctioning the practice were inserted in the later sacred books. Farquhar quotes one:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>If a woman’s husband dies, let her lead a life of chastity, or else mount his pyre. <em>Vishnusmṛiti</em> xxv. 14.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Position of women</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Since the head of the family was a man and every clan and family wanted men to protect it from its enemies, there was a tendency to set less value on women. The general opinion of the female sex seems to have been that put into the mouth of Indra: ‘Indra himself hath said, The mind of woman brooks not discipline. Her intellect hath little weight’ (<em>Rig-veda</em> viii. 33. 17), and ‘the hearts of women <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-26" data-page="26" data-folio="14" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n25_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 14">[p. 14]</button> are those of hyenas’ (x. 95. 15). From a very early date prostitution was an institution.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">That women were not debarred from hearing the Vedas is clear, for the authors of some of the hymns of the Aryan tribes were women. Apālā composed a hymn that is now the eightieth in the eighth book of the Rig-veda, and Ghoshā, a leper maiden, was author of two, the thirty-ninth and the fortieth in the tenth book.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Beyond saying that the king, <em>rāja</em>, was a ruler it is not possible to define his exact rank and authority.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>The king</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Probably it entirely depended on the individual. He was the chief of a group of families. Sometimes he was chosen. Sometimes his rank was hereditary. In time of peace he was ‘judge and protector’ of his people, who brought to him voluntary gifts. In time of war he was the leader of the warriors, and before a battle he would offer sacrifice for his tribe, or cause a priestly singer to offer it. This was the beginning of that difference of office which lay at the root of the later distinction of the warrior and priest castes among the Aryans.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Morality and crime</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Morality was a family and tribal matter. Truth, right-conduct, kindliness, loyalty to one’s neighbour and comrade, bravery, and—later—careful observance of religious rites and liberality to priests were counted high virtues. Fraud, malignant speech, lying and treachery were roundly condemned. Violence to defenceless maidens and the adultery of a wife were regarded as grave crimes.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Such crimes as one would expect to find among tribal peoples entering on a new civilization are mentioned in the hymns. Raiders carry off cattle. Robbers are found <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-27" data-page="27" data-folio="15" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n26_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 15">[p. 15]</button> on highways. A thief steals an honest man’s clothes. There are sorcerers who utter harmful spells, and seducers of women.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For protection from his enemies the Aryan trusted the gods and his own right arm. For crimes in the community there were judgements, ordeals and punishments, and the vengeance of the gods.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gambling</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Gambling was a terrible curse to the Aryan. The hymns say that the father’s punishment of the dissolute son is of no effect; the player is unmoved by the destruction of his home; he remains indifferent though his wife becomes the property of others; he rises early and indulges in the passion of play till evening; defeat in play is equivalent to starvation and thirst.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In one of the hymns<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-007" href="#sec-04-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> a gambler vividly describes his own experience:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>1. The tumbling, air-born (products) of the great Vibhidaka tree (i. e. the dice) delight me as they continue to roll on the dice board. The exciting dice seem to me like a draught of the soma-plant growing on mount Pujavat.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>7. Hooking, piercing, deceitful, vexatious, delighting to torment, the dice dispense transient gifts, and again ruin the winner; they appear to the gambler covered with honey.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>13. Never play with dice; practice husbandry; rejoice in thy property, esteeming it sufficient.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At a sacrifice, the Kshatriya in particular used to play at dice with his wife, or wives, and sons.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-28"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="28" data-folio="16" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n27_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 16">[p. 16]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Dancing</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Dancers or actors afforded entertainment to the Aryans. Ushas is said to display herself like a dancer who decks herself with ornaments. Allusion is made to the living going forth to dance and laugh after a funeral. Drums are mentioned, and a hymn in the Atharva-veda is addressed to that musical instrument.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Chariot races</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Aryans delighted in chariot races. The sixty-ninth hymn in the eighth book of the Rig-veda is a prayer to Indra, called Śatakratu the god of a hundred rites, for success in a coming chariot race. It reads as follows in Griffith’s translation:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">1. O Śatakratu, truly I have made none else my</span><br>
<span class="line">comforter.</span><br>
<span class="line">Indra, be gracious unto us.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">2. Though who hast ever aided us kindly of old to</span><br>
<span class="line">win the spoil,</span><br>
<span class="line">As such, O Indra, favour us.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">3. What now! As prompter of the poor thou</span><br>
<span class="line">helpest him who sheds the juice.</span><br>
<span class="line">Wilt thou not, Indra, strengthen us?</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">4. O Indra, help our chariot on, yea, thunderer,</span><br>
<span class="line">though it lag behind:</span><br>
<span class="line">Give this my car the foremost place.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">5. Ho there! why sittest thou at ease? Make</span><br>
<span class="line">thou my chariot to be first:</span><br>
<span class="line">And bring the fame of victory near.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">6. Assist our car that seeks the prize. What can</span><br>
<span class="line">be easier for thee?</span><br>
<span class="line">So make thou us victorious.</span></div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-29"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="29" data-folio="17" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n28_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 17">[p. 17]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>War</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">These chariot races were a training for war. And the tribes of the Aryans were constantly at war, sometimes among themselves and sometimes with the Dasyus, the races in India before the Aryans entered it. These Aryan expeditions were often raids for cattle or reprisals on tribes that had attacked the Aryan villages. Indeed the term <em>gosuyudh</em> ‘fighting among or for cows’ is used in the Veda as a name for a warrior in general (i. 112, 122) and a common word for battle is <em>gaviṣṭi</em>, literally ‘striving for cows’.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The bands of the Aryans marched under their leaders, who had banners. They would sing or shout the prowess of their ancestors, and boast of the aid which Indra or Bṛihaspati granted them. Conches were blown as horns. Sometimes the leader drove in a war-chariot covered with cowhides; some warriors used bows and arrows; others had darts. The bands often slew all in the villages they conquered. Sometimes they were content to carry off the plunder. When an invader attacked an Aryan settlement, ramparts were thrown up, trees were made into barricades, and the gods were called to aid. The ‘Weapon-song’ is a hymn that echoes with the clamour of the strife.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-008" href="#sec-04-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>The childhood of the Aryan race</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">From the Vedas it is clear that like their civilization, the science of the Aryans was of a rude and elementary character. The earliest Aryans only knew a few stars or constellations, and there is no certain evidence that the planets were known to the singers of the Rig-vedic age. Aryan ideas of the origin of the universe are utterly <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-30" data-page="30" data-folio="18" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n29_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 18">[p. 18]</button> inadequate and primitive; and though Aryan medical science distinguished several diseases, its remedies were charms and amulets and herbs used in conjunction with incantations. The world was very wonderful to the Aryans as they lived and fought in the Land of the Five Rivers. But theirs was a very limited life, unsophisticated in its aims, direct and frank in its activities, lived in the open air, full of health from sun and wind and rains. As Max Müller says:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>In the hymns of the Veda we see man left to himself to solve the riddle of this world. We see him crawling on like a creature of the earth with all the desires and weakness of his animal nature. Food, wealth and power, a large family and a long life, are the theme of his daily prayers. But he begins to lift up his eyes. He stares at the tent of heaven, and asks who supports it? He opens his eyes to the winds, and asks them whence and whither? He is awakened from darkness and slumber by the light of the sun, and him whom his eyes cannot behold, and who seems to grant him the daily pittance of his existence, he calls ‘his life, his breath, his brilliant Lord and Protector’.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-04-fn-009" href="#sec-04-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-04-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">And it is only as this is remembered that it is possible to appreciate the hopes and fears, the prayers and aspirations, the courage and the patience of the bards who sang, in those far away centuries, the very heart’s thoughts of the men whose pilgrimage into India was to have such a mighty effect on the history and the thought of the world.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Their hymns</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">They were the direct ancestors of many of the tribes comprised in the great Brahman caste of modern India. Their speech was the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-31" data-page="31" data-folio="19" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n30_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 19">[p. 19]</button> mother language of many of the languages spoken in India to-day, and from it all have borrowed the terms of philosophy, worship and faith. Their religion was the beginning of religions that have stirred the hearts of millions. To know how they prayed and built their earliest altar-fires and offered sacrifice with song is to know how the forefathers of Hindu and Greek and Briton sought divine grace and divine protection. One of the most precious heir-looms of the ages for all thinking men are the hymns which were first sung by the poets among those primitive warriors and herdsmen. For those hymns have come down to us, and however much they may have been changed between the moment that the inspired bard chanted them to his tribesmen and the time when they were written down to abide for ever, they bring those far away days back to us. Through them the hopes and fears of the singer and his hearers ring in our ears. In them we come face to face with the joys and sorrows, the war and peace, the funerals and festivals of forty centuries ago.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-04-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-04-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body"><span class="sc">Macdonnell</span>, <em>Sanskrit Literature</em>, p. 152.</span></li><li id="sec-04-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-04-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body"><span class="sc">Macdonnell</span>, <em>Sanskrit Literature</em>, p. 153; <em>Vedic Index</em> ii. 382.
