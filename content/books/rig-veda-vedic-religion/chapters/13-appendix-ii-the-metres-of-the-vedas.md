<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-271"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="271" data-folio="259" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n270_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 259">[p. 259]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX II</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">The Metres of the Vedas</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">While</span> Greek Prosody makes the ‘foot’ the unit in its metrical systems, and while in later Sanskrit the quantity of every syllable in each line was fixed in all metres Vedic prosody is less formal. The Vedic ‘foot’ or ‘pāda’ like the foot of a quadruped, means a quarterverse because the ordinary Vedic verse contains four lines.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-13-fn-001" href="#sec-13-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-13-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> A pāda may have eight, eleven or twelve syllables. The <em>Ṛich</em> is a stanza generally formed of three or four lines. There are altogether fifteen different metres, but only seven are at all common. As a rule the whole of one hymn is in the same metre throughout, but sometimes different parts of a hymn are in verses of different metres. One hymn in Book I contains verses in nine different metres.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As regards quantity the first syllables of the line are not defined strictly, but generally, though not always, the last four syllables are of fixed length. In the eight and twelve syllable verses these syllables are iambic (short and long) and trochaic (long and short) in lines of eleven syllables.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Max Müller gives a list, according to Śaunaka, of the metres employed in the Rig-veda. The number of verses in which the principal occur are as follows : <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-272" data-page="272" data-folio="260" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n271_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 260">[p. 260]</button> Trishtubh, 4,253 ; Gāyatrī, 2,451 ; Jagatī, 1,348 ; Anushtubh, 855 ; Ushnih, 341 ; Pankti, 312 ; various, 849 ; total, 10,409.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Ancient Sanskrit Literature</em> p. 22.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Examples of one or two of the most common forms will be of interest to the student.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><strong>The Gayatri.</strong>—This is a common metre, one-fourth of the Rig-veda being in this metre. It is so called because the Gāyatrī, the most sacred text in the Vedas, is composed in it. It contains three lines of eight syllables. The first hymn is in this metre. The following is the first verse :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Agnimīle purohitam |</em><br>
<em>Yajñasya devam ṛtvijam |</em><br>
<em>Hotāram ratnadhātamam ||</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Macdonell renders this verse in lines closely resembling the original,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">‘ I praise Agni, domestic priest,<br>
God, minister of sacrifice,<br>
Herald, most prodigal of wealth.’</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><strong>Anushtubh.</strong>—This contains four lines of eight syllables each, like the three lines of the Gāyatrī. It is now generally called the <em>Śloka</em>, and in post Vedic times took the place of the Gāyatrī. The following is an example :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Srushṭivāno hi dāśushe</em><br>
<em>Devāḥ Agne vichetasaḥ |</em><br>
<em>Tān rohidaśva girvaṇas</em><br>
<em>Trayastrimśatam ā vaha |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ Agni, the wise gods lend an ear to their worshipper. God with the ruddy steeds, who lovest praise, bring hither those three and thirty.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Rig-veda</em> i. 45. 2.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-273"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="273" data-folio="261" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n272_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 261">[p. 261]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><strong>Trishtubh.</strong>—About two-fifths of the Rig-veda are composed in the Trishtubh metre. It consists of four lines of eleven syllables. The name means three steps, one short and two long. The following is an example :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Anārambhaṇetad avīrayethām</em><br>
<em>Anāsthāne agrabhaṇe samudre |</em><br>
<em>Yad aśvinā ūhuthur Bhujyum astām</em><br>
<em>Śatāritrām nāvam ātasthivāṁsam |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Ye put forth your vigour in the ocean, which offers no stay, or standing-place, or support, when he bore Bhujyu to his home, standing on a ship propelled by a hundred oars.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> i. 116. 5.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><strong>Jagati.</strong>—This metre of four twelve-syllable lines is said to ‘express the idea of cattle. Any one who wishes for wealth in cattle must use it.’ Example :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Na tam rājānāv adite kutaś chana na</em><br>
<em>Amho aśnoti duritam nakir bhayam |</em><br>
<em>Yam Aśvinā suhavā rudravarttanī</em><br>
<em>Puroratham kṛiṇuthah patnyá saha |</em></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Neither distress, nor calamity, nor fear, O ye two kings, whom none may check or stay, from any quarter assails the man whom ye Asvins, swift to hear, along with (your) wife, cause to lead the van in his car.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> x. 39. 11.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Aryan had a firm belief in the power of the rightly pronounced prayer to secure its aim. This appears in the way in which the gods are addressed in some of the hymns. It also underlies the worship of the goddess Vāch. As the more elaborate system of Brahmanical Hinduism arose a complete science of the mysterious values of the various metres used in the hymns was formulated.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-274"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="274" data-folio="262" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n273_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 262">[p. 262]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In his introduction to that Brāhmaṇas Dr. Haug summarizes the teaching of the Brāhmaṇas on the subject. He points out that the power of the Hotṛi priest at a sacrifice lay in his being able to use the sacred words frequently personified by Vāch. The sacred words, pronounced rightly by the Hotṛi priest, effect, by the innate power of Vāch, the spiritual birth of the sacrificer, shape his body, secure heaven for him, cause him to live a hundred years, and procure him wealth and offspring, will slay his enemies and destroy the consequences of his sins. But pronounced against a man by the priest or his enemies words will curse him unless he finds other words more powerful to counteract the hostile spell. This influence lies mainly in the form or metre in which the given words are uttered; hence the importance of metre, terms and words. Each metre is specially influential in the securing of some particular boon.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Gāyatrī metre is the most sacred, and is the proper metre for Agni, the God of fire, and chaplain of the gods. It expresses the idea of Brahmā : therefore the sacrificer must use it when he wishes anything closely connected with Brahmā, such as acquirement of sacred knowledge, and the thorough understanding of all problems of theology. The Trishtubh expresses the idea of strength and royal power ; thence it is the proper metre by which Indra, the king of the gods, is to be invoked. Any one wishing to obtain strength and royal power, especially a Kshattriya, must use it. A variety of it, the Ushnih metre of twenty-eight syllables, is to be employed by a sacrificer who aspires for longevity, for ‘twenty-eight’ is the symbol of life. The Jagatī expresses the idea of cattle. He who wishes for wealth in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-275" data-page="275" data-folio="263" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n274_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 263">[p. 263]</button> cattle, must use it. The same idea (or that of the sacrifice) is expressed by the Paṅkti metre (five times eight syllables). The Brihat, which consists of thirty-six syllables, is to be used when a sacrificer is aspiring to fame and renown ; for this metre is the exponent of those ideas. The Anushtubh is the symbol of the celestial world ; thence he who seeks a place in heaven should make his prayer in it. The Viraj, of thirty syllables, is food and satisfaction ; thence one who wishes for abundance of food, must employ it.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Thus the Aitareyā Brāhmaṇa says :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ He who wishes for long life, should use two verses in the <em>Ushnih</em> metre ; for Ushnih is life. He who having such a knowledge uses two Ushnihs arrives at his full age (i. e. one hundred years).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ He who desires heaven should use two <em>Anushtubhs</em>. There are sixty-four syllables in two Anushtubhs. Each of these three worlds (earth, air, and sky) contains twenty-one places, one rising above the other (just as the steps of a ladder). By twenty-one steps he ascends to each of these worlds severally ; by taking the sixty-fourth step he stands firm in the celestial world. He who having such a knowledge uses two Anushtubhs gains a footing (in the celestial world).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ He who desires strength should use two Trishtubhs. Trishtubh is strength, vigour, and sharpness of senses. He who knowing this, uses two Trishtubhs, becomes vigorous, endowed with sharp senses and strong.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ He who desires cattle should use two Jagatīs. Cattle are Jagatī-like. He who knowing this uses two Jagatīs, becomes rich in cattle.’</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-13-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-13-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body"><span class="sc">Macdonell</span>, <em>Sanskrit Literature</em>, p. 55. <em>Vedic Index</em>, i. 516.</span></li></ol></section>
