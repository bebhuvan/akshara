<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-276"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="276" data-folio="264" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n275_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 264">[p. 264]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX III</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">Vedic Sanskrit</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Whether</span> the Vedic hymns present to us the language of the early Aryans as they actually used it in their daily speech, or whether it was a refined poetical dialect even in early days, as was classical Sanskrit assuredly in later times, there is not sufficient evidence to decide. Probably neither assertion is altogether true. Some of the hymns are nearer to the actual life of the people than others, more speculative, can be. In some there are what seem to be thoroughly colloquial expressions. Others are the carefully expressed utterances of thinkers who have followed generations of thinkers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On one point however, there can be no doubt. Generally speaking the language of the Vedas represents a stage in which Sanskrit is still very like the language of the old Persian Avesta, a stage in which the Aryans of Iran and the Aryans of the Panjab have still much in common in their speech. And the language of the Indian Aryans is simpler, more direct, less trammelled by grammatical conventions than the Sanskrit of later days.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The subject has been carefully investigated by Sanskrit grammarians, both European and Indians. The greatest of Indian Sanskrit grammarians Pāṇini constantly recognizes differences between the ancient and the more modern dialects.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-277"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="277" data-folio="265" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/rigvedavedicreli00clayiala/page/n276_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 265">[p. 265]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Muir<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-14-fn-001" href="#sec-14-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-14-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> gives the following among other examples of the differences of Vedic from later Sanskrit :</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Vedic Text</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Vāyav āyāhi darśata ime somāḥ arankṛitāḥ</em> | <em>teshām</em><br>
<em>pāhi śrudhi havam</em>‖</div></div><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Modern Sanskrit</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><em>Vāyav āyāhi darśanīya ime somāḥ arankṛitāḥ—</em><br>
<em>teshām piba śriṇu havam</em>‖</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘Come, O Vayu, these somas are prepared. Drink of them ; hear our invocation.’ <em>Rig-veda</em> i. 2. 1.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Here it will be observed that four Vedic words darśata, arankṛitāḥ, pāhi, śrudhi, differ from the modern Sanskrit forms.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The student will also find it useful to consult P. Peterson’s <em>Hymns from the Rig-veda</em>, Bombay, 1900.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-14-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-14-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body"><span class="sc">MUIR</span>, <em>Sanskrit Texts</em>, vol. ii, p. 205.</span></li></ol></section>
