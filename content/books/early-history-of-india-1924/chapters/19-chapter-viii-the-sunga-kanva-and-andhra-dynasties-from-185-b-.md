<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-244"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="244" data-folio="208" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n243_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 208">[p. 208]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">THE SUNGA, KANVA, AND ĀNDHRA DYNASTIES, 185 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> TO <span class="sc">a. d.</span> <em>c.</em> 225.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>The Sunga Dynasty.</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 185 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> Usurpation of Pushyamitra. Sunga.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Pushyamitra</span>, the commander-in-chief, having slain his master Brihadratha Maurya, usurped the vacant throne, and established himself as sovereign of the now contracted Maurya dominions;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-001" href="#sec-19-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> thus founding a dynasty known to history as that of the Sungas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-002" href="#sec-19-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-245"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="245" data-folio="209" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n244_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 209">[p. 209]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Extent of Sunga dominions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The capital presumably continued to be, as of old, Pātaliputra, and probably all the central or home provinces of the empire recognized the usurper’s authority, which perhaps extended to the south as far as the Narmadā river,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-003" href="#sec-19-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and may be assumed to have embraced the territories in the Gangetic basin, corresponding with the modern Bihār, Tirhūt, and the United Provinces of Agra and Oudh. It is unlikely that either the later Mauryas or the Sungas exercised any jurisdiction in the Panjāb. Wilson’s belief that the arms of Pushyamitra reached the Indus was due to a misunderstanding.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-004" href="#sec-19-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 165 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> First invasion of Khāravela.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Pushyamitra did not enjoy his dominions unchallenged. In or about 165 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Khāravela, King of Kalinga, who was descended from the Cheta (Chaitra) family, invaded his territory and advanced to within a few miles of Pātaliputra. Pushyamitra made a strategic withdrawal to Mathurā, and Khāravela apparently considered it wise at the moment not to proceed farther than the Barābar Hills (Gorathagiri).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 161 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> Second invasion of Khāravela.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The second invasion of Khāravela, four years later, was, however, more successful. Entering Northern India and marching at the foot of the Himalayas, he suddenly appeared before the capital of Magadha on the north side of the Ganges, which he crossed with the help of the famous elephants of Kalinga. Pushyamitra was forced to submit, and the treasures of his capital were seized by the victor, among them being a statue of the first Jina (Rishabhadeva), which had been carried away from Kalinga three centuries earlier by King Nanda I.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-005" href="#sec-19-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-246"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="246" data-folio="210" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n245_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 210">[p. 210]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 155–3 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Invasion and repulse of Menander.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the latter years of his reign, Pushyamitra was threatened by serious danger. Menander, a relative of the Bactrian monarch Eukratides, and king of Kābul and the Panjāb, having formed the design of emulating the exploits of Alexander, advanced with a formidable force into the interior of India. He annexed the Indus delta, the peninsula of Surāshtra (Kāthiāwār), and some other territories on the western coast; occupied Mathurā on the Jumna; besieged Madhyamikā (now Nagarī near Chitōr) in Rājputāna; invested Sākētam in Southern Oudh; and threatened Pātaliputra, the capital.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The invasion was repelled after a severe struggle, and the Greek king was obliged to retire to his own country, but he may have retained his conquests in Western India for a few years longer.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-006" href="#sec-19-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">India and Europe.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Thus ended the second and last attempt by a European general to conquer India by land. All subsequent invaders from the western continent have come in ships, trusting to their command of the sea, and using it as their base. From the repulse of Menander in or about 153 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> until the bombardment of Calicut by Vasco da Gama in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1502 India enjoyed immunity from attack under European leadership; and so long as the power in occupation of the country retains command of the sea, no attack made from the land side in the footsteps of the ancient invaders can have any prospect of permanent success.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Agnimitra’s war with Vidarbha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the progress of the war with Menander, the outlying southern provinces extending to the Narmadā river were administered by the Crown Prince, Agnimitra, as viceroy, who had his capital at Vidisā, the modern Bhīlsā on the Betwā in Sindhia’s territory. Agnimitra’s youthful son, Vasumitra, was employed on active service under the orders of the king, his grandfather. Pushyamitra, who at this time must have been advanced in years, resolved to crown his military successes by substantiating and proclaiming <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-247" data-page="247" data-folio="211" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n246_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 211">[p. 211]</button> a formal claim to the rank of Lord Paramount of Northern India. His pretensions received confirmation by the success of Agnimitra in a local war with his southern neighbour, the Rāja of Vidarbha (Berār), which resulted in the complete defeat of the Rāja, who was obliged to cede half of his dominions to a rival cousin; the river Varadā (Warda) being constituted the boundary between the two principalities.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Pushyamitra determined to revive and celebrate with appropriate magnificence the antique Vedic rite of the horse-sacrifice (<em>aœvamedha</em>), which, according to immemorial tradition, could only be performed by a paramount sovereign, and involved as a preliminary a formal and successful challenge to all rival claimants to supreme power, delivered after this fashion:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>Aœvamedha</em>, or horse-sacrifice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">‘A horse of a particular colour was consecrated by the performance of certain ceremonies, and was then turned loose to wander for a year. The king, or his representative, followed the horse with an army, and when the animal entered a foreign country, the ruler of that country was bound either to fight or to submit. If the liberator of the horse succeeded in obtaining or enforcing the submission of all the countries over which it passed, he returned in triumph with all the vanquished Rājas in his train; but, if he failed, he was disgraced, and his pretensions ridiculed. After his successful return, a great festival was held, at which the horse was sacrificed.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-007" href="#sec-19-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Yāvanas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The command, at least nominally, of the guard attendant on the consecrated steed liberated by Pushyamitra was entrusted to his young grandson, Vasumitra, who is said to have encountered and routed a band of certain Yavanas, or western foreigners, who took up the challenge on the banks of the river Sindhu, which now forms the boundary between Bundēlkhand and the Rājputāna states.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-008" href="#sec-19-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> These disputants may have been part of the division of Menander’s army which had undertaken the siege of Madhyamikā in Rājputāna.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-248"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="248" data-folio="212" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n247_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 212">[p. 212]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Celebration of the sacrifice.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Yavanas and all other rivals having been disposed of in due course, Pushyamitra was justified in his claim to rank as the paramount power of Northern India, and straightway proceeded to announce his success by a magnificent celebration of the sacrifice at his capital. The dramatist, who has so well preserved the traditions of the time, professes to record the very words of the invitation addressed by the victorious king to his son the Crown Prince, as follows:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘May it be well with thee! From the sacrificial enclosure the commander-in-chief Pushpamitra sends this message to his son Agnimitra, who is in the territory of Vidisā, affectionately embracing him. Be it known unto thee that I, having been consecrated for the <em>Rājasūya</em> sacrifice,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-009" href="#sec-19-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> let loose free from all check or curb a horse which was to be brought back after a year, appointing Vasumitra as its defender, girt with a guard of a hundred Rājpūts. This very horse wandering on the right [or “south”] bank of the Sindhu was claimed by a cavalry squadron of the Yavanas. Then there was a fierce struggle between the two forces. Then Vasumitra, the mighty bowman, having overcome his foes, rescued by force my excellent horse, which they were endeavouring to carry off. Accordingly, I will now sacrifice, having had my horse brought back to me by my grandson, even as Ansumat brought back the horse to Sagara. Therefore, you must dismiss anger from your mind, and without delay come with my daughters-in-law to behold the sacrifice.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-010" href="#sec-19-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Patañjali.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The performance of the solemn rite probably was witnessed by the celebrated grammarian Patañjali, who alludes <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-249" data-page="249" data-folio="213" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n248_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 213">[p. 213]</button> to the event in terms which imply that it occurred in his time.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Beginning of Brahmanical reaction.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The exaggerated regard for the sanctity of animal life, which was one of the most cherished features of Buddhism, and the motive of Asoka’s most characteristic legislation, had necessarily involved the prohibition of bloody sacrifices, which are essential to certain forms of Brahmanical worship, and were believed by the orthodox to possess the highest saving efficacy. The memorable horse-sacrifice of Pushyamitra marked an early stage in the Brahmanical reaction, which was fully developed five centuries later in the time of Samudragupta and his successors.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Pushyamitra alleged to be a persecutor.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">If credit may be given to the semi-mythological stories of Buddhist writers, Pushyamitra was not content with the peaceful revival of Hindu rites, but indulged in a savage persecution of Buddhism, burning monasteries and slaying monks from Magadha to Jālandhar, in the Panjāb. Many monks who escaped his sword are said to have fled into the territories of other rulers. It would be rash to reject this tale as wholely baseless, although it may be exaggerated.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-011" href="#sec-19-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Persecutions in India.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although the alleged proscription of Buddhism by Pushyamitra is supported by some evidence, it is true that the gradual extinction of that religion in India was due in the main to causes other than persecution; while it is also true that from time to time fanatic kings indulged in savage outbursts of cruelty, and committed genuine acts of persecution directed against Jains or Buddhists as such. Well-established instances of such proceedings will be met with in the course of this history, and others, which do not come within its limits, are on record. That such outbreaks of wrath should have occurred is not wonderful, if we consider the extreme oppressiveness of the Jain and Buddhist prohibitions when ruthlessly enforced, as they certainly were by some Rājas, and presumably by Asoka. The wonder rather is that persecutions were so rare, and that as a rule the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-250" data-page="250" data-folio="214" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n249_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 214">[p. 214]</button> various sects managed to live together in harmony, and in the enjoyment of fairly impartial official favour.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-012" href="#sec-19-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 149 <span class="sc">B.C.</span> The later Śungas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When Pushyamitra, some five years subsequent to the retreat of Menander, died, after a long and eventful reign, he was succeeded by his son the Crown Prince, Agnimitra, who had governed the southern provinces during his father’s lifetime. He reigned but a few years, and was succeeded by Vasujyeshtha, or Sujyeshtha, probably a brother, who was followed seven years later by Vasumitra, presumably that son of Agnimitra who as a youth had guarded the sacrificial horse on behalf of his aged grandfather. The next four reigns are said to have been abnormally short, amounting together to only seventeen years. The inference that the extreme brevity of these reigns indicates a period of confusion during which palace revolutions were frequent is confirmed by the one incident of the time which has survived in tradition. Sumitra, another son of Agnimitra, who was, we are told, inordinately devoted to the stage, was surprised when in the midst of his favourite actors by one Mitradeva, who ‘severed his head with a scimitar, as a lotus is shorn from its stalk’.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-013" href="#sec-19-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The ninth king, Bhāgavata, is credited with a long reign of thirty-two years, but we know little about him.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-014" href="#sec-19-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The tenth king, Devabhūti or Devabhūmi, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-251" data-page="251" data-folio="215" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n250_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 215">[p. 215]</button> was, we are assured, a man of licentious habits, and lost his life while engaged in a discreditable intrigue. The dynasty thus came to an unhonoured end after having occupied the throne for a hundred and twelve years.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-015" href="#sec-19-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><em>The Kāṇva or Kāṇvāyana Dynasty.</em></h2><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 73 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> Vasudeva Kāṇva.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The plot which cost the royal debauchee, Devabhūti, his throne and life was contrived by his Brahman minister Vasudeva, who seems to have controlled the state even during the lifetime of his nominal master.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-016" href="#sec-19-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Mitradeva, the slayer of Prince Sumitra, probably belonged to the same powerful family, which is known to history as that of the Kānvas, or Kānvāyanas. The distinct testimony of both the Purānas and Bāna that Devabhūti, the tenth and last Sunga, was the person slain by Vasudeva, the first Kānva, forbids the acceptance of Professor Bhandarkar’s theory that the Kānva dynasty should be regarded as contemporary with the Sunga.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-017" href="#sec-19-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-252"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="252" data-folio="216" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n251_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 216">[p. 216]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 63–28 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> The later Kāṇvas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Vasudeva seized the throne rendered vacant by his crime, and was succeeded by three of his descendants. The whole dynasty, comprising four reigns, covers a period of only forty-five years.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-018" href="#sec-19-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The figures indicate, as in the case of the Sungas, that the times were disturbed, and that succession to the throne was often effected by violent means. Nothing whatever is known about the reigns of any of the Kāṇva kings.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-019" href="#sec-19-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The last of them was slain about 28 or 27 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> by a king of the Āndhra or Sātavāhana dynasty, which at that time possessed wide dominions stretching across the table-land of the Deccan from sea to sea. Although no coins or monuments connecting the Āndhra kings with Pātaliputra, the ancient imperial capital, have yet been discovered, it is possible that they may have controlled the kingdom of Magadha for a time. The most ancient coins of the dynasty at present known are of northern type, and bear the name of Sāta, probably Sātakarni, the sixth king in the Purānic list, who was reigning about 150 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> The Āndhra coinage from first to last has many obvious affinities with the mintages of the north, which may be explained by the hypothesis that the dynasty really held Magadha as a dependency for a considerable period. But there is little evidence to support such a conjecture.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-020" href="#sec-19-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Identity of the Āndhra king.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Purānas treat the whole Āndhra dynasty as following the Kānva, and consequently identify the slayer of the last Kānva prince with Simuka or Sipraka, the first of the Āndhra line. But, as a matter of fact, the independent <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-253" data-page="253" data-folio="217" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n252_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 217">[p. 217]</button> Āndhra dynasty must have begun about 240 or 230 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-021" href="#sec-19-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> long before the suppression of the Kāṇvas about 28 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, and the Āndhra king who slew Susarman cannot possibly have been Simuka. It is impossible to affirm with certainty who he was, because the dates of accession of the several Āndhra princes are not known with accuracy. All that can be affirmed at present is that the slayer of Susarman, the last Kāṇva, apparently must have been one or other of three Āndhra kings, namely Nos. 11, 12, or 13. The year 28 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> may be accepted as the approximately true date of the extinction of the Kāṇva dynasty; because it depends, not on the duration assigned to each several Āndhra reign, but on the periods of 112 and 45 years respectively allotted to the Sunga and Kāṇva dynasties, which seem worthy of credence; and this date, 28 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, apparently must fall within the limits of one or other of the three Āndhra reigns named above.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-022" href="#sec-19-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><em>The Āndhra or Andhra Dynasty.</em></h2><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Earliest mention of the Āndhras.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Before proceeding to narrate the history of the Āndhra kings after the extinction of the Kāṇva dynasty we must cast back a glance to the more distant past, and trace the steps by which the Āndhra kingdom became one of the greatest powers in India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote is-locus">300 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the days of Chandragupta Maurya and Megasthenes, ths Āndhra nation, a Dravidian people, mentioned in the <em>Aitareya Brahmana</em> of very early date, and now represented by the large population speaking the Telugu language, occupied the deltas of the Godāvarī and Krishnā (Kistna) rivers on the eastern side of India, and was reputed to possess a military force second only to that at the command of the king of the Prasii, Chandragupta Maurya. The Andhra territory included thirty walled towns, besides <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-254" data-page="254" data-folio="218" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n253_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 218">[p. 218]</button> numerous villages, and the army consisted of 100,000 infantry, 2,000 cavalry, and 1,000 elephants.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-023" href="#sec-19-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The capital of the state is believed to have been then Srī Kākulam, on the lower course of the Krishnā.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-024" href="#sec-19-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The nation thus described evidently was independent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">256 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Āndhras tributary to Asoka.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When next mentioned in Asoka’s edicts (256 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>) they were reckoned among the tribes and nations resident in or adjoining the outer circle of the empire, and perhaps subject to the imperial command, although doubtless enjoying a considerable degree of autonomy under their own Rāja.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-025" href="#sec-19-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The withdrawal of the strong arm of Asoka was the signal for the disruption of his vast empire. While the home provinces continued to obey his feeble successors upon the throne of Pātaliputra, the distant governments, including Kalinga, which he had taken so much trouble to annex, shook off the imperial yoke and reasserted their independence.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 240 or 230 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Kings Simuka and Krishṇa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Āndhras were not slow to take advantage of the opportunity given by the death of the great emperor, and very soon after the close of his reign, or possibly even before its close, to assert their independent power under the government of a king named Simuka. The new dynasty extended its sway with such extraordinary rapidity that, in the reign of the second king, Krishna (Kanha), the town of Nāsik, near the source of the Godāvarī in the Western <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-255" data-page="255" data-folio="219" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n254_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 219">[p. 219]</button> Ghāts, was included in the Āndhra dominions, which thus stretched across India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 171 <span class="sc">b.c.</span> Khāravela.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The third king, Srī Sātakarṇi, who is described as Lord of the West, was defied by Khāravela, king of Kalinga in the east, which kingdom also had recovered its independence after the death of Asoka.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-026" href="#sec-19-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 28 or 27 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Extinction of Kāṇva dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Nothing more is heard of the Āndhra kings until one of them, as above related, in or about 28 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, slew the last of the Kāṇvas, and no doubt annexed the territory, whatever it may have been, which still recognized the authority of that dynasty. The Āndhra kings all claimed to belong to the Sātavāhana family, and many of them assumed the title or bore the name of Sātakarṇi. They are consequently often referred to by one or other of these designations, without mention of the personal name of the monarch, and it is thus sometimes impossible to ascertain which king is alluded to. As already observed, the real name of the slayer of Susarman Kāṇva is not known.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-256"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="256" data-folio="220" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n255_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 220">[p. 220]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">King Hāla and Prākrit literature.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The name of Hāla, the seventeenth king, by virtue of its association with literary tradition, possesses special interest. The anthology of erotic verses, written in the ancient dialect of Mahārāshtra, and entitled <em>Saptaśataka</em>, or ‘Seven Centuries’, professes to be the composition of Hāla, and is ascribed by tradition to Sālivāhana, another form of Sātavāhana. Prof. Sir R. G. Bhandarkar therefore has suggested that probably either king Hāla may have been the author of the work, or it may have been dedicated to him.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-027" href="#sec-19-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Other traditions also associate literature written in Prākrit with kings of the Āndhra dynasty. In their time and territory Sanskrit, apparently, was not in ordinary use as the language of polite literature.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Wars between Āndhras and foreigners.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the reigns of kings No. 23, Rāja Gautamīputra Srī Sātakarni, and No. 24, Rāja Vāsishtīputra Srī Pulumāyi, the Āndhras engaged in conflicts with the foreign tribes which had formed settlements and carved out kingdoms in Western India, subordinate apparently at first to the Indo-Parthian and subsequently to the Kushān sovereigns. Such conflicts between indigenous Rājas and alien chiefs frequently recur in the history of ancient India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Satrap Bhūmaka Kshaharāta.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The story of the foreign settlements in the regions now mostly included in the Bombay Presidency is fragmentary and obscure, but can be made out to some extent from study of coins and inscriptions. The earliest foreign ruler in the west whose name has been preserved was the Satrap Bhūmaka Kshaharāta, who struck coins with Parthian affinities, and may be presumed to have been subordinate to one or other of the Indo-Parthian kings, perhaps Gondophares. His exact date is not known, but he may be assigned approximately to the early years of the first century after Christ, and may have had predecessors. The Kshaharātas were connected with the Sakas, and may have immigrated from Sakastēnē, the modern Sīstān.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-257"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="257" data-folio="221" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n256_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 221">[p. 221]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Great Satrap Nahapāna Kshaharāta.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The next recorded Kshaharāta chief is Nahapāna, who may or may not have been the immediate successor of Bhūmaka, and may be assigned approximately to the middle of the first century after Christ, or possibly earlier. His name indicates Persian origin. At first he held the rank of Satrap, like Bhūmaka, but subsequently assumed the higher style of Great Satrap (<em>mahākshatrapa</em>), and was also known by the Indian title of Rāja. His dominions comprised a large area, extending from Southern Rājputāna as far northward as Ajmēr and Pushkar, to the Nāsik and Poona Districts in the Western Ghāts, and including the peninsula of Surāshtra or Kāthiāwār. His titles of Satrap and Great Satrap indicate subordination to a northern power, which can only have been that of the Kushāns.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-028" href="#sec-19-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Extirpation of Kshaharātas by Gautamīputra Śātakarṇi.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Āndhra king, No. 23, Rāja Gautamīputra Srī Sātakarṇi, who may be assumed to have come to the throne about the beginning of the second century <span class="sc">a. d.</span>, succeeded in extirpating the Kshaharāta dynasty and annexing their dominions about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 119. He signalized his victory by calling in the money issued by the vanquished princes during many years, restamping it in a crude fashion with his own insignia. At the height of his power he was master of the whole country watered by the Godāvarī, Berar, Mālwā, Kāthiāwār, Gujarāt, and the north Konkan.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-029" href="#sec-19-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> He posed as the champion of the Hindu religions, including both Brahmanical Hinduism and Buddhism, as against the creeds of casteless foreigners, Sakas, Pahlavas, and others, and prided himself on having re-established the practice of caste rules. He thus ‘restored the glory of the Sātavāhana race’, and was in a position to gratify his Hindu sentiment <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-258" data-page="258" data-folio="222" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n257_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 222">[p. 222]</button> by liberal donations to both Brahmans and Buddhists. It is a curious fact that, although the Āndhra kings clearly were officially Brahmanical Hindus, most of their recorded donations were made to Buddhist institutions.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Pulumāyi by Rudradāman I.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">About <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 128 Rāja Gautamīputra Srī Sātakarṇi was succeeded by his son, Rāja Vāsishṭhīputra Srī Pulumāyi, who had been his colleague,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-030" href="#sec-19-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and reigned for some thirty years. He was married to a daughter of Rudradāman I, the Saka Great Satrap of Ujjain, but the matrimonial connexion did not prevent the Great Satrap from twice defeating his son-in-law and taking from him most of the territory which Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi had won from the Kshaharātas. The relationship, however, so far influenced the victor that he did not proceed to extremities, as he would have done to a stranger. The aggrandizement of Rudradāman I must have been largely completed before <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 130, by which date he was in possession of Cutch, and certainly before <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 150, because we know that shortly after that date he placed on record a list of the numerous regions in Western India which owned his sway.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Careers of Rudradāman and Chashtana.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rudradāman I, a learned and accomplished prince, who thus raised his house to the position of the leading power in the West, was the grandson of the great Satrap Chashtana, whose coins in silver and copper, inscribed with Greek, Brahmī, and Kharoshṭhī legends, are found in Gujarāt. The events of Chashtana’s reign are not on record, but his approximate date is easily ascertained from the facts that his grandson is known to have been reigning in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 130 and 150. Chashtana, therefore, may be placed in the period from about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 80 to 110. These dates imply that Chashtana held his office as Great Satrap under the Kushān dynasty, that is to say, under Kadphises II, according to my chronology.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-031" href="#sec-19-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The Saka satraps of Surāshtra and Mālwā <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-259" data-page="259" data-folio="223" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n258_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 223">[p. 223]</button> naturally followed the examples of their Kushān sovereigns by using the Saka era, then newly established. The abundance of dated coins and inscriptions permits of no doubt as to the outlines of the chronology of the dynasty founded by Chashtana, the history of which will be further noticed in connexion with the Gupta kings.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Yajña Srī, <span class="sc">c. A.D.</span> 166 to 196.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After the death of Vāsishtīputra Pulumāyi about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 156, the only notable Āndhra monarch was Gautamīputra Yajna Srī, who reigned for twenty-nine years, from about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 166. His rare silver coins, imitating the satrap coinage, certainly prove a renewal of relations with the western satraps, and probably point to unrecorded conquests. It would seem that Yajna Srī must have renewed the struggle in which Pulumāyi II had been worsted, and that he recovered some of the provinces lost by that prince. The silver coins would then have been struck for circulation in the conquered western districts, just as similar coins were minted by Chandra-gupta Vikramāditya when he finally shattered the power of the Saka satraps. The numerous and varied, although rude, bronze and leaden coins of Yajna Srī, which formed the currency of the eastern provinces, confirm the testimony of inscriptions by which the prolonged duration of his reign is attested.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-032" href="#sec-19-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Some pieces bearing the figure of a ship probably should be referred to this reign, and suggest the inference that Yajna Srī’s power was not confined to the land.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The last three kings.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">His successors, apparently, in the eastern provinces, named Vijaya, Chandra Srī, and Pulumāyi IV, with whom <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-260" data-page="260" data-folio="224" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n259_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 224">[p. 224]</button> the long series of Āndhra kings came to an end about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 225, are mere names; but the real existence of Chandra Srī is attested by the discovery of a few leaden coins bearing his name.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-033" href="#sec-19-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Research probably will detect coins struck by both his next predecessor and immediate successor.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Duration of the dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The testimony of the Purānas that the dynasty endured for either 456 or 460 years, or, in round numbers, four centuries and a half, appears to be substantially accurate. The number of the kings also appears to be correctly stated as having been thirty. The following dynastic list has been constructed on the assumption that the best texts of the Purānas are right in fixing the number of kings as thirty, and therefore omits an extra king, No. 24 <em>a</em>, who appears only in a single manuscript of the <em>Vāyu Purāṇa</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-034" href="#sec-19-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Western India under the Āndhras.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Professor D. R. Bhandarkar has given a description, based upon the rock and cave inscriptions of Western India, of the social and economic features of the Deccan during the sway of the Āndhras.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-035" href="#sec-19-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Both Buddhism and Brahmanic Hinduism flourished at that period. Almost all the Buddhist caves in the Deccan were excavated under the rule of this dynasty, villages and lands being granted to defray the cost of their maintenance and to provide also for the sustenance of the <em>Bhikshus</em> who inhabited the caves during the rainy season. The provision of new robes for these mendicants was secured by the investment of funds in one of the craft-guilds (<em>sreni</em>), established in the neighbouring towns. The popularity of Brahmanism is apparent from the fact that Royalty performed many sacrifices, including the <em>Aśvamedha</em> and <em>Gavāmayana</em>, and paid heavy <em>dakshina</em> to Brahmans. The worship of Siva was popular, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-261" data-page="261" data-folio="225" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n260_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 225">[p. 225]</button> and also the cult of Krishna under the names Samkarshana and Vāsudeva, while Indra and Dharma were widely revered. There seems to have been little or no antagonism between the two faiths, for the followers of Brahmanism excavated several caves for Buddhist monks, while foreigners like the Sakas and Ābhiras freely embraced either religion and assumed Hindu names.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Society was officially divided into at least four classes, the highest class being composed of the Mahārathis, the Mahābhojas, and the Mahāsenāpatīs, who were feudatory chieftains in charge of <em>rashtras</em> or districts, the Mahābhojas being located in the north Konkan, and the Mahārathis in the country above the Western Ghāts. The second class comprised both officials and non-officials, among the former being the Amātyas and Mahāmātras, and the Bhāndāgārikas, who were in charge of the treasuries; while the latter included the <em>Naigama</em> (merchant), the <em>Sārthavāha</em> (head of a caravan of traders), and the <em>Sresthin</em> (head of a tradeguild). In the third class were the <em>Lekhaka</em> (scribe), <em>Vaidya</em> (physician), <em>Hālakīya</em> (cultivator), <em>Suvarnakāra</em> (goldsmith) and <em>Gāndhika</em> (druggist). The fourth class included the <em>Vardhaki</em> (carpenter), <em>Mālākāra</em> (gardener), <em>Lohavanija</em> (blacksmith), and <em>Dāsaka</em> (fisherman). The mercantile and cultivating classes were apparently subdivided into various <em>grihas</em> (homesteads) or <em>kutumbas</em> or <em>kulas</em> (families), the head of each of which was considered of sufficient importance to be designated respectively <em>Grihapati</em> or <em>Kutumbin</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The currency of the country consisted of <em>kārshāpanas</em>, which were both silver and copper; <em>suvarnas</em>, the gold coins of the Kushān kings, one of which was equal in value to 35 silver <em>kārshāpanas</em>; and <em>kusanas</em>, a silver coinage probably introduced by Nahapāna or another Saka ruler, eight of which were equivalent to nine silver <em>kārshāpanas</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Craft-guilds were a feature of the age. At Govardhan, near Nāsik, there were guilds of oil-pressers, hydraulic machine-artisans, potters, and weavers; while at Junnar there were similar guilds of corn-dealers, bamboo-workers, and braziers. These guilds acted as banks, in which money <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-262" data-page="262" data-folio="226" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n261_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 226">[p. 226]</button> could be deposited at interest, such deposits being always made in the indigenous currency (<em>kārshāpanas</em>). Permanent endowments, such as those for religious purposes, were publicly proclaimed and registered in the records of a <em>nigama-sabha</em> or town-assembly.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The country profited from a flourishing foreign trade. Ships from the West sailed down the Red Sea to Broach and the Malabar coast, which supplied the two great inland marts of Paithan and Tagara, and probably visited the two harbours of Sopārā and Kalyān. The latter port was raised to the rank of a regular mart in the time of the elder Sarganes (probably Sātākarni, the third ruler of the dynasty); but its trade was subsequently restricted to narrow limits by Sandanes, who may have been a Saka official. The Saka Satraps certainly endeavoured to divert the trade from Broach direct through their own dominions in the northern Deccan. Other seaports supplying the Āndhra dominions were Semulla (Chaul), Mandagora (? Mandangad to the south of Bānkot), Palaipatmai, Melizeigara (? Jaygad or Janjira) and Buzantion.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The last of the Āndhras.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At present nothing is known concerning the causes which brought about the downfall of this dynasty, which had succeeded in retaining power for a period so unusually prolonged. It would seem that Yajna Srī was the last king to retain control of both the eastern and western provinces.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-036" href="#sec-19-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Scions of the Sātavāhana race appear to have established minor kingdoms in different parts of the Deccan. But the third century after Christ is one of the dark spaces in the spectrum of Indian history, and almost every event of that time is concealed from view by an impenetrable veil of oblivion. Vague speculation, unchecked by the salutary limitations of verified fact, is, at the best, unprofitable; and so we must be content to let the Āndhras pass away in the darkness. The Purānas present confused and corrupt <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-263" data-page="263" data-folio="227" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n262_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 227">[p. 227]</button> lists of numerous local dynasties, including Yavanas and Sakas, obviously foreigners, as having succeeded the Āndhras, which it is impossible to arrange in any intelligible fashion.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-19-fn-037" href="#sec-19-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-19-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-19-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-19-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">The Puranic account of Pushyamitra’s usurpation is confirmed by Bāṇa (seventh century), who evidently had access to documents now lost. His text is: <em>Pratijñā durbalam cha baladarśanavyapa</em>-<em>deśa-darśitāśesha-sainyaḥ senānīr anāryo Mauryam Brihadratham pipesha Pushpamitraḥ svāminam</em>, which may be translated: ‘And reviewing the whole army, under the pretext of showing him his forces, the base-born (<em>anārya</em>) general Pushpamitra crushed his master, Bṛihadratha the Maurya, who, was weak in keeping his coronation oath (<em>pratijñā</em>).’ The rendering combines the versions of Cowell and Thomas (<em>Harṣacarita</em>, transl. p. 193), of Bühler (<em>Ind. Ant.</em>, ii, 363), and of Jayaswal. The best text of the Purāṇas (Pargiter, pp. 31, 70) states simply that ‘Pushyamitra, the commander-in-chief, will uproot Bṛihadratha and will rule the kingdom as king 36 years’.</span></li><li id="sec-19-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-19-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">2.</a> <span class="fn-body">Manuscripts usually read Pushpamitra, but Pushyamitra is the correct form (Bühler, <em>Ind. Ant.</em>, ii, 362). Pushyamitra has been shown to be a synonym of Bahasatimitra or Bahaspati of the Khāravela inscription (K. P. Jayaswal, in <em>J. B. &amp; O. Res. Soc.</em>, Part iv, Dec. 1917, pp. 473–80); and in view of the connexion between Brihaspati and the Pushya asterism, we must accept Pushya as correct. As the name of the founder of the dynasty and some of his descendants ended in <em>mitra</em>, M. M. Haraparshād Sāstri suggests, in my opinion wrongly, that the Sungas were Persians, worshippers of the sun (Mithra).
