<span id="page-265" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX J</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>The Āndhras and connected Dynasties</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The inscriptions and coins of both the Āndhras and the connected dynasties are fully discussed in Rapson, <em>Catalogue of the Coins of the Āndhra Dynasty, &amp;c.</em>, B. M., 1908, but the inscriptions <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-266" data-page="266" data-folio="230" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n265_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 230">[p. 230]</button> are most conveniently cited by the numbers in Prof. H. Lüders’s excellent work, ‘A List of Brahmī Inscriptions from the earliest times to about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 400’, published as an Appendix to <em>Epigraphia Indica</em>, vol. x, 1910. Mr. F. E. Pargiter’s book, entitled <em>The Purāṇa Text of the Dynasties of the Kali Age</em>, Oxford, 1913, gives the Purāṇic lists in their most authentic form, with a full apparatus of variants. Mr. R. D. Banerji’s essay, entitled ‘The Scythian Period of Indian History’, in <em>Indian Antiquary</em>, 1908, includes certain valuable hints on the history of Nahapāna, &amp;c., which have helped to guide my judgement. The paper on the ‘Nasik Hoard of Nahapāna’s and Satakarni’s Coins’, with four plates, by the Rev. H. R. Scott, reprinted from the <em>J. Br. Br. R. A. S.</em>, 1907, supplies full details of the important Jogaltembhi hoard.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I have also considered Mr. V. Gopala Aiyar’s paper, ‘The Saka and Samvat Eras,’ in the <em>Journal of the South Indian Association</em>, April 1911, vol. i, pp. 425–49.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">With reference to those authorities I now proceed to give concisely in this Appendix, in lieu of footnotes, the reasons for the presentation of the history offered in this edition.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">References to inscriptions apparently mentioning the Āndhra kings named in the Purāṇic list, as indicated by serial numbers, are:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">King No. 1—Lüders, No. 1113; No. 2—346, 1144; No. 3— 1144, 1345; No. 23—1123, 1124, 1125; No. 24—1100, 1106, 1122, 1123, 1124, 1248; No. 25?—1279; No. 27—987, 1024, 1146, 1340; No. 29—1341. Doubtful identity—1112, 1120, 1202, 1203, 1204. Coins exist attributable, in some cases with doubt, to Nos. 6, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 29, in the Āndhra list.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Kshaharāta inscriptions are 1099, 1125, 1131, 1132, 1133, 1134, 1135, 1174. Coins exist of Bhūmaka and Nahapāna, and certain coins of Gautamīputra (Āndhra king, No. 23), are restruck on those of Nahapāna.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The list of Āndhra kings is taken from Pargiter, pp. 38–43, 71. The Purāṇas give the name of the first king as Śiśuka (<em>Mt.</em>), Sindhuka (<em>Vā</em>, <em>Bḍ</em>), or Śipraka (<em>Vṣ</em>), and state that ‘the Āndhra S., with his fellow tribesmen, the servants of Suśarman, will assail the Kāṇvāyanas and him (Suśarman), and destroy the remains of the Śuṅgas’ power and will obtain this earth’. He is identified with Simuka of the Nānāghāṭ inscription, No. 1113, incised in script of about 200 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Kṛishṇa, king No. 2, clearly is Kaṇha of Nānāghāṭ record No. 1144; and king No. 3, Śrī Sātakarṇi or Mallakarṇi, must be the monarch mentioned in Khāravēla’s inscription No. 346, and the Nānāghāṭ epigraph, No. 1114.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Hardly anything else being known about the first eighteen kings, it will suffice to enumerate their names, with the length of their reigns, as stated in Mr. Pargiter’s list. They are (1) Śiśuka, &amp;c., of MSS., Simuka of inscription, 23 years; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-267" data-page="267" data-folio="231" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n266_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 231">[p. 231]</button> (2) Kṛishṇa, his brother, 10; (3) Sātakarṇi or Mallakarṇi, son of (2), 10; (4) Pūrṇotsaṅga, 18; (5) Skandastambhi, 18; (6) Sātakarṇi, 56; (7) Lambodara, 18; (8) Āpīlaka, 12; (9) Meghasvāti, 18; (10) Svāti, 18; (11) Skandasvāti, 7; (12) Mṛigendra Svātikarṇa, 3; (13) Kuntala Svātikarṇa, 8; (14) Svātikarṇa, 1; (15) Pulomāvi [I], 36; (16) Arishṭakarṇa, 25; (17) Hāla, 5; (18) Mantalaka, 5.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The remaining twelve kings are exhibited in the annexed synchronistic table.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">No doubt seems possible as to the identity of Gautamīputra, king No. 23, with <em>Śrī Sātakarṇi Gautamīputra</em>, or <em>Rāja Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi</em> of the inscriptions, who is known to have reigned at least twenty-four years, and was the father of No. 24, Pulomā[vi II]. That king, No. 24, seems to be the <em>Rāja Vāśishṭīputra Śrī Pulumāyi</em>, or <em>Śrī Pulumāyi Vā<sup>o</sup></em>, or <em>Navanara-svāmī Vā<sup>o</sup> Śrī Pulumāyi</em>, or <em>Rāja Vā<sup>o</sup> Svāmī Śrī Pulumāyi</em>, or [<em>Rāja</em>] <em>Vā<sup>o</sup> Śrī Sātakarṇi</em> of various inscriptions.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But a difficulty arises as to the identity of three kings who issued coins of the ‘bow and arrow’ type, found, it is believed, only at Kolhāpur, in the Marāthā state of that name in the Western Ghāts. The coin legends, transcribed in Sanskrit form, are:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I. <em>Rāja Vāśishṭhīputra Vilivāyakura</em> [I], sometimes restruck with II. <em>Rāja Māṭharīputra Sivalakura</em>—sometimes restruck with III. <em>Rāja Gautamīputra Vilivāyakura</em> [II]. The restrikings permit of no doubt about the serial order of these kings, but according to one view they were merely local governors and viceroys, and according to another, which I adopted definitely in earlier publications, they were members of the main dynasty. If the latter view be correct, the last named, Vilivāyakura II, must be king No. 23, the Gautamīputra of the Purāṇas. Professor D. R. Bhandarkar, however, rejects the identification of Vilivāyakura II with Gautamīputra, and regards him as belonging to a different line, which ruled separately in the country round Kolhāpur. According to the same authority, he or one of his predecessors was a contemporary of Pulumāyi II (<em>Ind. Ant.</em>, vol. xlix (1920), pp. 30–4). Another question is whether the strange word Vilivāyakura, which is probably either Telugu or Kanarese, should be regarded as a title or a proper name. In the previous edition of this work, the author inclined to the view that Vilivāyakura must be regarded as a name. The point is discussed by D. R. Bhandarkar in ‘Dekkan of the Sātavāhana Period’ (<em>Ind. Ant.</em> xlix (1920), pp. 31–4), and in my opinion still awaits a final solution.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Pulumāyi II, king No. 24, takes the name or title Sātakarṇi in the Kaṇheri inscription No. 11 = Lüders 994, and appears to have married the daughter of the Great Satrap Rudradāman I, who twice defeated him before <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 130. Pulumāyi, as stated in the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-268" data-page="268" data-folio="232" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n267_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 232">[p. 232]</button> Purāṇas, was the son of Gautamīputra. It seems to me quite clear that this Pulumāyi II was the king defeated by Rudradāman I. If that view is correct, though it is not absolutely free from doubt, a firm chronological datum is obtained from which the dates of the dynasty can be reckoned approximately both backwards and forwards.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The identification of king No. 27 with the Yajña Śrī of numerous coins and inscriptions is obvious and certain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Archaeologists have got into a bad habit of mixing up as ‘Western Satraps’ two distinct dynasties—namely, the Kshaharātas of Mahārāshṭra and the line of Chashṭana originally settled at Ujjain in Mālwā. No doubt both dynasties were satraps in the west, but they were entirely distinct, and it is better not to apply a common designation to both. Probably the capital of Nahapāna Kshaharāta was at or near Nāsik in the Western Ghāts; the original capital of Chashṭana certainly was Ujjain. Chashṭana’s grandson Rudradāman annexed from Pulumāyi II Āndhra most of the territories which Pulumāyi’s father had wrested from the Kshaharātas some years earlier. It is not necessary to believe that Gautamīputra Āndhra fought with Nahapāna personally. Study of the great Jogaltembhi hoard of more than 13,000 coins of Nahapāna proves that the coinage extended over many years, although always bearing the name of Nahapāna, who, I believe, was dead before Gautamīputra extirpated his family or clan. The arrow and thunderbolt of Nahapāna’s coins connect him with the Parthians and the Northern Satraps Hagāna and Hagāmāsha.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-21-fn-001" href="#sec-21-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-21-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The coinage of Chashṭana and his successors is quite different.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Greek geographer Ptolemy, who died after <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 161, and lived at Alexandria for forty years, described Ujjain as the capital of Tiastanēs, who, no doubt, is rightly identified with Chashṭana. The date of composition of the <em>Geography</em> is not known, but if the book was written about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 130 the information about Tiastanēs was not many years out of date.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-21-fn-002" href="#sec-21-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-21-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">My view of the relations between the Āndhras and the two distinct dynasties of foreign satraps is concisely exhibited in the following tabular statement. It seems to me that all the data harmonize admirably. Almost all students are agreed that the inscriptions and coins of the Chashṭana line of satraps are dated in the Śaka era, and it is possible that the Kshaharāta records are dated in the same way.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-21-fn-003" href="#sec-21-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-21-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-269"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="269" data-folio="233" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n268_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 233">[p. 233]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">THE LATER ĀNDHRA KINGS AND CONNECTED DYNASTIES</h2><div class="ledger-table-block" id="tbl-006"><div class="table-toolbar"><span class="table-id-badge">TBL-006 · FOLIO 269</span><span class="table-csv-links"><a href="/downloads/early-history-of-india-1924/datasets/tables/tbl-006_p0269.csv" download>Diplomatic CSV</a> · <a href="/downloads/early-history-of-india-1924/datasets/tidy/tbl-006_p0269.tidy.csv" download>Tidy CSV</a></span></div><div class="ledger-table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Statistical table" tabindex="0"><table class="ledger-table"><caption>THE LATER ĀNDHRA KINGS AND CONNECTED DYNASTIES</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Āndhra Kings, Purāṇa List, Pargiter, pp. 35–43, 71.</th><th scope="col">Acc. c.
