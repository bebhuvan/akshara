<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-52"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="52" data-folio="28" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n51_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 28">[p. 28]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER II</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">THE DYNASTIES BEFORE ALEXANDER</h3><h4 class="sec-h3">600 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> TO 326 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">History limited by chronology.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> political history of India begins for an orthodox Hindu more than three thousand years before the Christian era with the famous war waged on the banks of the Jumna, between the sons of Kuru and the sons of Pāṇḍu, as related in the vast epic known as the Mahābhārata.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-001" href="#sec-07-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> But the modern critic fails to find sober history in bardic tales, and is constrained to travel down the stream of time much farther before he comes to an anchorage of solid fact. In order to be available for the purpose of history, events must be susceptible of arrangement in definite chronological order, and capable of being dated approximately, if not exactly. Facts to which dates cannot be assigned, although they may be invaluable for the purposes of ethnology, philology, and other sciences, are of no use to the historian. Modern research has brought to light innumerable facts of the highest scientific value concerning prehistoric India, but the impossibility of assigning dates to the phenomena discovered excludes them from the domain of the historian, whose vision cannot pass the line which separates the dated from the undated.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Beginning of historical period.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">That line, in the case of India, may be drawn, at the earliest, through the middle of the seventh century <span class="sc">b. c.</span> ; a period of progress, marked by the development of maritime commerce, and probably by the diffusion of a knowledge of the art of writing. Up to about that time the inhabitants of India, even the most intellectual races, seem to have been <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-53" data-page="53" data-folio="29" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n52_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 29">[p. 29]</button> generally ignorant of the art of writing, and to have been obliged to trust to highly trained memory for the transmission of knowledge.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-002" href="#sec-07-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sixteen states in N. India.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In those days vast territories were still covered by forest, the home of countless wild beasts and scanty tribes of savage men ; while regions of great extent in Northern India had been occupied for untold centuries by more or less civilized communities of the higher races who, from time to time, during the unrecorded past, had pierced the mountain barriers of the north-western frontier. Practically nothing is ascertained concerning the immigration of the possibly equally advanced Dravidian races who entered India, we know not how, where, or whence, spread over the plateau of the Deccan, and extended to the extremity of the Peninsula. Our slender stock of knowledge is limited to the fortunes of the vigorous races, speaking an Aryan tongue, who poured down from the mountains of the Hindū Kush and Pāmīrs, filling the plains of the Panjāb and the upper basin of the Ganges with a sturdy and quick-witted population, unquestionably superior to the aboriginal races of those regions. The settled country between the Himalaya mountains and the Narbadā river was divided into a multitude of independent states, some monarchies and some tribal republics, owning no allegiance to any paramount power, secluded from the outer world, and free to fight among themselves. The most ancient literary traditions, compiled probably in the fourth or fifth century <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, but looking back to an older <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-54" data-page="54" data-folio="30" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n53_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 30">[p. 30]</button> time, enumerate sixteen of such states or powers, extending from Gandhāra, on the extreme north-west of the Panjāb, comprising the modern districts of Peshāwar and Rāwalpindi, to Avanti or Mālwa, with its capital Ujjain, which still retains its ancient name unchanged.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-003" href="#sec-07-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Religion and history.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The works of ancient Indian writers from which our historical data are extracted do not ordinarily profess to be histories, and are mostly religious treatises of various kinds. In such compositions the religious element necessarily takes the foremost place, and the secular affairs of the world occupy a very subordinate position. The particulars of political history incidentally recorded refer in consequence chiefly to the countries most prominent in the development of Indian religion.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Jainism and Buddhism.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The systems which we call Jainism and Buddhism had their roots in the forgotten speculations of the prehistoric past; but, as we know them, were founded respectively by Vardhamāna Mahāvīra and Gautama Buddha. Both these philosophers, who were for many years contemporary, were born, lived, and died in or near the kingdom of Magadha, the modern South Bihār. Mahāvīra, the son of a nobleman of Vaisāli, the famous city north of the Ganges, was nearly related to the royal family of Magadha, and died at Pāwā, in the modern district of Patna, within the territory of that kingdom.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Gautama Buddha, although born farther north, in the Sākya territory at the foot of the Nepāl hills,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-004" href="#sec-07-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> underwent his most memorable spiritual experiences at Bōdh Gayā in Magadha, and spent many years of his ministry within the limits of that state. The Buddhist and Jain books, therefore, tell us much about the Vrijjian confederacy, of which Vaisāli <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-55" data-page="55" data-folio="31" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n54_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 31">[p. 31]</button> was the capital,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-005" href="#sec-07-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and about Magadha, with its subordinate kingdom of Anga (Bhāgalpur).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kosala and Kāsī.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The neighbouring realm of Kosala, the modern kingdom of Oudh, was closely connected with Magadha by many ties ; and its capital Srāvastī (Savatthi), situated on the upper course of the Rāpti near the foot of the hills, was the reputed scene of many of Buddha's most striking discourses.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-006" href="#sec-07-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the sixth century <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Kosala appears to have occupied the rank afterwards attained by Magadha, and to have enjoyed precedence as the premier state of Upper India. It is therefore mentioned as often as the rival power. At the beginning of the historical period, the smaller kingdom of Kāsī, or Benares, apparently had lost its independence, and had been annexed by Kosala, with which its fortunes were indissolubly bound up. The lesser state owes its fame in the ancient books not only to its connexion with its powerful neighbour, but also to its being one of the most sacred spots in Buddhist church history, the scene of Buddha's earliest public preaching, where he first 'turned the wheel of the Law'.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kings of Magadha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reputation for special sanctity enjoyed by both Benares and Gayā in Magadha among orthodox Brahmanical Hindus adds little to the detailed information available, which is mainly derived from the writings of Jains and Buddhists, who were esteemed as heretics by the worshippers of the old gods. But the Brahmanical Purāṇas, compiled <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-56" data-page="56" data-folio="32" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n55_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 32">[p. 32]</button> centuries later in honour of the orthodox deities,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-007" href="#sec-07-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> happily include lists of the Buddhist and other kings of Magadha, which had become, before the time of their compilation, the recognized centre both religious and political of India; and so it happens that the Jain, Buddhist, and Brahmanical books combined tell us much about the history of Magadha, Anga, Kosala, Kāsī, and Vaisāli, while they leave us in the dark concerning the fortunes of most other parts of India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Śaiśunāga dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the Purānic lists the earliest dynasty which can claim historical reality is that known as the Saiśunāga, from the name of its founder Śiśunāga, or Śiśunāka.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-008" href="#sec-07-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> 642 <span class="sc">B. C.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">He was, apparently, the king, or Rāja, of a petty state, corresponding roughly with the present Patna and Gayā Districts; his capital being Rājagriha (Rājgīr), among the hills near Gayā. Nothing is known about his history, except the statement that he placed his son in Benares, and himself took up his abode at Girivraja near Rājagriha. The second, third, and fourth kings, likewise, are mere names.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bimbisāra <em>c.</em> 582 <span class="sc">B. C.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The first monarch about whom anything substantial is known is Bimbisāra, or Srēnika, the fifth of his line. He is credited with the building of New Rājagriha, the outer town to the north of the ring of hills encircling the ancient fort; and with the annexation of Anga, the small kingdom to the east, corresponding with the modern district of Bhāgalpur, and probably including Monghyr (Mungir).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-009" href="#sec-07-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The annexation <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-57" data-page="57" data-folio="33" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n56_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 33">[p. 33]</button> of Anga was the first step taken by the kingdom of Magadha in its advance to greatness and the position of supremacy which it attained in the following century; so that Bimbisāra may be regarded as the real founder of the Magadhan imperial power. He strengthened his position by matrimonial alliances with the more powerful of the neighbouring states, taking one consort from the royal family of Kosala, and another from the influential Lichchhavi clan at Vaisāli.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-010" href="#sec-07-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The latter lady was the mother of Ajātasatru, also called Kūnika, or Kūniya, the son who was selected as heir-apparent and crown prince. If our authorities may be believed, the reign of Bimbisāra lasted for twenty-eight years; and it is said that, towards its close, he resigned the royal power into the hands of his favourite son, and retired into private life.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Devadatta.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Orthodox Buddhist tradition affirms that Ajātasatru, weary of awaiting the slow process of nature, murdered his father by starvation, at the instigation of Devadatta, Buddha's cousin, who figures in the legends as a malignant plotter and wicked schismatic.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-011" href="#sec-07-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> It is probable, however, that the story is the product of <em>odium theologicum</em>, or sectarian rancour, which has done so much to falsify the history of ancient India. Devadatta certainly refused to accept the teaching of Gautama, and, preferring that of 'the former Buddhas', became the founder and head of a rival sect, which still survived in the seventh century after Christ.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-012" href="#sec-07-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-58"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="58" data-folio="34" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n57_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 34">[p. 34]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Schism has always been esteemed by the orthodox a deadly sin, and in all ages the unsuccessful heretic has been branded as a villain by the winning sect. Such, probably, is the origin of the numerous tales concerning the villanies of Devadatta, including the supposed incitement of his princely patron to commit the crime of parricide.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There seems to be no doubt that both Vardhamāna Mahāvīra, the founder of the system known as Jainism, and Gautama, the last Buddha, the founder of Buddhism as known to later ages, were preaching in Magadha during the reign of Bimbisāra, although it is difficult to reconcile traditional dates.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Death of Mahāvīra and Buddha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Jain saint, who was a near relative of Bimbisāra’s queen, the mother of Ajātasatru, possibly passed away towards the close of Ajātasatru’s reign, while the death of Gautama Buddha occurred in the earlier years of the same reign. There is reason to believe that the latter event took place in or about the year 543 <span class="sc">b. c.</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-013" href="#sec-07-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Interview of Buddha with Ajātaśatru.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Gautama Buddha was certainly an old man when Ajātasatru, or Kūnika, as the Jains call him, came to the throne about 554 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>; and he had at least one interview with that king.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-014" href="#sec-07-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">One of the most ancient Buddhist documents narrates in detail the story of a visit paid to Buddha by Ajātasatru, who is alleged to have expressed remorse for his supposed crime, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-59" data-page="59" data-folio="35" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n58_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 35">[p. 35]</button> and to have professed his faith in Buddha, who accepted his confession of sin. The concluding passage of the tale may be quoted as an illustration of an ancient Buddhist view of the relations between Church and State.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘And when he had thus spoken, Ajātasatru the king said to the Blessed One: “Most excellent, Lord, most excellent! Just as if a man were to set up that which has been thrown down, or were to reveal that which is hidden away, or were to point out the right road to him who has gone astray, or were to bring a lamp into the darkness so that those who have eyes could see external forms—just even so, Lord, has the truth been made known to me, in many a figure, by the Blessed One. And now I betake myself, Lord, to the Blessed One as my refuge, to the Truth, and to the Order. May the Blessed One accept me as a disciple, as one who, from this day forth, as long as life endures, has taken his refuge in them. Sin has overcome me, Lord, weak and foolish and wrong that I am, in that for the sake of sovranty, I put to death my father, that righteous man, that righteous king! May the Blessed One accept it of me, Lord, that I do so acknowledge it as a sin, to the end that in future I may restrain myself.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ “Verily, O king, it was sin that overcame you in acting thus. But inasmuch as you look upon it as sin, and confess it according to what is right, we accept your confession as to that.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ “For that, O king, is custom in the discipline of the noble ones, that whosoever looks upon his fault as a fault, and rightfully confesses it, shall attain to self-restraint in future.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘When he has thus spoken, Ajātasatru the king said to the Blessed One, “Now, Lord, we would fain go. We are busy, and there is much to do.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘ “Do, O king, whatever seemeth to thee fit.”</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Then Ajātasatru the king, pleased and delighted with the words of the Blessed One, arose from his seat, and bowed to the Blessed One, and keeping him on the right hand as he passed him, departed thence.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Now the Blessed One, not long after Ajātasatru the king had gone, addressed the brethren, and said: “This king, brethren, was deeply affected, he was touched in heart. If, brethren, the king had not put his father to death, that righteous man, and righteous king, then would the clear and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-60" data-page="60" data-folio="36" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n59_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 36">[p. 36]</button> spotless eye for the truth have arisen in him, even as he sat here."</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘Thus spake the Blessed One. The brethren were pleased and delighted at his words.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-015" href="#sec-07-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Comment.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It is difficult to sympathize with the pleasure and delight of the brethren. The stern and fearless reprobation of a deed of exceptional atrocity which we should expect from a great moral teacher is wholly wanting in Buddha’s words, and is poorly compensated for by the politeness of a courtier. But, as stated above, it is difficult to accept the story of the parricide as historically true; and our doubts are not lessened when the Ceylonese chronicler asks us to believe that Ajātasatru was followed in due course by four other parricide kings, of whom the last was dethroned by his minister, with the approval of a justly indignant people. The fact that the history of Parthia presents a nearly exact parallel in the succession of three parricide monarchs is of little value in establishing the credibility of the Buddhist tradition.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-016" href="#sec-07-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Probably Ajātasatru, like many later Indian sovereigns, did not confine his royal favour to any one sect, but at different times patronized the followers of the ‘former Buddhas’ led by Devadatta, the adherents of Gautama’s reformed Buddhism, and the Jains. Later when in consequence of Asoka’s patronage Buddhism became pre-eminent in northern India, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-61" data-page="61" data-folio="37" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n60_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 37">[p. 37]</button> leanings towards Jainism became criminal in the eyes of ecclesiastical chroniclers, who were ready to blacken the memory of persons deemed heretical with unfounded accusations of the gravest character.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">War with Kosala.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">One of the chief events of Ajātasatru's reign was a war with the aged king of Kosala, whose sister was the queen of Bimbisāra. Fortune in the contest inclined, now to one side, and now to another ; and on one occasion, it is said, Ajātasatru was carried away as a prisoner in chains to his opponent's capital. Ultimately peace was concluded, and a princess of Kosala was given in marriage to the king of Magadha. The facts of the struggle are obscure, being wrapped up in legendary matter from which it is impossible to disentangle them ; but the probability is that Ajātasatru won for Magadha a decided preponderance over its neighbour of Kosala. It is certain that the latter kingdom is not again mentioned as an independent power, and that in the fourth century <span class="sc">b. c.</span> it formed an integral part of the Magadhan empire.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Vaisāli.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The ambition of Ajātasatru, not satisfied with the humiliation of Kosala, next induced him to undertake the conquest of the country to the north of the Ganges, now known as Tirhūt, in which the Lichchhavi clan, famous in Buddhist legend, and probably of Tibetan origin, then occupied a prominent position. The invasion was successful ; the Lichchhavi capital, Vaisāli, was occupied, and Ajātasatru became master of his maternal grandfather's territory.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-017" href="#sec-07-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> It may be presumed that the invader carried his victorious arms to their natural limit, the foot of the mountains, and that from this time the whole region between the Ganges and the Himalaya became subject, more or less directly, to the suzerainty of Magadha.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Foundation of Pāṭaliputra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The victor erected a fortress at the village of Pātali on the northern bank of the Sōn near its confluence with the Ganges <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-62" data-page="62" data-folio="38" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n61_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 38">[p. 38]</button> to curb his Lichchhavi opponents. The foundations of a city nestling under the shelter of the fortress were laid by his grandson Udaya. The city so founded, including settlements of various ages, not precisely on one site, was known variously as Kusumapura, Pushpapura, or Pātaliputra, and rapidly developed in size and magnificence; until, under the Maurya dynasty, it became the capital, not only of Magadha, but of India.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-018" href="#sec-07-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Massacre of the Śākyas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Buddha, as has been mentioned above, died in the reign of Ajātasatru, in the eighth year of the reign, according to the <em>Mahāvaṁśa</em>, which cannot be relied on for details.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-019" href="#sec-07-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Shortly before his death, Kapilavastu, his ancestral home, was captured by Virūdhaka, king of Kosala, who is alleged to have perpetrated a ferocious massacre of the Sākya clan to which Buddha belonged. The story is so thickly encrusted with miraculous legend that the details of the event cannot be ascertained, but the coating of miracle was probably deposited upon a basis of fact, and we may believe that the Sākyas suffered much at the hands of Virūdhaka.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-020" href="#sec-07-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>cir.</em> 527 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> Darsaka.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When Ajātasatru died (<em>cir.</em> 527 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>), he was succeeded, according to the Purāṇas, by a son named Darsaka, who was in turn succeeded by his son Udaya.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-021" href="#sec-07-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> The Buddhist books <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-63" data-page="63" data-folio="39" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n62_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 39">[p. 39]</button> erroneously omit the intermediate name, and represent Udaya as the son and immediate successor of Ajātasatru. The reality of the existence of Darsaka, as king of Magadha, with his capital at Rājagriha, is established by the discovery of a play named <em>Svapnavāsavadatta</em>, attributed to Bhāsa, perhaps in the third century after Christ, which represents Darsaka as the contemporary of Udayana, king of Vatsa, and Mahāsena (<em>alias</em> Pradyota), king of Avanti, or Ujjain.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-022" href="#sec-07-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Udaya, &amp;c., <em>cir.</em> 503 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reign of Udaya may be assumed to have begun about 503 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> The tradition that he built Pātaliputra, or more accurately, the adjoining town of Kusumapura, is all that is known about him.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Persian conquests.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">If the chronology adopted in this chapter be even approximately correct, Ajātasatru's son, Darsaka, and his grandson, Udaya, must be regarded as the contemporaries of Darius, the son of Hystaspes, autocrat of the Persian Empire from 521 to 485 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> Darius, who was a very capable ruler, employed his officers in the exploration of a great part of Asia by means of various expeditions.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>cir.</em> 500 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">One of these expeditions was dispatched at some date later than 516 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> to prove the feasibility of a passage by sea <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-64" data-page="64" data-folio="40" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n63_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 40">[p. 40]</button> from the mouth of the Indus to Persia. The commander, Skylax of Karyanda in Karia, managed somehow to equip a squadron on the waters of the Panjāb rivers in the Gandhāra country, to make his way down to the ocean, and ultimately, in the thirteenth month, to reach the Red Sea. The particulars of his adventurous voyage have been lost, but we know that the information collected was of such value that, by utilizing it, Darius was enabled to annex the Indus valley, and to send his fleets into the Indian Ocean. The archers from India formed a valuable element in the army of Xerxes, and shared the defeat of Mardonius at Plataea (479 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Indian satrapy.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The conquered provinces were formed into a separate satrapy, the twentieth, which was considered the richest and most populous province of the empire. It paid the enormous tribute of 360 Euboic talents of gold-dust, or 185 hundredweights, worth fully a million sterling, and constituting about one-third of the total bullion revenue of the Asiatic provinces. Although the exact limits of the Indian satrapy cannot be determined, we know that it was distinct from Arīa (Herāt), Arachosia (Kandahār), and Gandaria (North-western Panjāb). It must have comprised, therefore, the course of the Indus from Kālabāgh to the sea, including the whole of Sind, and perhaps included a considerable portion of the Panjāb east of the Indus. But when Alexander invaded the country, nearly two centuries later, the Indus was the boundary between the Persian empire and India, and both the Panjāb and Sind were governed by numerous native princes.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-023" href="#sec-07-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> In ancient times the courses of the rivers were <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-65" data-page="65" data-folio="41" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n64_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 41">[p. 41]</button> quite different from what they now are, and vast tracts in Sind and the Panjāb, now desolate, were then rich and prosperous.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-024" href="#sec-07-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> This fact largely explains the surprising value of the tribute paid by the twentieth satrapy.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">?470 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote is-locus">413 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">According to the Purānic lists Udaya's successors were Nandivardhana and Mahānandin, both of whom are shadowy figures, mere <em>nominis umbrae</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-025" href="#sec-07-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The long reigns attributed to them, of forty (or forty-two) and forty-three years respectively, total eighty-three or eighty-five years, are not likely to be correct. The names of both kings, <em>Nandivardhana</em> and <em>Mahānandin</em>, seem to justify the inference that they were Nandas; and Mahānandin, the last of the dynasty, is said to have had by a Sūdra, or low-caste, woman a son named Mahāpadma Nanda, who usurped the throne, and so established the Nanda family or dynasty. This event may be dated in or about 413 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Nandas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At this point all our authorities become unintelligible and incredible. According to the Purāṇas the last two kings of the Saiśunāga dynasty were followed by the ‘Nine Nandas’, namely king Mahāpadma (eighty-eight years) and his eight sons (twelve years), of whom the first was named Sukalpa, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-66" data-page="66" data-folio="42" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n65_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 42">[p. 42]</button> with variants.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-026" href="#sec-07-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> These two generations are thus supposed to have reigned for a century. It is clear that the history has somehow been falsified and that the chronology cannot be correct. The Jains, doing still greater violence to reason, extend the duration of the dynasty to 155 years, while the Buddhist Mahāvaṁsa, Dīpavaṁsa, and Asokāvadāna deepen the confusion by hopelessly muddled and contradictory stories not worth repeating. Some powerful motive, possibly <em>odium theologicum</em>, as in other cases, must have existed for the distortion of the history of the so-called ‘Nine Nandas’ in all forms of the tradition.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-027" href="#sec-07-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Greek accounts.</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote is-locus">326 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Greek and Roman historians, who derived their information from either Megasthenes or the companions of Alexander, and thus rank as contemporary witnesses reported at second-hand, throw a little light on the real history. When Alexander was stopped in his advance at the Hyphasis in 326 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, he was informed by a native chieftain named Bhagala or Bhagēla, whose statements were confirmed by Pōros, that the king of the Gangaridae and Prasii nations on the banks of the Ganges was named, as nearly as the Greeks could catch the unfamiliar sounds, Xandrames or Agrammes. This monarch was said to command a force of 20,000 horse, 200,000 foot, 2,000 chariots, and 3,000 or 4,000 elephants. Inasmuch as the capital of the Prasii nation undoubtedly was Pātaliputra, the reports made to Alexander can have referred only to the king of Magadha, who must have been one of the Nandas mentioned in native tradition.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-028" href="#sec-07-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The reigning king was alleged to be extremely unpopular, owing to his wickedness and base origin. He was, it is said, the son of a barber, who, having become the paramour of the queen of the last legitimate sovereign, contrived the king’s death, and, under pretence of acting as <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-67" data-page="67" data-folio="43" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n66_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 43">[p. 43]</button> guardian to his sons, got them into his power, and exterminated the royal family. After their extermination he begat the son who was reigning at the time of Alexander's campaign, and who, ‘more worthy of his father's condition than his own, was odious and contemptible to his subjects.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-029" href="#sec-07-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Indian traditions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This story confirms the statements of the Purāṇas that the Nanda dynasty was of ambiguous origin and comprised only two generations. The oldest Purāṇa brands the first Nanda, Mahāpadma, as a prince, ‘urged on by prospective fortune’, whose reign marked the end of the Kshatriya, or high born, kings, and the beginning of the rule of those of low degree, ranking as Sūdras. The Mahāvaṁsa, when it dubs the last Nanda by the name of Dhana or ‘Riches’, seems to hint at an imputation of avariciousness against the first Nanda; and the Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang also refers to the Nanda Rāja as the reputed possessor of great wealth.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-030" href="#sec-07-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Summary.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">By putting all the hints together we may conclude with tolerable certainty that the Nanda family really was of base origin, that it acquired power by the assassination of the legitimate king, and retained possession of the throne for two generations only. The great military power of the usurpers, as attested by Greek testimony, was the result of the conquests effected by Bimbisāra and Ajātasatru, and presumably continued by their successors; but the limits of the Nanda dominions cannot be defined, nor can the dates of the dynasty be determined with accuracy. It is quite certain that the two generations did not last for a hundred and fifty-five, and improbable that they lasted for a hundred, years; but it is impossible to determine their actual duration.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-031" href="#sec-07-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-68" data-page="68" data-folio="44" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n67_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 44">[p. 44]</button> The period of ninety-one years has been assumed as fitting into a definite chronological scheme.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rise of Chandragupta Maurya. <span class="sc">c.</span> 322 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">However mysterious the Nine Nandas may be—if, indeed, they really were nine<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-032" href="#sec-07-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>—there is no doubt that the last of them was deposed and slain by Chandragupta Maurya, who seems to have been an illegitimate scion of the family.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-033" href="#sec-07-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sir G. Grierson informs me that the Nandas were reputed to be bitter enemies of the Brahmans, and that their reign was therefore excluded from chronological computation by the poet Chand in the twelfth century, who used the <em>Ananda</em> ('without Nanda') form of the Vikrama era, less by ninety or ninety-one than the ordinary reckoning. The word 'nanda' seems to be used as equivalent to 'nine' (100–9 = 91). It is very probable that the Nandas were Jains and therefore hateful to the Brahmans, who would naturally regard them as unholy persons unworthy of inclusion in orthodox Hindu annals. It is unquestionable that the Nanda king dethroned by Chandragupta was a heretic in Hindu eyes, for the concluding verse of Kantilya's <em>Arthasāstra</em> (transl. by Shāma Sastri) states that 'this <em>Sāstra</em> has been made by him who from intolerance (of misrule) quickly rescued the scriptures and the science of weapons and the earth which had passed to the Nanda king'. (The text is quoted by R. K. Mukerji in Introd., p. xiii, to N. N. Law, <em>Studies in Ancient Hindu Polity.</em>) The supposition that the last Nanda was either a Jain or a Buddhist is strengthened by the fact that one form of the local tradition attributed to him the erection of the Pānch Pahārī at Patna, a group of ancient <em>stūpas</em> which might be either Jain or Buddhist.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-69"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="69" data-folio="45" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n68_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 45">[p. 45]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There is no difficulty in believing the tradition that the revolution involved the extermination of all related to the fallen monarch, for revolutions in the East are not effected without much shedding of blood. Nor is there any reason to discredit the statements that the usurper was attacked by a confederacy of the northern powers, including Kashmīr, and that the attack failed owing to the Machiavellian intrigues of Chandragupta's Brahman adviser, who is variously named Chānakya, Kautilya, or Vishnugupta. But it would not be safe to rely on the details given in our only authority, a play written centuries after the events referred to; nor would there be any use in recounting the wondrous tales, mostly belonging to the world's common stock of folk-lore, which have been recorded in various books, and relate the miracles attendant upon the birth and youth of Chandragupta, the first strictly historical universal monarch of India.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-034" href="#sec-07-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Accession of Chandragupta.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">His accession to the throne of Magadha may be dated between 325 and 320 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, perhaps in 322 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> The dominions of the Magadha crown were then extensive, certainly including the territories of the nations called Prasii and Gangaridae by the Greeks, and probably comprising at least the kingdoms of Kosala, Tirhūt or North Bihār, and Benares, as well as Anga and Magadha proper or South Bihār. Two or three years before the revolution at Pātaliputra, Alexander had swept like a hurricane through the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-70" data-page="70" data-folio="46" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n69_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 46">[p. 46]</button> Panjāb and Sind, and it is said that Chandragupta, then a youth, had met the mighty Macedonian.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-035" href="#sec-07-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Whether that anecdote be true or not, and I see no reason to doubt its truth, it is certain that the troubles consequent upon the departure of Alexander gave young Chandragupta his opportunity. He assumed the command of the native revolt against the foreigner, and destroyed most of the Macedonian garrisons. The language of our authorities seems to imply that the destruction of the Nanda royal family preceded the attack on the foreign settlements in the basin of the Indus. The revolution was not completed in a moment, it being clear that the various stages occupied at least a year. When all opposition had been crushed by force or circumvented by guile, Chandragupta, in the vigour of his early manhood, stood forth as the unquestioned master of Northern India.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-036" href="#sec-07-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> But before the story of the deeds of Chandragupta Maurya and the descendants who succeeded him on the throne of Magadha can be told, we must pause to unfold the wondrous tale of the Indian adventure of ‘Philip’s warlike son’.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-07-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-07-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">The epoch of the Kaliyuga, 3102 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, is usually identified with the era of Yudhishṭhira, and the date of the Mahābhārata war.
