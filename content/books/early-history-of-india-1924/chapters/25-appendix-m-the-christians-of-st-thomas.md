<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-298"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="298" data-folio="260" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n297_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 260">[p. 260]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX M</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>The Christians of St. Thomas</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Books cited.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In this Appendix I confine myself to the limited task of justifying the propositions in the text. It is out of the question to discuss fully the problems connected with the legend of St. Thomas and the origin of the ancient congregations of the 'Christians of St. Thomas' in Southern India. The following books are cited under the names of their respective authors:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(1) <span class="sc">Mackenzie, G. T.</span>—'History of Christianity in Travancore', 3rd ed., <em>in The Travancore State Manual</em>, 1906, vol. ii, pp. 114– 219.</li><li>(2) <span class="sc">Rae, G. Milne.</span>—<em>The Syrian Church in India.</em> (Blackwood, 1892.)</li><li>(3) <span class="sc">Medlycott, Bishop A. E.</span>—<em>India and the Apostle Thomas.</em> (Nutt, 1905.)</li><li>(4) <span class="sc">Richards, W. J.</span>—<em>The Indian Christians of St. Thomas.</em> (Bemrose, 1908.)</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Seven Churches.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The seven churches founded by St. Thomas are enumerated by Richards (p. 77) as (1) Kotta-kāyalil, (2) Gōkamangalam, (3) Niranam, (4) Chayil, (5) Kurakēni, (6) Quilon, (7) Palūr. Mackenzie gives the same list, with some variation of spelling, except that he substitutes Maliankara for Kurakēni. Rae (p. 361) gives the list as (1) Cranganore, (2) Quilon, (3) Palur, (4) Parur, (5) South Pallipuram<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-25-fn-001" href="#sec-25-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-25-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> or Kokamungalum, (5) Neranum, (7) Nellakkul, called also Chael or Shail. Richards is responsible for the statement that</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>'One of the seven churches founded by St. Thomas was at a place named Chayal in the eastern hills of Travancore. It has long been abandoned, owing to wild animals, but the ruins remain, and would repay antiquarian research' (p. 91).</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I cannot explain the discrepancies in the lists, or say anything more about the alleged ruins at Chayal (<em>alias</em> Nellakkul, Chael, or Shail).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Facts supporting the tradition.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">St. Thomas is believed to have ordained priests from two families: namely, one at Shankarapuri, which died out, and the other at Pakalōnmattam, which survived into the nineteenth century, and supplied archdeacons in the Portuguese and bishops in the Dutch period (Mackenzie, p. 137; Richards, p. 76).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">'There is no doubt,' Mr. V. Nagam Aiya observes, 'as to the tradition that St. Thomas came to Malabar and converted a few families of Nambudiris, some of whom were ordained by him as priests, such as <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-299" data-page="299" data-folio="261" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n298_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 261">[p. 261]</button> those of Sankarapuri and Pakalomattam.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-25-fn-002" href="#sec-25-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-25-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> For in consonance with this long-standing traditional belief in the minds of the people of the Apostle's mission and labours among high-caste Hindus, we have [it] before us to-day the fact that certain Syrian Christian women, particularly of a Desom called Kunnamkolam, wear clothes as Nambudiri women do, move about screening themselves with huge umbrellas from the gaze of profane eyes as those women do, and will not marry, except perhaps in exceptional cases and that only recently, but from among dignified families of similar aristocratic descent. This is a valuable piece of evidence of the conduct of the community, corroborating the early tradition extant on the coast.' (<em>Manual</em>, II, 122.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The martyrdoms.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Mr. Aiya goes on to notice the Malabar version of the Abbanes story of the <em>Acta</em>, which is given more fully from a Malayālam manuscript by Richards (p. 72).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The martyrdom of St. Thomas at any place may be doubted, because an early writer named Heracleon, cited by Clement of Alexandria (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 200), states that Thomas was not martyred (Medlycott, p. 120). The Roman Catholic writers, of course, impugn the statement of Heracleon, but anybody is at liberty to believe it if he chooses to do so.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Christianity in Socotra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The earliest testimony to the existence of a Christian Church in Socotra, derived from Persia, is that of Cosmas Indicopleustes, who wrote in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 535. About a thousand years later (1542), St. Francis Xavier found nominally Christian congregations in the island, who claimed descent from the converts made by St. Thomas. The belief that Theophilus, the missionary sent out by Constantine about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 354, visited Socotra appears to be erroneous (Medlycott, pp. 136, 138, 196–201).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-25-fn-003" href="#sec-25-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-25-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Bishop Medlycott is, I think, right in holding that Theophilus visited Malabar and found Christians in that region.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">A Ceylonese tradition.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Historical traditions of India and Ceylon when read together seem to carry the evidence for the existence of the Church in Malabar back to the third century. We learn from the Ceylonese chronicle, the <em>Mahāvaṁśa</em> (ch. xxxvi), composed about the beginning of the sixth century, that in the reign of king Gothakābhaya or Meghavarṇābhaya, whom Geiger places in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 302 –15, a learned Tamil heretic overcame an orthodox Buddhist theologian in controversy and gained the favour of the king, who placed his son under his tuition. The <em>Mahāvaṁśa</em> represents the victor in the disputation as being a monk named Sanghamitra, ‘versed in the teachings concerning the exorcism of spirits and so forth’. Mr. K. G. Seshar Aiyar interprets this statement as meaning really that the successful controversialist was a Hindu, and identifies him with the famous Śaiva saint Māṇikka (or Māṇi) <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-300" data-page="300" data-folio="262" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n299_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 262">[p. 262]</button> Vāśagar.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-25-fn-004" href="#sec-25-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-25-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The Tamil lives of that personage affirm that the saint actually converted the king of Ceylon towards the end of his career. That king may be identified with Gothakābhaya, and it is possible that the author of the <em>Mahāvaṁśa</em> may have misrepresented the Śaiva Hindu Māṇikka Vāśagar as Sanghamitra, a Buddhist heretic.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Māṇikka Vāśagar in Malabar.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">However little credit we may be disposed to give to the story about the conversion of the king of Ceylon, or to the identification of that king with Gothakābhaya of the <em>Mahāvaṁśa</em>, I see no reason for hesitating to believe the Indian tradition that Māṇikka Vāśagar visited Malabar and reconverted two families of Christians to Hinduism. The descendants of those families, who are still known as Manigrāmakars, are not admitted to full privileges as caste Hindus. Some traditions place the reconversion as having occurred about <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 270. If that date be at all nearly correct, the Malabar Church must be considerably older. So far as I can appreciate the value of the arguments from the history of Tamil literature, there seems to be good independent reason for believing that Māṇikka Vāśagar may have lived in the third century. Some authors even place him about the beginning of the second century.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-25-fn-005" href="#sec-25-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-25-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> If he really lived so early, his relation with the Church in Malabar would confirm the belief in its apostolic origin.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-25-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-25-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">'Palli' in the name Pallipuram means 'church' or any non-Hindu place of worship.</span></li><li id="sec-25-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-25-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">Richards spells ‘Pakalōnmattam’.
