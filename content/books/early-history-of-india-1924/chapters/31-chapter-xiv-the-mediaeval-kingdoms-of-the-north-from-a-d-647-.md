<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-420"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="420" data-folio="374" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n419_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 374">[p. 374]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER XIV</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">THE MEDIAEVAL KINGDOMS OF THE NORTH</h3><h4 class="sec-h3"><span class="sc">From a.d. 647 to 1200</span></h4><h4 class="sec-h3">I</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Relations with China and Tibet</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Chinese influence on northern frontier of India.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> tenacity of the Chinese government in holding on to the most distant possessions of the empire has been exemplified in recent times by the recovery of Kāshgaria and Yunnan from Muhammadan powers, and of Kulja from the Russians. The history of the seventh and eighth centuries offers many illustrations of the same characteristic, and exhibits China as making the most determined efforts to exercise influence in, and assert suzerainty over, the countries on the northern frontier of India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 502– 56. Ephthalite empire.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the first half of the sixth century the power of China in the ‘Western countries’ had vanished, and the Ephthalites, or White Huns, ruled a vast empire, which included Kāshgaria—the ‘Four Garrisons’ of Chinese writers— Kashmīr,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-001" href="#sec-31-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and Gandhāra, the region near Peshāwar.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 565. Empire of Western Turks.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">About the year 565 (‘between 563 and 567’) the Ephthalite dominion passed into the hands of the Western Turks and Persians; but the grasp of the latter power on the provinces south of the Oxus soon relaxed, and the Turks became the heirs of the Ephthalites in the whole of their territory as far as the Indus. Accordingly, in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 630, when Hiuen Tsang was on his way to India, his safety was assured by passports granted by Tong-she-hū, the ‘Kazan’, or supreme chief of the Western Turks, which guaranteed him protection as far as Kapisa.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-002" href="#sec-31-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-421"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="421" data-folio="375" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n420_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 375">[p. 375]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 630. Chinese defeat of Northern Turks.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the same year the pilgrim's powerful protector was assassinated, and the Chinese, under the guidance of the emperor T'ai-tsung, the second prince of the Tang dynasty, inflicted upon the Northern or Eastern Turks a defeat so decisive that the vanquished became slaves to the Chinese for fifty years.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 640– 8. Chinese conquest of Kuchā, &amp;c.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When relieved from fear of the Northern Turks, the Chinese were able to turn their arms against the western tribes, and in the years 640–8 succeeded in occupying Turfān, Kara-shahr, and Kuchā, thus securing the northern road of communication between the East and West.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Friendly relations with Tibet.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At this time Tibet was under the rule of the famous king, Srong-tsan-Gampo (acc. <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 629), who founded Lhāsa in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 639, introduced Buddhism into his country, and, with the help of Indian scholars, devised the Tibetan alphabet.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-003" href="#sec-31-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> While still very young he married Bhrikuti, a daughter of the king of Nepāl, and two years later, in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 641, he succeeded with much difficulty in winning by his victories the hand of the princess Wen-cheng, daughter of the Chinese emperor, T'ai-tsung. Both these ladies being zealous Buddhists, converted their young husband, and so determined the whole course of Tibetan history. The Church has not been slow to recognize the merit of its patrons. The king has been deified as an incarnation of Buddha, Avalokitesvara, the Saviour, while his Nepalese consort is revered as the 'Green Tārā' and the Chinese princess as the 'White Tārā'. The Chinese marriage secured the maintenance of friendly relations between Tibet and China during the life of Srong-tsan-Gampo, which ended, according to most authorities, in or about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 698, but may possibly have come to a close several years earlier. In consequence, the Chinese envoys, in the years 643–5, when on their way to the court of Harsha, were able to pass through Tibet and its dependency Nepāl as allied countries, and both those kingdoms willingly sent <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-422" data-page="422" data-folio="376" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n421_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 376">[p. 376]</button> troops to rescue Wang-Hiuen-tse from the troubles into which he fell after Harsha's death.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-004" href="#sec-31-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 659– 61. China in possession of empire of Western Turks.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The work of subduing the Turks, begun by the emperor T'ai-tsung, was continued by his successor Kao-tsung (649–83), and, by the year 659, China was nominally mistress of the entire territory of the Western Turks, which was then formally annexed. In 661–5 China enjoyed unparalleled prestige, and had reached a height of glory never again attained. Kapisa (Ki-pin) was a province of the empire, and the imperial retinue included ambassadors from Udyāna, or the Suwāt valley, and from all the countries extending from Persia to Korea.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 670. Occupation of Kāshgaria by the Tibetans.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">But this magnificent extension of the empire did not last long. A terrible defeat inflicted by the Tibetans in 670 deprived China of Kāshgaria, or the 'Four Garrisons', which remained in the hands of the victors until <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 692, when the province was recovered by the Chinese.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 744. Final overthrow of the Northern Turks.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Between 682 and 691 the Northern Turks had regained a good deal of the power which had been shattered by the defeat of 630, and even exercised a certain amount of control over the western tribes. But internal dissension was at all times the bane of the Central Asian nations, and the Chinese well knew how to take advantage of the national failing. They intervened in the tribal quarrels, with the support of the Uigurs and Karluks, with such effect that in 744 the Uigurs established themselves on the Orkhon in the eastern part of the Turkish territory; while, on the west, the Karluks gradually occupied the country of the Ten Tribes, and took <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-423" data-page="423" data-folio="377" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n422_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 377">[p. 377]</button> possession of Tokmak and Talas, the former residences of the Turkish chiefs, to the west of Lake Issyk-kūl.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 665– 715. Routes between China and the west closed.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Between 665 and 715 the government of China was unable to interfere effectually in the affairs of the countries between the Jaxartes (Syr Daryā) and the Indus; the southern route to the west through Kāshgaria having been closed by the Tibetans, and the roads over the Hindū Kush blocked by the conquests of Kotaiba, the Arab general, who was busily engaged in spreading the religion of the Prophet throughout Central Asia.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 715– 47. Revival of Chinese influence on borders of India.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The accession of the emperor Hiuen-tsung, in 713, marks a revival of Chinese activity; and determined efforts were made by means of both diplomacy and arms to keep open the Pāmīr passes, and to check the ambition of the Arabs and Tibetans, who sometimes combined. In 719, Samarkand and other kingdoms invoked the aid of China against the armies of Islam; while the Arab leaders sought to obtain the co-operation of the minor states on the Indian borderland. The chiefs of Udyāna (Suwāt), Khottal (west of Badakshān), and Chitrāl, having refused to listen to Muslim blandishments, were rewarded by the emperor of China with letters patent conferring on each the title of king; and a similar honour was bestowed upon the rulers of Yasin (Little Po-lu), Zabulistan (Ghaznī), Kapisa, and Kashmīr. China made every effort to organize these frontier kingdoms, so as to form an effective barrier against both Arabs and Tibetans. Chandrāpīda, king of Kashmīr, received investiture as king from the emperor in 720, and his brother Muktāpīda-Lalitāditya was similarly honoured in 733.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A few years later—in 744 and 747—Chinese influence had been so far extended that the emperor granted titles to the king of Tabaristān, south of the Caspian. In the latter year a Chinese army crossed the Pāmīrs, in spite of all difficulties, and reduced the king of Yasin to subjection.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 751. Rout of Chinese by Arabs and Karluks.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">But, as in the seventh century, so in the eighth, the Chinese dominion over the western countries was short-lived, and was shattered by a disastrous defeat inflicted in 751 on the Chinese general Sien-chi by the Arabs, who were aided by <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-424" data-page="424" data-folio="378" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n423_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 378">[p. 378]</button> the Karluk tribes. Indirectly this disaster had an important consequence for European civilization. The art of making paper, up to that time a monopoly of remote China, was introduced into Samarkand by Chinese prisoners, and so became known to Europe, with results familiar to all.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-005" href="#sec-31-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Buddhism in Tibet.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the long reign of Thi-(or Khri-)srong-de-tsan (A.D. 743–789)<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-006" href="#sec-31-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> the development of Buddhism in Tibet was encouraged with a zeal which did not shrink from persecution of the adherents of the rival indigenous Bon (or Pon) religion. The Indian sages, Sānta-rakshita and Padma-sambhava, were invited to court, and with their aid a system of clerical government was instituted, which survives to this day as Lamaism. The work of Thi-srong-de-tsan was continued and carried further by King Ralpachan (<span class="sc">a. d.</span> 816–38), but his successor, Langdarma, hated Buddhism, and did his best to extirpate it. A Lama avenged the wrongs of his co-religionists by assassinating the king, <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 842. During the eleventh century (<span class="sc">a.d.</span> 1013 and 1038), Buddhist missionaries from Magadha securely re-established Buddhism as the official and predominant religion of Tibet.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-007" href="#sec-31-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Contact with China.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the reign of Ralpachan a severe struggle with China took place, which was terminated by a peace recorded (822) in bilingual inscriptions at Lhāsa. In subsequent ages Tibetan relations with the Chinese empire varied much from time to time, but whatever they might be, they did not concern India. The final attainment of supremacy by China over Tibet was deferred until 1751. Since that date the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-425" data-page="425" data-folio="379" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n424_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 379">[p. 379]</button> Chinese government has always endeavoured to keep Europeans out of Tibet, and has generally succeeded in doing so. Tibetan affairs, consequently, long remained completely apart from Indian history. Contact between the politics of India and those of China had ceased in the eighth century,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-008" href="#sec-31-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> owing to the growth of Tibetan power at that time. It was not renewed until the conquest of Upper Burma in 1885, which made the Indian and Chinese empires conterminous. In these latter days, Tibet, which had been a dependency of China in greater or less degree for several centuries, has again come within the purview of the Indian government, and its affairs have been the subject of Anglo-Chinese diplomacy.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-009" href="#sec-31-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">II</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Nepāl</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Extent of Nepāl.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The kingdom of Nepāl, as at present constituted, is a considerable self-governed state extending from Sikkim on the east to Kumāon on the west, for a distance of about 500 miles along the northern frontier of Tirhūt, Oudh, and the Agra Province. Except for a narrow strip of lowlands known as the Tarāi, the whole country is a maze of mountains and valleys. Strictly speaking, the name Nepāl should be restricted, and was confined in ancient times to the enclosed valley, about 20 miles in length by 15 in breadth, within which Kāthmandū, the capital, and many other towns and villages are situated. The policy of the existing government rigorously excludes Europeans from almost every part of the state except that valley, and consequently very little is known about the rest.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">In Samudragupta's time.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The earliest definite historical information concerning Nepāl, meaning the valley, is the statement in Samudragupta's great Allāhābād inscription of the fourth century <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-426" data-page="426" data-folio="380" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n425_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 380">[p. 380]</button> after Christ, that, like Kāmarūpa or Assam, it was an autonomous frontier state, paying tribute and yielding obedience to the paramount Gupta power. The tribute probably was little more than nominal and the obedience intermittent. At the present day the Nepālese Government, although practically independent, sends presents or tribute to the Emperor of China, and recognizes in a vague way the suzerainty of that potentate, while receiving a British Resident and subordinating its foreign policy to the direction of the Government of India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">In Asoka's time.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Local tradition affirms that long before the time of Samudragupta, in the days of Asoka, in the third century <span class="sc">b.c.</span>, the valley was under his control, and this tradition is confirmed by the existence at the town of Pātan of monuments attributed to him and his daughter, and by inscriptions which prove that the lowlands at the foot of the hills were an integral part of his empire.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-010" href="#sec-31-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The distance from Pātaliputra to the valley of Nepāl not being great, it is probable that that territory formed part of the home provinces and was administered directly from the Maurya capital.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Local annals.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It is impossible to say exactly what happened between the time of Asoka and that of Samudragupta. The local annals, which exist in abundance, do not bear strict criticism, and give little information of value. The ruling dynasty during the sixth and the early part of the seventh century was a Lichchhavi family, but its exact connexion with the Lichchhavis of Vaisāli is not ascertainable. The Nepālese Lichchhavis are described by Hiuen Tsang as being eminent scholars and believing Buddhists, ranking as Kshatriyas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-011" href="#sec-31-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Seventh century and later.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the seventh century Nepāl occupied the position of a buffer state between Tibet on the north, then a great power in Asia, and the empire of Harsha of Kanauj on the south. King Amsuvarman, founder of the Thākuri dynasty, who died about <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 642, was in close touch with Tibet by reason <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-427" data-page="427" data-folio="381" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n426_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 381">[p. 381]</button> of his daughter's marriage to Srong-tsan-Gampo, the monarch of that country, who was strong enough to compel the emperor of China to give him the princess Wen-cheng as second consort in 641.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-012" href="#sec-31-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> After Harsha's death Tibetan and Nepālese troops acted together in support of Wang-hiuen-tse, the Chinese envoy, and against the usurper of Harsha's throne (<em>ante</em>, p. 366). It is also certain that at the beginning of the eighth century Nepāl was still dependent on Tibet, and continued in that position until <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 703, when it, together with Tirhūt, shook off dependence on Tibet. The Tibetan king was killed in the course of the war.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-013" href="#sec-31-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The reason for the introduction of a new Nepālese era dating from October, <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 879, is not known. Chinese relations with Nepāl and India had come to an end soon after the middle of the eighth century. In recent times wars between China and Nepāl have resulted in a complimentary recognition by the smaller state of the suzerainty of the greater.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gurkha conquest.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The confused and bloodstained story of the various petty dynasties which ruled in Nepāl up to <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1768 possesses no general interest. In that year the Gurkhas conquered the country, and established the dynasty which now rules Nepāl through the agency of powerful ministers who have taken over all the substantial functions of sovereignty, reducing the nominal monarchs to a position of absolute insignificance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nepālese Buddhism.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Buddhism, in its early pure form, was introduced into the valley by Asoka, whose daughter is believed to have erected sacred edifices near the capital, which are still pointed out. Little or nothing is known concerning the religious history of the country for many hundred years afterwards. In the seventh century the prevailing religion appears to have been a much modified Tantric variety of the 'Great Vehicle' Buddhist doctrine, allied so closely to the orthodox Hindu cult of Siva as to be distinguishable from it with difficulty.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-428"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="428" data-folio="382" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n427_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 382">[p. 382]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the course of ages the corruption of the church increased, and Nepāl now presents the strange spectacle of so-called monasteries swarming with the families of married 'monks' engaged in all sorts of secular occupations.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-014" href="#sec-31-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The spontaneous progress of the decay of Buddhism, which had been operating in Nepāl for centuries, has been much hastened by the action of the Gurkha Government, to which Buddhist rites are obnoxious; and there is good reason to believe that in the course of a few generations Nepālese Buddhism will be almost extinct.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Decay of Buddhism in India.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The total disappearance of the Buddhist worship from India, the land of its birth, has been the subject of much discussion and some misconception. Until lately the assumption commonly was made that Buddhism had been extinguished by a storm of Brahman persecution. That is not the true explanation. Occasional active persecutions by Hindu kings, like Sasāṅka, which no doubt occurred, though rarely, formed a factor of minor importance in the movement which slowly restored India to the Brahmanical fold. The furious massacres perpetrated in many places by Musalman invaders were more efficacious than orthodox Hindu persecutions, and had a great deal to do with the disappearance of Buddhism in several provinces. But the main cause was the gradual, almost insensible, assimilation of Buddhism to Hinduism, which attained to such a point that often it is nearly impossible to draw a line between the mythology and images of the Buddhists and those of the Hindus. This process of assimilation is going on now before our eyes in Nepāl, and the chief interest which that country offers to some students is the opportunity presented by it for watching the manner in which the octopus of Hinduism is slowly strangling its Buddhist victim. The automatic compression of the dying cult by its elastic rival is aided by the action of the Government, which throws its influence and favour on <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-429" data-page="429" data-folio="383" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n428_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 383">[p. 383]</button> the side of the Hindus, while abstaining from violent persecution of the Buddhists.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-015" href="#sec-31-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">III</h3><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Kāmarūpa or Assam</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Extent of kingdom.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The ancient kingdom of Kāmarūpa, although roughly equivalent to Assam, generally occupied an area larger than that of the modern province, and extended westward to the Karatōya river,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-016" href="#sec-31-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> thus including the Kūch Bihār State and the Rangpur District. The earliest notice of the kingdom which is of any use for the purposes of the historian is the statement in Samudragupta's inscription on the Allāhābād pillar, recorded about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 360 or 370, that Kāmarūpa was then one of the frontier states outside the limits of the Gupta empire, but paying tribute and owing a certain amount of obedience to the paramount power.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-017" href="#sec-31-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Hiuen Tsang.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The next glimpse of this remote region is afforded by the Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang. When he was staying for the second time at the Nālandā monastery, early in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 643, he was compelled, much against his will, to pay a visit to the king of Kāmarūpa, who insisted on making the acquaintance of the renowned scholar, and would not take a refusal. After a short stay at the capital of Kāmarūpa, Harsha Sīlāditya, the Kanauj sovereign, sent a message commanding that Hiuen Tsang should be sent to him. The king replied that Harsha might take his head if he could, but should not get his Chinese visitor. However, when Harsha sent a peremptory order to the effect that he would trouble the king to send back his head by the messenger, that potentate, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-430" data-page="430" data-folio="384" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n429_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 384">[p. 384]</button> on second thoughts, deemed it advisable to comply with the request of his suzerain, and hastened to meet Harsha, bringing the pilgrim with him.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bhāskaravarman or Kumāra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This king was named Bhāskaravarman, and was also known as Kumāra. He belonged to a very ancient dynasty, which claimed to have existed for a thousand generations. Hiuen Tsang describes him as being a Brahman by caste, but the form of his name indicates that he considered himself to be a Kshatriya or Rājpūt, and it would seem that the pilgrim really meant that Bhāskaravarman was a Brahmanical Hindu in religion. He may have been a ‘Brahmakshatrī’, as the Sena kings were in later times. Buddhism was scarcely known in his country, which did not contain a single monastery.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-018" href="#sec-31-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Pāla dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Practically nothing more is on record concerning the political history of Kāmarūpa for several centuries. The kingdom was included in the dominions of some of the Pāla kings of Bengal, and Kumārapāla, a member of that dynasty, in the twelfth century appointed his minister Vaidyadeva as ruler of the province with royal powers.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-019" href="#sec-31-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Āhōms.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Early in the thirteenth century, about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1228, the invasions of the Shān tribe named Āhōm began. Gradually the Āhōm chiefs made themselves masters of the country, and established a dynasty which lasted until the British occupation in 1825.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-020" href="#sec-31-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The dynastic history of Kāmarūpa, being only of local interest, need not be considered further.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Religion.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The claims which the province can fairly make on the respectful attention of the outer world rest on other grounds. It is a gate through which successive hordes of immigrants from the great hive of the Mongolian race in Western China have poured into the plains of India, and many of the resident tribes still are almost pure Mongolians. The religion of such tribes is of more than local concern, because it supplies the clue to the strange Tantric developments of both Buddhism and Hinduism which are so characteristic of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-431" data-page="431" data-folio="385" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n430_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 385">[p. 385]</button> mediaeval and modern Bengal. The temple of Kāmākhyā near Gauhāti is one of the most sacred shrines of the Sākta Hindus, the worshippers of the female forms of deity, while the whole country is renowned in Hindu legend as a land of magic and witchcraft. The old tribal beliefs are being abandoned gradually in favour of extreme, or even fanatical, Hindu orthodoxy, and the history of Assam offers many examples of the process by which Brahman priests have established their influence over non-Aryan chiefs step by step, and drawn them within the roomy fold of Hinduism. All the various methods of conversion and absorption enumerated by Sir Alfred Lyall and Sir H. Risley have been adopted from time to time.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-021" href="#sec-31-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Muhammadan attacks.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Another good claim to notice is based upon the fact that Assam is one of the few Indian provinces the inhabitants of which successfully beat back the flowing tide of Muhammadan conquest, and maintained their independence in spite of repeated attempts to subvert it. The only Musalman invasion of Kāmarūpa which comes within the limits of the period treated in this volume is the expedition rashly undertaken in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1204–5 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 601) by the son of Bakhtyār, Muhammad, the conqueror of Bengal and Bihār. He advanced northwards along the bank of the Karatōya river, which then formed the western frontier of Kāmarūpa, and succeeded in penetrating into the mountains to the north of Darjeeling, but being unable to obtain any secure foothold, was obliged to retreat. His retirement was disastrous. The people of Kāmarūpa having broken down the great stone bridge of many arches, which was the only means by which he could cross the river in safety, nearly all his men were drowned. The leader of the expedition managed to swim across with about a hundred horsemen, and then fell ill from distress at his failure. Next year, <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1205–6 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 602), he was assassinated.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-022" href="#sec-31-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Subsequent Muhammadan incursions <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-432" data-page="432" data-folio="386" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n431_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 386">[p. 386]</button> were equally unsuccessful. The Muslim historian, who accompanied Mīr Jumla on his invasion of the country in 1662, expresses in striking phrases the horror with which the country and its people were regarded by strangers.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-023" href="#sec-31-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The kingdom retained its autonomy until 1816, when the Burmese appeared and occupied the country until 1824. They were expelled by British troops, and early in 1826 Assam became a province of the Indian empire.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">IV</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Kashmīr</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kashmīr: early history.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A detailed account of the history of Kashmīr would fill a volume; in this place a brief notice of some of the leading passages will suffice. The valley had been included in the Maurya empire in the time of Asoka, and again in the Kushān dominion in the days of Kanishka and Huvishka. Harsha, although not strong enough to annex Kashmīr, was yet able to compel the king to surrender a cherished relic, an alleged tooth of Buddha, which was carried off to Kanauj. The authentic chronicles of the kingdom begin with the Karkota dynasty, which was founded by Durlabhavardhana during Harsha's lifetime. Hiuen Tsang spent two years in Kashmīr, from about May 631 to April 633, and was received with distinguished honour by the unnamed reigning king, presumably Durlabhavardhana. That prince and his son Durlabhaka are credited with long reigns.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 720. Chandrāpīḍa; <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 733– 69. Muktāpīḍa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The latter was succeeded by his three sons in order; the eldest of whom, Chandrāpīḍa, received investiture as king from the Emperor of China in 720; by whom the third son Muktāpīḍa, also known as Lalitāditya, was similarly honoured in 733. This prince, who is said to have reigned for thirty-six years, extended the power of Kashmīr far beyond its normal mountain limits, and about the year 740 inflicted a crushing defeat upon Yasovarman, king of Kanauj.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-024" href="#sec-31-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> He also vanquished the Tibetans, Bhūtias, and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-435" data-page="435" data-folio="387" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n434_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 387">[p. 387]</button> the Turks on the Indus. His memory has been perpetuated by the famous Mārttāṇḍa temple of the Sun, built by him, and still existing. The acts of this king, and all that he did, with something more, are set forth at large in Kalhaṇa's chronicle.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-434"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="434" data-folio="386" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n433_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 386">[p. 386]</button></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-016"><img src="/figures/early-history-of-india-1924/fig-016_p0434.webp" alt="THE MĀRTĀṆḌA TEMPLE OF THE SUN, KASHMĪR" loading="lazy"><figcaption>THE MĀRTĀṆḌA TEMPLE OF THE SUN, KASHMĪR</figcaption></figure><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Jayāpīda; close of the eighth century.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Jayāpīda, or Vinayāditya, the grandson of Muktāpīda, is credited with even more adventures than those ascribed to his grandfather. Probably it is true that he defeated and dethroned the king of Kanauj, apparently Vajrāyudha. But the romantic tale of his visit <em>incognito</em> to the capital of Pauṇḍravardhana in Bengal, the modern Rājshāhi District, then the seat of government of a king named Jayanta, unknown to sober history, seems to be purely imaginary. The legend of his expedition against a king of Nepāl, with the strange name Aramudi, of his capture and imprisonment in a stone castle, and of his marvellous escape, equally belongs to the domain of romance. The details of the acts of cruelty and oppression, due to avarice, which disgraced the later years of his reign, read like matters of fact, and unhappily are quite in accordance with the low moral standard of most of the rulers of Kashmīr. The chronicler closes his narrative with the following quaint comment :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>'Such was for thirty-one years the reign of this famous king, who could not restrain his will. Princes and fishes, when their thirst is excited by riches and impure water respectively, leave their place and follow evil ways, with such result that they are brought into the strong net of death—the former by changes which fate dictates, and the latter by troops of fishermen.'</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The substantial existence of Jayāpīda is testified by the survival of multitudes of exceedingly barbarous coins inscribed with his title Vinayāditya.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-025" href="#sec-31-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">A.D. 855– 83. Avantivarman.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reign of Avantivarman, in the latter part of the ninth century, was notable for his enlightened patronage of literature, and for the beneficent schemes of drainage and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-436" data-page="436" data-folio="388" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n435_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 388">[p. 388]</button> irrigation carried out by Suyya, his minister of public works.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-026" href="#sec-31-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 883– 902. Śankaravarman.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The next king, Sankaravarman, distinguished himself in war ; but is chiefly remembered as the author of an ingenious system of fiscal oppression, and the plunderer of temple treasures. The details of his exactions are worth reading as proving the capacity for unlimited and ruthless extortion of an Oriental despot without a conscience.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-027" href="#sec-31-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">End of the Shāhiya dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During his reign the last of the Turki Shāhiya kings, the descendants of Kanishka, was overthrown by the Brahman Lalliya. The Turkī Shāhiya kings had ruled in Kābul until the capture of that city by the Arab general Yakūb-i-Lāis in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 870 (<span class="sc">A. H.</span> 256).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-028" href="#sec-31-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> After that date the capital was shifted to Ohind, on the Indus. The dynasty founded by Lalliya, known as that of the Hindu Shāhiyas, lasted until <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1021, when it was extirpated by the Muhammadans.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-029" href="#sec-31-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Famine in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 917– 18.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the reign of the child-king Pārtha and his father Pangu, the regent, an awful famine occurred in the year <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 917–18, thus described by the Brahman historian of a Hindu government :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘ One could scarcely see the water in the Vitastā (Jihlam), entirely covered as the river was with corpses soaked and swollen by the water in which they had long been lying. The land became densely covered with bones in all directions, until it was like one great burial-ground, causing terror to all beings. The king’s ministers and the Tantrins (Praetorian guards) became wealthy, as they amassed riches by selling stores of rice at high prices. The king would take that person as minister who raised the sums due on the Tantrins’ bills, by selling the subjects in such a condition. As one might look from his hot bath-room upon all the people outside distressed by the wind and rain of a downpour in the forest, thus for a long time the wretched Pangu, keeping in his palace, praised his own comfort while he saw the people in misery.’ <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-030" href="#sec-31-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This gruesome picture may give cause for reflection to some critics of modern methods of famine relief.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-437"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="437" data-folio="389" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n436_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 389">[p. 389]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Unmattāvanti, <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 937– 9.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Pārtha chastised his people with whips, but his son Unmattāvanti, ‘who was worse than wicked,’ chastised them with scorpions. ‘With difficulty’, sighs the chronicler, ‘I get my song to proceed, since from fear of touching the evil of this king’s story it keeps back like a frightened mare.’ Parricide was one of his many crimes. The details of his brutalities are too disgusting for quotation. Happily his reign was short, and he died the victim of a painful disease in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 939.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-031" href="#sec-31-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 950– 1003. Queen Diddā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the latter half of the tenth century, power was in the hands of an unscrupulous queen, named Diddā, the granddaughter of a Shāhiya king, who, first as a queenconsort, then as regent, and ultimately as sovereign for twenty-three years, misgoverned the unhappy state for half a century.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1003–28. Saṅgrāma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In the reign of her nephew, Saṅgrāma, the kingdom suffered an attack from Mahmūd of Ghaznī; and, although its troops were defeated by the invader, preserved its independence, which was protected by the inaccessibility of the mountain barriers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1063–89. Kalasa. <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1089– 1101. Harsha.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the second half of the eleventh century, Kashmīr, which has been generally unfortunate in its rulers, endured unspeakable miseries at the hands of the tyrants Kalasa and Harsha. The latter, who was evidently insane, imitated Sankaravarman in the practice of plundering temples, and rightly came to a miserable end. Few countries can rival the long Kashmīr list of kings and queens who gloried in shameless lust, fiendish cruelty, and pitiless misrule.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1339. Muhammadan local dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A local Muhammadan dynasty obtained power in 1339, and the religion of Islam gradually spread in the valley during the fourteenth century; but the natural defences of the kingdom effectually guarded it against the ambition of the sovereigns of India, until Akbar conquered it in 1587, and incorporated it in the Moghal empire.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-032" href="#sec-31-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-438"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="438" data-folio="390" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n437_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 390">[p. 390]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3">V</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>The kingdoms of Kanauj (Pañchāla), the Panjāb, Ajmēr, Delhi, and Gwālior; Muhammadan conquest of Hindustan.</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><strong>Kanauj city.</strong></aside></div><p class="prose-p">Before proceeding to discuss the history of the kingdom of Kanauj, it will be well to give some account of the famous capital city, which is now represented by a petty Muhammadan country town (N. lat. 27° 3′, E. long. 79° 56′) in the Farrukhābād District of the United Provinces. Kanauj was of high antiquity. It is mentioned in several passages of the <em>Mahābhārata</em>, and alluded to by Patanjali in the second century <span class="sc">B.C.</span> as a well-known place. It has been so completely destroyed that nothing beyond rubbish heaps remains to testify to the former existence of its gorgeous temples, monasteries, and palaces. Commentators usually take it for granted that Kanauj is mentioned twice, under the variant names of Kanagora and Kanogiza,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-033" href="#sec-31-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> in Ptolemy's <em>Geography</em>, written about <span class="sc">A.D.</span> 140, but there is little reason to warrant the belief. The first certain mention of the city with any descriptive details is in the <em>Travels</em> of the Chinese pilgrim Fa-hien, who visited Kanauj about <span class="sc">A.D.</span> 405, during the reign of Chandra-gupta II, Vikramāditya. His remark that the city possessed only two Buddhist monasteries of the Hīnayāna school and one <em>stūpa</em> suggests that it was not of much importance at the beginning of the fifth century.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-034" href="#sec-31-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Probably it grew under the patronage of the Gupta kings, but the great development of the city clearly was due to its selection by Harsha for his capital. When Hiuen Tsang stayed there, in 636 and 643, a marked change had occurred since Fa-hien's time. The later pilgrim, instead of two monasteries, found upwards of a hundred such institutions, crowded by more than 10,000 brethren of both the great schools. Hinduism flourished as well as Buddhism, and could show more than two hundred temples, with thousands <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-439" data-page="439" data-folio="391" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n438_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 391">[p. 391]</button> of worshippers. The city, which was strongly fortified, then extended along the east bank of the Ganges for about 4 miles, and was adorned with lovely gardens and clear tanks. The inhabitants were well-to-do, including some families of great wealth ; they dressed in silk, and were skilled in learning and the arts.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-035" href="#sec-31-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Captured and destroyed.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although Kanauj had been captured several times by hostile armies during the ninth and tenth centuries, it recovered quickly from its wounds, and when Mahmūd appeared before its walls, at the end of <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1018, was still a great and stately city, defended by seven distinct forts or fortifications and reputed to contain 10,000 temples. The Sultan destroyed the temples, but seems to have spared the city. The removal of the capital of Panchāla to Bārī must have greatly reduced the population and importance of Kanauj, although it revived to some extent under the rule of the Gaharwār Rājas in the twelfth century. The subjugation of Rāja Jaichand’s territory, including the city, in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1194 (<span class="sc">A. H.</span> 590), by Shihāb-ud-dīn, reduced it to insignificance for ever. Its final destruction was the work of Shēr Shah, who built a new town close by, called Shēr Sūr, to commemorate his victory over Humāyūn in 1540. The Muhammadan historian who chronicles the event observes that he could not find any satisfactory reason for the destruction of the old city, and that the act was very unpopular.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-036" href="#sec-31-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kingdom of Pañchāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Kanauj, although it twice attained the dignity of being the capital of Northern India, for the first time under Harsha in the seventh century, and for the second time under Mihira Bhoja and Mahendrapāla in the ninth and tenth centuries, was primarily the capital of the kingdom of Panchāla. According to the story told in the <em>Mahābhārata</em>, Northern Panchāla, with its capital Ahichchhatra, fell to the share of Drona, while Southern Panchāla, with its capital Kāmpilya, became the kingdom of Drupada. Ahichchhatra, the modern <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-440" data-page="440" data-folio="392" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n439_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 392">[p. 392]</button> Rāmnagar in the Barēli (Bareilly) District, was still a considerable town when visited by Hiuen Tsang in the seventh century. Little is known about the history of Kāmpilya, apparently the modern Kampil in the Farrukhābād District.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-037" href="#sec-31-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Both the ancient capitals were thrown into obscurity by the rapid development of Kanauj under Harsha's rule, and after his time that city was the undisputed capital of Panchāla.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Anarchy after Harsha's death.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Harsha's death, in <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 647, was followed by a period of disturbance and anarchy throughout his wide dominions. We do not know what happened to the kingdom of Panchāla immediately after the suppression of the usurper, about <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 650, by the Chinese ambassador with the help of his Nepalese and Tibetan allies, as related in the thirteenth chapter.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rājas of Kanauj in the eighth century.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">After Harsha's death the earliest known king of Kanauj was Yasovarman, who sent an embassy to China in <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 731,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-038" href="#sec-31-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> and nine or ten years later was dethroned and slain by Lalitāditya Muktāpīda of Kashmir.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-039" href="#sec-31-fn-039" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-039" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> In the history of Sanskrit literature Yasovarman's name holds an honoured place as that of the patron of Bhavabhūti, the famous author of the <em>Mālatīmādhava</em>, and of Vākpatirāja, a less renowned author, who wrote in Prākrit. The next occupant of the throne of Kanauj apparently was Vajrāyudha, who, like his predecessor, suffered the fate of defeat and dethronement by Lalitāditya's son, Jayāpīda.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-040" href="#sec-31-fn-040" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-040" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> Similar ill-luck attended his successor, Indrāyudha, who is known to have been reigning in <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 783, and was dethroned, about <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 810, by Dharmapāla, king of Bengal and Bihār. The eastern monarch, while probably insisting on a right to homage and tribute, did not keep the administration of Panchāla in his own hands, but entrusted it to Chakrāyudha, presumably <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-441" data-page="441" data-folio="393" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n440_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 393">[p. 393]</button> a relative of the defeated rāja. The new ruler was consecrated with the consent of the kings of all the neighbouring states.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-041" href="#sec-31-fn-041" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-041" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> His fortune was no better than that of his predecessors. About <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 816 he was deprived of his throne by Nāgabhaṭa, the ambitious king of the Gurjara-Pratihāra kingdom in Rājputāna, the capital of which was at Bhilmāl.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-042" href="#sec-31-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nāgabhaṭa and Rāmabhadra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Nāgabhaṭa presumably transferred the head-quarters of his government to Kanauj, which certainly was the capital of his successors for many generations, and so again became for a considerable time the premier city of Northern India. During the reign of Nāgabhaṭa the chronic warfare between the Gurjaras, descendants of foreign invaders, and the Rāshtrakūtas (Rāthōrs) of the Deccan, representing the indigenous ruling races, continued, and the southern king, Govinda III, claims to have won a victory over his northern rival early in the ninth century.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-043" href="#sec-31-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Nothing particular is recorded about Nāgabhaṭa's successor, Rāmabhadra (Rāmadeva), who reigned from about <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 834 to 840.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mihira Bhoja.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The next king, Rāmabhadra's son Mihira, usually known by his title Bhoja, enjoyed a long reign of about half a century (<em>c.</em> 840–90), and beyond question was a very powerful monarch, whose dominions may be called an 'empire' without exaggeration. They certainly included the Cis-Sutlaj districts of the Panjāb, most of Rājputāna, the greater part, if not the whole, of the present United Provinces of Agra and Oudh, and the Gwālior territory. The next two kings being known to have held the remote province of Surāshtra, or Kāthiāwār, in the extreme west, the possession of which implies control over Gujarāt and Mālava or Avanti, it is highly probable that these distant regions also were <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-442" data-page="442" data-folio="394" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n441_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 394">[p. 394]</button> subject to the sway of Bhoja. On the east his dominions abutted on the realm of Devapāla, king of Bengal and Bihār, which he invaded successfully; on the north-west his boundary probably was the Sutlaj river; on the west the lost Hakrā or Wahindah river separated his territories from those of his enemies, the Muhammadan chiefs of Sind; on the south-west his powerful Rāshtrakūta rival, the ally of the Muhammadans, kept his armies continually on the alert; while on the south his next neighbour was the growing Chandēl kingdom of Jejākabhukti, the modern Bundelkhand, which probably acknowledged his suzerainty.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-044" href="#sec-31-fn-044" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-044" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Bhoja liked to pose as an incarnation of Vishnu, and therefore assumed the title of <em>Ādi Varāha</em>, 'the primaeval boar,' one of the incarnations of the god. Base silver coins inscribed with this title are exceedingly common in Northern India, and by their abundance attest the long duration and wide extension of Bhoja's rule.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-045" href="#sec-31-fn-045" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-045" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Unfortunately no Megasthenes or Bāna has left a record of the nature of his internal government, and it is impossible to compare the polity of Bhoja with that of his great forerunners.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mahendrapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Bhoja's son and successor, Mahendrapāla (Mahendrāyudha) (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 890–908) preserved unimpaired the extensive heritage received from his father, and ruled all Northern India, except the Panjāb and Indus valley, from the borders of Bihār (Magadha) to the shore of the Arabian sea. Inscriptions of his eighth and ninth years found at Gayā seem to prove that Magadha was included in the Parihār (Pratihāra) dominions for some time. His teacher (<em>Guru</em>) was the celebrated poet Rājasekhara, author of the <em>Karpūra-mañjarī</em> play and other works, who continued to reside at the court of Mahendrapāla's younger son.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-046" href="#sec-31-fn-046" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-046" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-443"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="443" data-folio="395" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n442_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 395">[p. 395]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bhoja II and Mahīpāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The throne was occupied for two or three years by Bhoja II, elder son of Mahendrapāla, who died early, and was succeeded by his half-brother, Mahīpāla (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 910–40).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-047" href="#sec-31-fn-047" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-047" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The beginning of the decline and fall of the empire of Kanauj dates from his reign. In <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 916 the armies of the Rāshtrakūta king, Indra III, once more captured Kanauj, and gave a severe blow to the power of the Pratīhāra dynasty.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-048" href="#sec-31-fn-048" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-048" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Surāshtra, which was still subject to Mahīpāla in 914,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-049" href="#sec-31-fn-049" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-049" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> probably was then lost along with other remote provinces, in consequence of the successes gained by the southern monarch. Indra III not being in a position to hold Kanauj, Mahīpāla recovered his capital with the aid of the Chandēl king, and probably other allies.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-050" href="#sec-31-fn-050" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-050" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Devapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The waning power of Kanauj and the waxing strength of Jejākabhukti are shown by the incident that king Devapāla of Kanauj (<em>c.</em> 940–55) was obliged to surrender a muchprized image of Vishnu to the Chandēl king, Yasovarman, who enshrined it in one of the finest temples at Khajurāho.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-051" href="#sec-31-fn-051" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-051" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> Yasovarman had established his power by the occupation of the strong fortress of Kālanjar, and no doubt became absolutely independent of Kanauj. In the reign of Dhanga, the successor of Yasovarman, the Jumna is known to have formed the boundary between the territories of Panchāla and those of Jejākabhukti.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vijayapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Devapāla was succeeded by his brother, Vijayapāla (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 960–90),<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-052" href="#sec-31-fn-052" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-052" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> whose reign is marked by the loss of Gwālior, the ancient possession of his house, which was captured by a Kachchhwāha (Kachchhapaghāta) chief named <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-444" data-page="444" data-folio="396" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n443_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 396">[p. 396]</button> Vajradāman,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-053" href="#sec-31-fn-053" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-053" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> the founder of a local dynasty which held the fortress until <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 1128. The establishment of the Solankī (Chaulukya) kingdom of Anhilwāra in Gujarāt by Mūlarāja, about the middle of the tenth century, shows that the king of Kanauj no longer had any concern with Western India.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-054" href="#sec-31-fn-054" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-054" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The Gwālior chieftain became a feudatory of the Chandēl monarchy, which, under Dhanga (<em>c.</em> 1000–1050), evidently was stronger than its rival of Kanauj.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Muhammadan invasions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At this period the politics of the Hindu Rājpūt states of Northern India became complicated by the intrusion of Muhammadan invaders. The Arab conquest of Sind, in <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 712, did not seriously affect the kingdoms of the interior. The Arabs maintained friendly relations on the whole with their powerful Rāshtrakūta neighbours on the south, and their attacks on the dominions of the Gurjara kings of Rājputāna and Kanauj do not seem ever to have exceeded the proportions of frontier raids. But now the armies of Islam began to appear in more formidable fashion through the north-western passes, the gates which had so often admitted the enemies of India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sabuktigīn and Jaipāl.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In those days a large kingdom comprising the upper valley of the Indus and most of the Panjāb to the north of Sind, extending westward to the mountains and eastward to the Hakrā river, was governed by a Rāja named Jaipāl, whose capital was at Bathindah (Bhatinda), the Tabarhind of Muhammadan histories, now in the Patiālā State, and for many centuries an important fortress on the military road connecting Mūltān with India proper.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-055" href="#sec-31-fn-055" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-055" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Sabuktigīn, the Amīr of Ghaznī, made his first raid into Indian territory in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-445" data-page="445" data-folio="397" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n444_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 397">[p. 397]</button> <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 986–7 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 376). Two years later Jaipāl retaliated by an invasion of the Amīr's territory, but, being defeated, was compelled to accept a treaty binding him to pay a large sum in cash, and to surrender a number of elephants and four fortresses to the west of the Indus. Jaipāl having broken the compact, Sabuktigīn punished him by the devastation of the frontier and the annexation of Lamghān (Jalālābād). Soon afterwards (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 991) Jaipāl made a final effort to save his country by organizing a great confederacy of Hindu princes, including Ganda, the Chandēl king, Rājyapāla, then the king of Kanauj, and others. The vast host thus collected was disastrously defeated in or near the Kurram (Kurmah) valley, and Peshāwar was occupied by the Muhammadans. Jaipāl, who was again defeated in November, 1001, by Sultan Mahmūd, committed suicide, and was succeeded by his son, Anandpāl,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-056" href="#sec-31-fn-056" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-056" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> who, like his father, joined a confederacy of the Hindu powers under the supreme command of Vīsaladeva, the Chauhān Rājā of Ajmēr. In spite of assistance from the powerful Khokhar tribe of the Panjāb, the Hindus again sustained a heavy defeat.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Rājyapāla ; Sultan Mahmūd.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">At Kanauj, Vijayapāla had been succeeded by his son Rājyapāla, who took his share in opposing the foreign invader. A few years later (<span class="sc">a. d.</span> 997) the crown of Sabuktigīn descended, after a short interval of dispute, to his son, the famous Sultan Mahmūd, who made it the business of his life to harry the idolaters of India, and carry off their property to Ghaznī. He is computed to have made no less than seventeen expeditions into India. It was his custom to leave his capital in October, and then three months' steady marching brought him into the richest provinces of the interior. Early in January, <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1019, he appeared before Kanauj. Rājyapāla made no serious attempt to defend his capital, and the seven forts which guarded it all <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-446" data-page="446" data-folio="398" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n445_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 398">[p. 398]</button> fell into Mahmūd’s hands in a single day. The conqueror plundered the forts but seems to have spared the city, and quickly returned to Ghaznī laden with booty. Rājyapāla made the best terms that he could obtain, abandoned Kanauj, and retired to Bārī on the other side of the Ganges.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-057" href="#sec-31-fn-057" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-057" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Ganda and Mahmūd.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The pusillanimous submission of Rājyapāla incensed his Hindu allies, who felt that he had betrayed their cause. His fault was sternly punished by an army under the command of Vidhyādhara, heir-apparent of the Chandēl king, Ganda, supported by the forces of his feudatory, the chief of Gwālior, which attacked Kanauj in the spring or summer of <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 1019, soon after the departure of Sultan Mahmūd, and slew Rājyapāla, whose diminished dominions passed under the rule of Trilochanapāla. The Sultan was furious when he heard of the punishment inflicted on a prince whom he regarded as a vassal, and in the autumn of the same year (<span class="sc">a.h.</span> 410) started again from Ghaznī to take vengeance on the Hindu chiefs. Early in <span class="sc">a.d.</span> 1020 he captured Bārī, the new Pratihāra capital, without much difficulty, and then advanced into the Chandēl territory, where Ganda had assembled an apparently formidable force to oppose him. But the heart of the Chandēl king failed him, and, like Rājyapāla, he fled from the field without giving battle. His camp, munitions, and elephants were left a prey to the Sultan, who returned as usual to Ghaznī with heaps of spoil.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-058" href="#sec-31-fn-058" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-058" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Successors of Rājyapāla.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Nothing is known about Trilochanapāla except that he ineffectually resisted Mahmūd’s passage of the Jumna at the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-447" data-page="447" data-folio="399" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n446_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 399">[p. 399]</button> end of 1019 or the beginning of 1020, and made the grant of a village near Allāhābād in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1027.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-059" href="#sec-31-fn-059" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-059" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> A Rāja named Yasahpāla, who is mentioned in an inscription of <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1036, may have been his immediate successor.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-060" href="#sec-31-fn-060" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-060" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Other obscure chiefs continued to be recognized as Rājas of Kanauj, and governed a considerable territory, no doubt in subordination to Muhammadan kings, even after the reduction of Kanauj in 1194. The names of some of these chiefs have been preserved. They seem to have resided at Zafarābād near Jaunpur. But these later chiefs did not belong to the old Gurjara-Pratihāra dynasty, which disappeared utterly. Kanauj had been conquered and occupied, a little before <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1090, by a Rāja of the Gaharwār clan, named Chandradeva, who established his authority certainly over Benares and Ajodhyā, and perhaps over the Delhi territory.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-061" href="#sec-31-fn-061" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-061" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The city of Delhi had been founded about a century earlier, in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 993–4.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-062" href="#sec-31-fn-062" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-062" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gaharwār dynasty of Kanauj.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Gaharwār dynasty, subsequently known as Rāthōr,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-063" href="#sec-31-fn-063" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-063" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> thus founded by Chandradeva, lasted until the subjugation of the Kanauj kingdom by Shihāb-ud-dīn, in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1194</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-448"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="448" data-folio="400" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n447_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 400">[p. 400]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 590). Govindachandra, grandson of Chandradeva, enjoyed a long reign, which included the years <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1104 and 1155. His numerous land grants and widely distributed coins prove that he succeeded to a large extent in restoring the glories of Kanauj, and in making himself a power of considerable importance.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-064" href="#sec-31-fn-064" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-064" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The grandson of Govindachandra was Jayachchandra, renowned in the popular Hindī poems and tales of Northern India as Rāja Jaichand, whose daughter was carried off by the gallant Rāi Pithōra of Ajmēr. He was known to the Muhammadan historian as the king of Benares, which, perhaps, may be regarded as having been his capital, and was reputed the greatest king in India. It is alleged that his territory extended from the borders of China to the province of Mālwā, and from the sea to within ten days' journey of Lahore, but it is difficult to believe that it can have been really so extensive. Shihāb-ud-dīn met him at Chandāwar in the Etawah District near the Jumna, and having defeated his huge host with immense slaughter, in which the Rāja was included, passed on to Benares, which he plundered, carrying off the treasure on 1,400 camels.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-065" href="#sec-31-fn-065" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-065" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Thus ends the story of the independent kingdom of Kanauj. When the rājas of the Gaharwār line died out, their place was taken by chiefs of the Chandēl clan from Mahoba, who became the local rulers of Kanauj for eight generations.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-066" href="#sec-31-fn-066" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-066" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Chauhāns of Sāmbhar and</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Ajmēr; Delhi.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Inscriptions record the genealogy of a long line of Rājpūt kings belonging to the Chauhān (Chāhumāna) clan who governed the principality of Sāmbhar (Sākambharī) in <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-449" data-page="449" data-folio="401" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n448_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 401">[p. 401]</button> Rājputāna, to which Ajmēr was attached. Only two of these chiefs demand notice. Vigraha-rāja, in the middle of the twelfth century, extended his ancestral dominions considerably, and is erroneously alleged to have conquered Delhi from a chief of the Tomara clan. That chief was a descendant of Ānangapāla, who, a century earlier, had built the Red Fort, where the Kutb mosque now stands, and thus given permanence to the city, which had been founded in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 993–4.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-067" href="#sec-31-fn-067" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-067" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Europeans are so accustomed to associate the name of Delhi with the sovereignty of India that they do not easily realize the fact that Delhi is among the most modern of the great Indian cities. Vague legends, it is true, irradiate the lands along the bank of the Jumna near the village of Indarpat with the traditional glories of the prehistoric Indraprastha, and these stories may or may not have some substantial basis. But, as an historical city, Delhi dates only from the time of Ānangapāla in the middle of the eleventh century. The celebrated iron pillar, on which the eulogy of a powerful king named Chandra, who lived in the fourth century, is incised, was removed by the Tomara chief from its original position, probably at Mathurā, and set up in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1052 as an adjunct to a group of temples, from the materials of which the Muhammadans afterwards constructed the great mosque.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-068" href="#sec-31-fn-068" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-068" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vigraha-rāja.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Vigraha-rāja (IV) was a man of considerable distinction. Some years ago, during the progress of repairs executed at the principal mosque of Ajmēr, six slabs of polished black marble were discovered bearing inscriptions in Sanskrit and Prākrit, which on examination proved to be large portions <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-450" data-page="450" data-folio="402" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n449_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 402">[p. 402]</button> of two unknown dramas. One of these, the <em>Lalita-Vigraha</em>-<em>rāja-nāṭaka</em>, was composed in honour of Vigraha-rāja, while the other, the <em>Harakali-nāṭaka</em>, professes to be the composition of that prince himself.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-069" href="#sec-31-fn-069" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-069" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Prithivī-rāja, or Rāi Pithōra.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">His nephew was Prithivī-rāja, Prithīrāj, or Rāi Pithōra, lord of Sāmbhar and Ajmēr, famous in song and story as a chivalrous lover and doughty champion. His fame as a bold lover rests upon his daring abduction of the not unwilling daughter of Jaichand, the Gaharwār Rāja of Kanauj, which occurred in or about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1175. His reputation as a general is securely founded upon his defeat of the Chandēl Rāja, Parmāl, and the capture of Mahoba in 1182, as well as upon gallant resistance to the flood of Muhammadan invasion. Indeed, Rāi Pithōra may be described with justice as the popular hero of Northern India, and his exploits in love and war are the subject of rude epics and bardic lays to this day.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-070" href="#sec-31-fn-070" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-070" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-451"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="451" data-folio="403" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n450_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 403">[p. 403]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Battle of Tarāin or Talāwarī.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The dread inspired by the victorious Musalman army under the command of Shihāb-ud-dīn or Muhammad of Ghōr, who was now undisputed master of the greater part of the Panjāb, constrained the jarring states of Northern India to lay aside their quarrels and combine for a moment against the foreign foe. At first fortune smiled on the Indians; and in <span class="sc">A.D.</span> 1191 (<span class="sc">A. H.</span> 587) Prithivī-rāja succeeded in inflicting a severe defeat upon the invaders at Tarāīn or Talāwarī between Thānēsar and Karnāl, which forced them to retire beyond the Indus. A year later, in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1192 (<span class="sc">A. H.</span> 588), the Sultan, having returned with a fresh force, again encountered on the same field Prithivī-rāja, who was at the head of an immense host, swollen by contingents from numerous confederate princes. A vigorous charge by twelve thousand well-armed Musalman horsemen repeated the lesson given by Alexander long ages before, and demonstrated the inability of a mob of Indian militia to stand the onset of trained cavalry. Prithivī-rāja, having been taken prisoner, was executed in cold blood, and the wretched inhabitants of his capital Ajmēr were either put to the sword or sold into slavery.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-071" href="#sec-31-fn-071" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-071" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">supported by the inscriptions. The correct lineage of Pṛithivīrāja according to the <em>Pṛithivīrājavijaya</em> is:—</p></div><figure class="archival-plate" id="fig-017"><img src="/figures/early-history-of-india-1924/fig-017_p0451.webp" alt="Genealogical tree of Arṇorāja and descendants (Unnamed son, Vigraha-rāja, Someśvara, Pṛithivī-rāja I, Pṛithivī-rāja II, Hari-rāja)" loading="lazy"></figure><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Chand's statement that Rāi Pithōra (Prithirāj) was the son of the daughter of Ānangapāla, king of Delhi, is doubtful. The single imperfect MS. of the <em>Pṛithivīrāja vijaya</em> has been described and summarized by Har Bilas Sarda in <em>J. R. A. S.</em>, 1913, pp. 259–81, with references to earlier notices of the work. He uses the spelling <em>Pṛithvī</em>. The truth of the assertion that Vigraha-rāja wrested Delhi from the Tomaras is extremely doubtful</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Bühler, <em>Proc. A. S. B.</em>, 1893, p. 94). It seems to be contradicted by verse 22 of the Bijoli inscription (<em>J. A. S. B.</em>, part i, vol. lv (1886), p. 31).</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-452"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="452" data-folio="404" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n451_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 404">[p. 404]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Conquest of Hindustan.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1193 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 589), Delhi fell. Kanauj does not seem to have been molested, but must have come under the control of the invaders. Benares, the holy citadel of Hinduism, in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1194 became the prize of the victors, who could now feel confident that the final triumph of the arms of Islam over 'the land of the Brahmans' was assured. The surrender of Gwālior in 1196, the capture in 1197 of Anhilwāra the capital of Gujarāt, and the capitulation of Kālanjar in 1203 completed the reduction of Upper India, and when Shihāb-ud-dīn died in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1205–6 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 602), he—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>'Held, in different degrees of subjection, the whole of Hindustan Proper, except Mālwā and some contiguous districts. Sind and Bengal were either entirely subdued, or in rapid course of reduction. On Gujarāt he had no hold, except what is implied in the possession of the capital (Anhilwāra or Nahrwāla). Much of Hindustan was immediately under his officers, and the rest under dependent or at least tributary princes. The desert and some of the mountains were left independent from neglect'.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-072" href="#sec-31-fn-072" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-072" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Migration of Gaharwārs.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">An important consequence of the Muslim conquest of Kanauj was the migration of the bulk of the Gaharwār clan <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-453" data-page="453" data-folio="405" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n452_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 405">[p. 405]</button> to the deserts of Mārwār in Rājputāna, where they settled and became known as Rāṭhōrs. The state so founded, now generally designated by the name of its capital, Jodhpur, is one of the most important principalities of Rājputāna.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-073" href="#sec-31-fn-073" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-073" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Similar clan movements, necessitated by the pressure of Muhammadan armies, which were frequent at this period, account to a large extent for the existing distribution of the Rājpūt clans.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">VI</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>The Chandēls of Jejākabhukti and the Kalachuris of Chedi</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Jejākabhukti and Chedi.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The ancient name of the province between the Jumna and Narmadā, now known as Bundelkhand, and partly included in the United Provinces of Agra and Oudh, was Jejākabhukti.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-074" href="#sec-31-fn-074" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-074" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The extensive region, farther to the south, which is now under the administration of the Chief Commissioner of the Central Provinces, nearly corresponds with the old kingdom of Chedi. In the mediaeval history of these countries two dynasties—the Chandēls of Jejākabhukti and the Kalachuris of Chedi—which occasionally were connected by marriage, and constantly were in contact one with the other, whether as friends or enemies, are conspicuous. From the beginning of the eleventh century the Chedi country was divided into two kingdoms, Western Chedi, or Dāhāla, with its capital at Tripura, near Jabalpur, and Eastern Chedi, or Mahākosala, with its capital at Ratanpur.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Predecessors of the Chandēls.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Chandēls, like several other dynasties, first come into notice early in the ninth century, when Nannuka Chandēl, about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 831, overthrew a Parihār chieftain, and became lord of the southern parts of Jejākabhukti. The Parihārs of Bundelkhand, like their brethren of Bhinmāl, must have belonged to the Gurjara or Gūjar group of tribes which had <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-454" data-page="454" data-folio="406" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n453_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 406">[p. 406]</button> entered India in the sixth century. The Parihār capital had been at Mau-Sahaniya, between Nowgong (Naugāon) and Chhatarpur.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-075" href="#sec-31-fn-075" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-075" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The predecessors of the Parihārs were Gaharwār Rājas, members of the clan which afterwards gave Kanauj the line of kings commonly miscalled Rāthōrs.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Chandēl temples and lakes.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Chandēl princes were great builders, and beautified their chief towns, Mahoba, Kālanjar, and Khajurāho, with many magnificent temples and lovely lakes, formed by throwing massive dams across the openings between the hills. In this practice of building embankments and constructing lakes the Chandēls were imitators of the Gaharwārs, who are credited with the formation of some of the most charming lakes in Bundelkhand.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Yaśovarman.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Chandēls, who appear to have been Hinduized Gonds, closely connected with another autochthonous tribe, the Bhars, first acquired a petty principality near Chhatarpur, and gradually advanced northwards until the Jumna became the frontier between their dominions and those of Kanauj. The earlier Rājas may have been subject to the suzerainty of Bhoja and Mahendrapāla, the powerful kings of Panchāla, but in the first half of the tenth century the Chandēls certainly had become independent. Harsha Chandēl, aided perhaps by other allies, helped Mahīpāla to recover the throne of Kanauj from which he had been driven by Indra III Rāshtrakūta in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 916. Harsha's son and successor, Yasovarman, whose power had been greatly enhanced by the occupation of the fortress of Kālanjar, was strong enough to compel Mahīpāla's successor, Devapāla, to surrender a valuable image of Vishnu, which the Chandēl king wanted for a temple built by him at Khajurāho.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 950– 99. Dhanga.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">King Dhanga, son of Yasovarman (<span class="sc">A. D.</span> 950–99), who lived to an age of more than a hundred years, was the most notable of his family. Some of the grandest temples at Khajurāho are due to his munificence, and he took an active part in the politics of his time. In <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 989 or 990 he joined the league formed by Jaipāl, king of the Panjāb, to resist Sabuktigīn, and shared with the Rājas of Ajmēr and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-455" data-page="455" data-folio="407" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n454_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 407">[p. 407]</button> Kanauj in the disastrous defeat which the allies suffered between Bannū and Ghaznī, in or near the Kurram (Kurmah) valley.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-076" href="#sec-31-fn-076" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-076" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 999– 1025. Ganda.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">When Mahmud of Ghaznī threatened to overrun India, Dhanga's son Ganda (999–1025) joined the new confederacy of Hindu princes organized by Ānand Pāl, son of Jaipāl, king of the Panjāb, in 1008–9 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 399), which also failed to stay the hand of the invader. Ten years later, as already narrated, Ganda's son attacked Kanauj and killed the Rāja, Rājyapāla, who had made terms with the Muhammadans; but early in 1023 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 413) was himself compelled to surrender the strong fortress of Kālanjar to Mahmūd,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-077" href="#sec-31-fn-077" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-077" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> who, however, did not retain it or any of his conquests in the interior of India beyond the Panjāb.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1015–70. Gāngēyadeva and Karṇadeva Kalachuri.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Gāngēyadeva Kalachuri of Chedi (<em>circa</em> 1015–40), the contemporary of Ganda and his successors, was an able and ambitious prince, who aimed at attaining the position of paramount power in Upper India, and succeeded to a considerable extent. In 1019 his suzerainty was recognized in distant Tirhūt.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-078" href="#sec-31-fn-078" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-078" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> His projects of aggrandizement were taken up and proceeded with by his son Karnadeva (<em>circa</em> 1040– 70), who joined Bhīma, king of Gujarāt, in crushing Bhoja, the learned king of Mālwā, about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1060. He had attacked the Pāla king of Magadha at an earlier date, about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1035.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1049– 1100. Kīrtivarman Chandēl.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">But some years later Karnadeva was taught the lesson of the mutability of fortune by suffering defeats inflicted by several hostile kings, and notably one at the hands of Kīrtivarman Chandēl (1049–1100), who widely extended the dominion of his house. The earliest extant specimens of the Chandēl coinage were struck by this king in imitation of the issues of Gāngēyadeva of Chedi. Kīrtivarman is also memorable in literary history as the patron of the curious <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-456" data-page="456" data-folio="408" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n455_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 408">[p. 408]</button> allegorical play entitled the <em>Prabodha-chandrodaya</em>, or ‘Rise of the Moon of Intellect’, which was performed at his court in or about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1065, and gives in dramatic form a clever exposition of the Vedānta system of philosophy.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-079" href="#sec-31-fn-079" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-079" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1165–1203. Parmāl.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The last Chandēl king to play any considerable part upon the stage of history was Paramardi, or Parmāl (1165–1203), whose reign is memorable for his defeat in 1182 by Prithivī-rāja Chauhān, and for the capture of Kālanjar in 1203 (<span class="sc">a. h.</span> 599) by Kutb-ud-dīn Ībak.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-080" href="#sec-31-fn-080" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-080" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The Chauhān and Chandēl war occupies a large space in the popular Hindī epic, the <em>Chand-Rāisā</em>, which is familiar to the people of Upper India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1203 (spring). Capitulation of Kālanjar.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The account of the death of Parmāl and the capture of Kālanjar, as told by the contemporary Muhammadan historian, may be quoted as a good illustration of the process by which the Hindu kingdoms passed under the rule of their new Muslim masters :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘ “ The accursed Parmār,” the Rai of Kālanjar, fled into the fort after a desperate resistance in the field, and afterwards surrendered himself, and placed “the collar of subjection” round his neck; and, on his promise of allegiance, was admitted to the same favours as his ancestor had experienced from Mahmūd Sabuktigīn, and engaged to make a payment of tribute and elephants, but he died a natural death before he could execute any of his engagements. His Dīwān, or Mahtea, by name Aj Deo, was not disposed to surrender so easily as his master, and gave his enemies much trouble, until he was compelled to capitulate in consequence of severe drought having dried up all the reservoirs of water in the forts. “ On Monday, the 20th of Rajab, the garrison, in an extreme state of weakness and distraction, came out of the fort, and by compulsion left their native place empty; ... and the fort of Kālanjar, which was celebrated throughout the world for being as strong as the wall of Alexander,” was taken. “ The temples were converted into mosques and abodes of goodness, and the ejaculations of the bead-counters and the voices of the summoners to prayer ascended to the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-457" data-page="457" data-folio="409" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n456_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 409">[p. 409]</button> highest heaven, and the very name of idolatry was annihilated. . . . Fifty thousand men came under the collar of slavery, and the plain became black as pitch with Hindus.” Elephants and cattle, and countless arms also, became the spoil of the victors. ‘The reins of victory were then directed towards Mahobā, and the government of Kālanjar was conferred on Hazabbar-ud-dīn Hasan Arnal. When Kutb-ud-dīn was satisfied with all the arrangements made in that quarter, he went towards Badāūn, “which is one of the mothers of cities, and one of the chiefest of the country of Hind.” ’ <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-081" href="#sec-31-fn-081" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-081" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The last of the Chandēls.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Chandēl Rājas lingered on in Bundelkhand as purely local chiefs until the sixteenth century, but their affairs are of no general interest.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-082" href="#sec-31-fn-082" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-082" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The Chandēl clan was scattered, and its most notable modern representative is the Rāja of Gidhaur, near Mungir (Monghyr) in Bengal.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The last of the Kalachuris.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Kalachuri or Haihaya Rājas of Chedi are last mentioned in an inscription of the year <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1181, and the manner of their disappearance is not exactly known; but there is reason to believe that they were supplanted by the Bāghēls of Rēwā. The Hayobans Rājpūts of the Baliyā district in the east of the United Provinces claim descent from the Rājas of Ratanpur in the Central Provinces, and probably are really an offshoot of the ancient Haihaya race. The later kings of Chedi used an era according to which the year 1 was equivalent to <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 248–9. This era, also called the Traikūtaka, originated in Western India, where its use can be traced back to the fifth century.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-083" href="#sec-31-fn-083" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-083" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> The reason of its adoption by the kings of Chedi is not apparent.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-458"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="458" data-folio="410" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n457_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 410">[p. 410]</button></div><h4 class="sec-h3">VII</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Paramāras (Pawārs) of Mālwa</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>C.</em> <span class="sc">A. D.</span> <strong>820.</strong> Paramāra dynasty of Mālwa.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Paramāra dynasty of Mālwa, the region north of the Narmadā, anciently known as Avanti, or the kingdom of Ujjain, is specially memorable by reason of its association with many eminent names in the history of later Sanskrit literature. The dynasty was founded by a chief named Upendra or Krishnarāja, early in the ninth century, when so many ruling families attract notice for the first time, and it lasted for about four centuries. Upendra appears to have come from Chandrāvatī and Achalgarh, near Mount Ābū, where his clan had been settled for a long time.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D. 974–95.</span> Rāja Muñja.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The seventh Rāja, named Munja, who was famous for his learning and eloquence, was not only a patron of poets, but himself a poet of no small reputation, as attested by the anthologies, which include various compositions attributed to his pen. The author Dhanamjaya and his brother Dhanika were among the distinguished scholars who graced his court. His energies were not devoted solely to the peaceful pursuit of literature, much of his time being spent in fighting with his neighbours. Six times the Chalukya king, Taila II, was defeated by him. The seventh attack failed, and Munja, who had crossed the Godāvarī, Taila's northern boundary, was defeated, captured, and executed about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 995.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-084" href="#sec-31-fn-084" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-084" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> <strong>1018–60.</strong> Rāja Bhoja.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Munja's nephew, the famous Bhoja, ascended the throne of Dhārā, in those days the capital of Mālwa, about <span class="sc">A.D.</span> 1018, and reigned gloriously for more than forty years. Like his <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-459" data-page="459" data-folio="411" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n458_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 411">[p. 411]</button> uncle, he cultivated with equal assiduity the arts of peace and war. Although his fights with the neighbouring powers, including one of the Muhammadan armies of Mahmūd of Ghaznī, are now forgotten, his fame as an enlightened patron of learning and a skilled author remains undimmed, and his name has become proverbial as that of the model king according to the Hindu standard. Works on astronomy, architecture, the art of poetry, and other subjects are attributed to him, and there is no doubt that he was a prince, like Samudragupta, of very uncommon ability. A mosque at Dhārā now occupies the site of Bhoja's Sanskrit college, which seems to have been held in a temple dedicated appropriately to Sarasvatī, the goddess of learning.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-085" href="#sec-31-fn-085" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-085" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bhōjpur lake.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The great Bhōjpur lake, a beautiful sheet of water to the south-east of Bhopāl, covering an area of more than 250 square miles, formed by massive embankments closing the outlet in a circle of hills, was his noblest monument, and continued to testify to the skill of his engineers until the fifteenth century, when the dam was cut by order of a Muhammadan king, and the water drained off. The bed of the lake is now a fertile plain intersected by the Indian Midland Railway.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-086" href="#sec-31-fn-086" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-086" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Later history of Mālwā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">About <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1060 this accomplished prince succumbed to an attack by the confederate kings of Gujarāt and Chedi; and the glory of his house departed. His dynasty lasted as a purely local power until the beginning of the thirteenth century, when it was superseded by chiefs of the Tomara clan, who were followed in their turn by Chauhān Rājas,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-087" href="#sec-31-fn-087" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-087" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-460" data-page="460" data-folio="412" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n459_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 412">[p. 412]</button> from whom the crown passed to Muhammadan kings in 1401. Akbar suppressed the local dynasty in 1562, and incorporated Mālwā in the Moghal empire.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">VIII</h3><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>Pāla and Sena Dynasties of Bihār and Bengal</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">a. d.</span> 650. History of Bengal unknown.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Harsha, when at the height of his power, exercised a certain amount of control as suzerain over the whole of Bengal, even as far east as the distant kingdom of Kāmarupa, or Assam, and seems to have possessed full sovereign authority over western and central Bengal. After his death, the local Rājas no doubt asserted their independence; but, except for the strange story of Arjuna and Wang-Hiuen-tse, related in the thirteenth chapter, no particulars are known concerning the history of Bengal for nearly a century. Bengālī tradition traces the origin of many notable families to five Brahmans and five Kāyasths imported from Kanauj by a king named Ādisūra in order to revive orthodox Hindu customs, which had fallen into disuse during the time when Buddhism was predominant. But no authentic record of this monarch has been discovered. There is, however, no reason to doubt the actual existence of Ādisūra, who belonged to a local dynasty of Rājas ruling Gaur and the neighbourhood. He may be dated approximately in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 700, perhaps a little earlier, or possibly later.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-088" href="#sec-31-fn-088" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-088" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-461"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="461" data-folio="413" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n460_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 413">[p. 413]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><em>c.</em> A. D. 730–40. Rise of the ‘Pāla dynasty’.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Early in the eighth century (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 730–40) a chieftain named Gopāla was elected king of Bengal, which had been suffering from anarchy. Towards the close of his life he extended his power westwards over Magadha or South Bihār, and is said to have reigned forty-five years. He suffered defeat at the hands of Vatsarāja, the Gurjara king of Rājputāna.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-089" href="#sec-31-fn-089" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-089" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> He was a pious Buddhist, and founded a great monastery at Uddandapura, or Otantapuri, the existing town of Bihār, which seems to have been at times the capital of the later Pāla kings. Inasmuch as the word <em>pāla</em> was an element in the personal names of the founder of the family and his successors, the dynasty is commonly and conveniently designated as that of the ‘Pāla kings of Bengal’.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dharmapāla, <em>flor.</em> <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 800.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The second king, Dharmapāla, who is credited with a reign of sixty-four years, is known to have reigned for at least thirty-two years. The Tibetan historian Tāranāth expressly states that his rule extended from the Bay of Bengal to Delhi and Jālandhar in the north and to the valleys of the Vindhyan range in the south. This ascription of wide dominion is supported by the certain fact that Dharmapāla dethroned Indrāyudha, or Indrarāja, king of Panchāla, whose capital was Kanauj, and installed in his stead Chakrāyudha, with the assent of the neighbouring northern powers, enumerated as the Bhoja, Matsya, Madra, Kuru, Yadu, Yavana, Avanti, Gandhāra, and Kīra kings. This event took place soon after <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 800, and prior to the thirty-second year of Dharmapāla's reign, as recorded in two grants.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-090" href="#sec-31-fn-090" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-090" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> It is noticeable that the grant of four villages in the province of Paundravardhana was issued from the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-462" data-page="462" data-folio="414" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n461_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 414">[p. 414]</button> royal head-quarters at Pātaliputra.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-091" href="#sec-31-fn-091" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-091" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> When Hiuen Tsang visited the ancient imperial city in the seventh century he had found the buildings of Asoka in ruins, and the inhabitants limited to about a thousand persons occupying a small walled town on the bank of the Ganges in the northern portion of the site.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-092" href="#sec-31-fn-092" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-092" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Apparently the city had recovered to some extent when Dharmapāla held his court there about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 810. The famous monastery of Vikramasīla, which is said to have included 107 temples and six colleges, was founded by Dharmapāla. It stood on a hill overlooking the right bank of the Ganges, but its position has not been conclusively determined.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-093" href="#sec-31-fn-093" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-093" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Devapāla, ninth century.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Devapāla, the third sovereign of the dynasty, is regarded by the oldest writers on Brahman genealogy in Bengal as having been the most powerful of the Pālas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-094" href="#sec-31-fn-094" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-094" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> His general, Lāusena or Lavasena, is said to have conquered Assam and Kalinga. A grant dated in the thirty-third year of his reign was issued from the court at Mudgagiri, or Monghyr.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-095" href="#sec-31-fn-095" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-095" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> Like all the other kings of his house, he was zealous in the cause of Buddhism, and is reputed to have waged war with the unbelievers, destroying forty of their strongholds. He is said to have reigned for forty-eight years.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-096" href="#sec-31-fn-096" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-096" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Kāmboja rule.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">During the latter part of the tenth century the rule of the Pāla kings was interrupted by the intrusion of hillmen, known as Kāmbojas, who set up one of their chiefs as king. His rule is commemorated by an inscribed pillar at Dinājpur, erected apparently in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 966.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-097" href="#sec-31-fn-097" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-097" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Mahīpāla I, <em>c.</em> <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 978–1030.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Kāmbojas were expelled by Mahīpāla I, the ninth sovereign of the Pāla line, who is known to have been reigning in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1026, and may be assumed to have won <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-463" data-page="463" data-folio="415" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n462_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 415">[p. 415]</button> back his ancestral throne about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 978 or 980. He is credited with a long reign of 52 years, a statement which cannot be far wrong, as there is epigraphic evidence that his rule endured for 48 years.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-098" href="#sec-31-fn-098" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-098" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Of all the Pāla kings he is the best remembered, and songs in his honour, which used to be sung in many parts of Bengal until recent times, are still to be heard in remote corners of Orissa and Kūch Bihār. He was attacked by Rājendra, the Chola king of Kānchī, about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1023. His reign is marked by the revival of Buddhism in Tibet, which had been weakened by the persecution of Langdarma a century earlier. Pundit Dharmapāla and other holy men from Magadha accepted an invitation to Tibet in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1013, and did much to restore the religion of Gautama to honour in that country. A subsequent mission dispatched in 1038, during the reign of Mahīpāla's successor, Nayapāla, and headed by Atīsa, from the Vikramasīla monastery in Magadha, continued the work and firmly re-established Tibetan Buddhism.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-099" href="#sec-31-fn-099" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-099" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Kaivarta rebellion.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The son of Nayapāla, king Vigrahapāla III, who defeated Karna, king of Chedi, and died about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1080, left three sons, namely Mahīpāla II, Sūrapāla II, and Rāmapāla.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-464"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="464" data-folio="416" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n463_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 416">[p. 416]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">When Mahīpāla succeeded to the throne he imprisoned his brothers and misgoverned the realm. His evil deeds provoked a rebellion, headed by Divya or Divyoka, chief of the Chāsi-Kaivarta tribe or Māhishya caste, which at that time was powerful in Northern Bengal. Mahīpāla II was killed by the rebels, who took possession of the country. Divyoka's place was taken by his nephew Bhīma, who became king of Varendra. Prince Rāmapāla, having escaped from confinement, travelled over a large part of India in order to obtain help in the recovery of his kingdom. After much effort he collected a strong force, including contingents from the Rāshtrakūtas, to whom he was related by marriage, and many other princes. Bhīma was defeated and killed, and Rāmapāla regained the throne of his fathers.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-100" href="#sec-31-fn-100" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-100" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Reign of Rāma-pāla, <span class="sc">c. a. d.</span> 1084–1130.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rāmapāla is described by Tāranāth as possessing a vigorous understanding and widely extended power. After defeating the Kaivarta usurper, he conquered Mithilā or North Bihār, the modern Champāran and Darbhanga Districts, and it is clear that his dominions also included Kāmarūpa or Assam, because his son Kumārapāla conferred the government of that country, with kingly powers, upon a valiant minister named Vaidyadeva. Buddhism, although then declining in Hindustan, flourished in the Pāla dominions during the reign of Rāmapāla, the monasteries of Magadha being crowded with thousands of residents. Tāranāth and certain Bengal authors treat Rāmapāla as the last of his dynasty, or at any rate, the last who exercised considerable power, but the inscriptions prove that he was followed by five kings of his family.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-101" href="#sec-31-fn-101" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-101" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-465"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="465" data-folio="417" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n464_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 417">[p. 417]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Latest Pāla kings.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">King Govindapāla is known to have been on the throne in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1175 ; and, according to tradition, the ruler of Magadha at the time of the Muhammadan conquest, in <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 1197, was Indradyumna[-pāla]. Forts attributed to him are still pointed out in the Mungir (Monghyr) District.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-102" href="#sec-31-fn-102" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-102" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Importance of the Pāla dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Pāla dynasty deserves remembrance as one of the most remarkable of Indian dynasties. No other royal line in an important kingdom, save that of the Āndhras, endured so long, for four and a half centuries. Dharmapāla and Devapāla succeeded in making Bengal one of the great powers of India, and, although later kings had not the control of realms so wide or possessed influence so extensive, their dominion was far from being contemptible. The Pāla authority was considerably shaken by the Kāmboja usurpation in the latter part of the tenth century, and again by the Chāsi-Kaivarta or Māhishya revolt in the eleventh century, which prepared the way for the encroachments made by the Sena kings. The Pālas seem to have held Magadha or South Bihār, and Mungir in North Bihār, almost throughout to the end, with little interruption, but during the last century of their rule they lost nearly the whole of Bengal to the Senas.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-103" href="#sec-31-fn-103" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-103" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The details of the local history need to be worked out.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Intellectual and artistic activity.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reigns of Dharmapāla and Devapāla, extending over more than a century, from about <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 780 to 892, were a period of marked intellectual and artistic activity. Two artists of that time, Dhīmān and his son Bitpālo (Vitapāla), acquired the highest fame for their skill as painters, sculptors, and bronze-founders. Some works of their school are believed to be extant.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-104" href="#sec-31-fn-104" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-104" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> No building of Pāla age <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-466" data-page="466" data-folio="418" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n465_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 418">[p. 418]</button> appears to survive, but the numerous great tanks in the central districts of their territory, especially in Dinājpur, testify to the interest taken by the kings in the execution of undertakings intended for the public benefit.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Patronage of Buddhism.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">All the Pāla kings without exception were zealous Buddhists, ready to bestow liberal patronage on learned teachers and the numerous monastic communities. Dharmapāla, clearly a man of exceptional capacity, is credited with the merit of having been an ardent reformer of religion. His successors in the eleventh century, who were devoted to Tantric forms of Buddhism, enjoyed the services of many pious men, among whom Atīsa, already mentioned as a missionary in Tibet, was the most eminent.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-105" href="#sec-31-fn-105" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-105" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The beginning of the Senas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Sena dynasty was founded by a chief named Sāmantadeva, who came from the Deccan. About the middle of the eleventh century he, or his son Hemantasena, founded a principality at Kāsīpurī, which has been identified with the modern Kasiārī in the Mayūrabhanja State. Neither of those chiefs seems to have acquired extensive power.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vijayasena (<em>c.</em> A. D. 1070– 1108).</aside></div><p class="prose-p">But Sāmantasena's grandson, Vijayasena, certainly raised himself to the rank of an independent sovereign in the latter part of the eleventh or early in the twelfth century, and wrested a large part of the Bengal province from the Pālas, thus firmly establishing the Sena dynasty. He also carried on successful wars with other powers, and enjoyed a long reign of about forty years, more or less. He kept on terms of friendship with Choraganga of Kalinga, who ruled that kingdom for the extraordinary term of seventy-one years, and who about the time of the Chāsi-Kaivarta rebellion (<em>c.</em> A. D. 1080) extended his conquests to the extreme north of Orissa.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Vallālasena or</aside><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Ballāl Sen (<em>c.</em> 1108–19).</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The dominions acquired by Vijayasena were transmitted <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-467" data-page="467" data-folio="419" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n466_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 419">[p. 419]</button> (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1108) to his son Vallālasena, famous in Bengal tradition as Ballāl Sen, who is credited with having reorganized the caste system and introduced the practice of ‘Kulinism’ among Brahmans, Baidyas, and Kāyasths. Some accounts allege that he founded Gaur or Lakhnautī, but there is reason to believe that the city was in existence at an earlier date. The site of a palace attributed to him is pointed out at Rāmpāl near Bikrampur in the Dacca District.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-106" href="#sec-31-fn-106" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-106" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> All the Sena kings were Brahmanical Hindus, and so had a special reason for hostility to the Buddhist Pālas, and a keen interest in the maintenance of caste. The Hinduism of Ballāl Sen was of the Tantric kind. The Brahman genealogists assert that he sent numerous missionaries, all Brahmans, to Magadha, Bhotan, Chittagong, Arakan, Orissa, and Nepāl.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-107" href="#sec-31-fn-107" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-107" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lakshmaṇasena (<em>c.</em> 1119).</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Ballāl Sen was succeeded in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1119 by his son Lakshmanasena.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Muhammadan conquest of Bihār.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">In Bihār and Bengal both Pālas and Senas were swept away by the torrent of Muhammadan invasion at the end of the twelfth century, when Kutb-ud-dīn’s general, Muhammad the son of Bakhtyār, stormed Bihār in or about <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1197, and surprised Nūdīah (<em>vulgo</em> Nuddea) a year or two later. The Musalman general, who had already made his name a terror by repeated plundering expeditions in Bihār, seized the capital by a daring stroke. The almost contemporary historian met one of the survivors of the attacking party in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1243, and learned from him that the fort of Bihār was seized by a party of only two hundred horsemen, who boldly rushed the postern gate and gained possession of the place. Great quantities of plunder were <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-468" data-page="468" data-folio="420" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n467_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 420">[p. 420]</button> obtained, and the slaughter of the ‘shaven-headed Brahmans’, that is to say the Buddhist monks, was so thoroughly completed, that when the victor sought for some one capable of explaining the contents of the books in the libraries of the monasteries, not a living man could be found who was able to read them. ‘It was discovered’, we are told, ‘that the whole of that fortress and city was a college, and in the Hindī tongue they call a college Bihar.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-108" href="#sec-31-fn-108" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-108" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Destruction of Buddhism.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This crushing blow, followed up, of course, by similar acts of violence, destroyed the vitality of Buddhism in its ancient home. No doubt, a few devout, though disheartened, adherents of the system lingered round the desecrated shrines for a few years longer; and even to this day traces of the religion once so proudly dominant may be discerned in the practices of obscure sects; but Buddhism as an organized religion in Bihār, its last abode in Upper India south of the Himalaya, was destroyed once and for all by the sword of a single Musalman adventurer.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-109" href="#sec-31-fn-109" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-109" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Many monks who escaped death fled to Tibet, Nepāl, and Southern India. Their advent produced an important effect upon the Hindu revival in Southern India. In Tibet the arrival of the learned refugees enabled Buton, the Grand Lama appointed by Kublai Khan, to enrich the Tibetan language by translations from Sanskrit sources, which were included in the <em>Tangyur</em> encyclopaedia at the close of the thirteenth century. The preservation of the fruits of the joint labours of the Indian Pundits and the Tibetan Lamas was secured by the practice of the art of block-printing, which had been introduced into Tibet from China in the seventh century.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-110" href="#sec-31-fn-110" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-110" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote"><span class="sc">A. D.</span> ? 1199. Overthrow of the Sena dynasty.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The overthrow of the Sena dynasty was accomplished with equal or even greater ease. The ruler of eastern Bengal in those days was Lakshmanasena, described by the Muhammadan writer as an aged man and reputed, though erroneously, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-469" data-page="469" data-folio="421" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n468_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 421">[p. 421]</button> to have occupied the throne for eighty years. The portents which were said to have attended his birth had been justified by the monarch's exceptional personal qualities. His family, we are told, was respected by all the Rāis or chiefs of Hindustan, and he was considered to hold the rank of hereditary Khalīf (Caliph), or spiritual head of the country. Trustworthy persons affirmed that no one, great or small, ever suffered injustice at his hands, and his generosity was proverbial.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Nūdīah, the capital.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">This much-revered sovereign held his court at Nūdīah, situated in the upper delta of the Ganges, on the Bhāgīrathī river, about 60 miles north of the site of Calcutta. The town still gives its name to a British district (Nuddea, Nadiā), and is renowned as the seat of a Hindu college organized after the ancient manner.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Capture of Nūdīah, <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1199.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Probably in <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1199, not long after his facile conquest of Bihār, Muhammad the son of Bakhtyār equipped an army for the subjugation of Bengal. Riding in advance of the main body of his troops, he suddenly appeared before Nūdīah with a slender following of eighteen horsemen, and boldly entered the city, the people supposing him to be a horsedealer. But when he reached the gate of the Rāi's palace, he drew his sword and attacked the unsuspecting household. The Rāi, who was at his dinner, was completely taken by surprise,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">‘and fled barefooted by the rear of the palace; and his whole treasure, and all his wives, maidservants, attendants, and women fell into the hands of the invader. Numerous elephants were taken, and such booty was obtained by the Muhammadans as is beyond all compute. When his (Muhammad's) army arrived, the whole city was brought under subjection, and he fixed his head-quarters there.’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Lakhnautī, the Muhammadan capital.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Rāi Lakhmaniya, as the author calls him, fled to Bikrampur in the Dacca district,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-111" href="#sec-31-fn-111" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-111" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> where he died; and the conqueror presently destroyed the city of Nūdīah, establishing the seat of his government at the ancient Hindu city <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-470" data-page="470" data-folio="422" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n469_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 422">[p. 422]</button> of Lakhnautī, or Gaur. Mosques, colleges, and Muhammadan monasteries were endowed by him and his officers in all parts of the kingdom, and a great portion of the spoil was judiciously sent to his distant chief, Kutb-ud-dīn.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Dishonoured end of the Hindu kingdoms.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Such was the dishonoured end of the last Hindu kingdoms of Bengal and Bihār, which would have made a better fight for life if they had deserved to exist.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-112" href="#sec-31-fn-112" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-112" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The administration must have been hopelessly inefficient to permit a foreign army to march unobserved across Bengal, and to allow of the surprise of the palace by an insignificant party of eighteen horsemen.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Literature.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reign of the earlier king Lakshmanasena was remarkable for considerable literary activity and for his liberal patronage of Sanskrit literature. An imitation of Kālidāsa's <em>Meghadūta</em> by Dhoyi, or Dhoyīka, court-poet of Lakshmanasena, has been published. Jayadeva, the famous author of the <em>Gītagovinda</em>, seems to have lived in the reign of Lakshmanasena, who wrote verses himself. His father, Ballāl Sen, also was an author.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">IX</h4><h4 class="sec-h3"><em>The Rājpūt Clans</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Apparent dominance of the clans.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Ethnological speculations, or discussions about facial angles, thick or thin noses, long skulls or broad skulls, the mystery of the origin of caste, and so forth, are foreign to the purpose of this work, and cannot be even lightly handled in these pages.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-113" href="#sec-31-fn-113" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-113" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> But the narrative sections of this chapter <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-471" data-page="471" data-folio="423" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n470_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 423">[p. 423]</button> dealing with the political fortunes of many Rājpūt clans can hardly fail to suggest to the thoughtful reader inquiries which seem to demand with urgency some sort of answer. Who were these Rājpūts—Parihārs, Pawārs, Chandēls, and the rest—and why do they and their affairs make such a confused stir during the centuries intervening between the death of Harsha and the Muhammadan conquest ? The dominance of the Rājpūt clans is at first sight the conspicuous fact differentiating the mediaeval from the ancient period in the history of Northern India, and the mind craves for an explanation. It is proverbially easier to ask questions than to answer them, and in this case the facts are far too complex and imperfectly known to admit of concise satisfactory explanations. Still it may be worth while to make a few observations on the subject, designed to help the weary reader in his endeavour to find some sort of clue to guide him through the maze of dynasties.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Kshatriyas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The apparently sudden introduction of Rājpūt states on the stage during the eighth and ninth centuries is in part an illusion. Hardly anything is known about the caste or tribal position of the ancient ruling families. Nobody can tell exactly the rank of Hindu society to which the family of Asoka or Samudragupta belonged, and nothing is on record to indicate how far the kings whose names appear prominently on the scene were merely successful personal adventurers or how far they were the heads of dominant clans. In later times all Rājpūts have considered themselves to be Kshatriyas—members of the second of the four groups of castes according to the familiar Brahman theory.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-114" href="#sec-31-fn-114" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-114" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> So far back as the time when the <em>Dialogues of the Buddha</em> were composed the Kshatriyas were recognized as an important element in society, and in their own estimation stood higher <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-472" data-page="472" data-folio="424" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n471_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 424">[p. 424]</button> than the Brahmans.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-115" href="#sec-31-fn-115" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-115" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The fact probably is that from very remote days ruling clans of Kshatriyas, essentially similar to the Rājpūts of later days, existed and were continually forming new states, just in the same way as in the mediaeval period. But their records have perished, and only a few exceptionally conspicuous dynasties are at all remembered, and so stand out on the page of history in a manner that does not fully represent the truth. The term Kshatriya was, I believe, always one of very vague meaning, simply denoting the Hindu ruling classes which did not claim Brahman descent. Similarly all persons performing priestly functions could be regarded by Hindus only as Brahmans. Occasionally a Rāja might be a Brahman by caste, but the Brahman's natural place at court was that of minister rather than that of king.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-116" href="#sec-31-fn-116" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-116" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> Chandragupta Maurya presumably was considered to be a Kshatriya—his minister Chānakya or Kautilya certainly was a Brahman.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Break in tradition.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The real difference between the ancient and mediaeval periods is that the living tradition concerning the former has been broken, while that concerning the latter survives. The Mauryas and Guptas belong to a dead and buried past, remembered only through books, inscriptions, and coins, whereas the clans whose ruling families came into notice during the mediaeval period are still very much alive, and in many cases form numerous and influential sections of the existing population.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The ‘Scythian’ element.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Tod and the other older writers perceived long ago that the Rājpūt clans are in large part of foreign, or, as they called it, ‘Scythian’ descent. The more exact researches of recent times have fully confirmed this opinion, and it is now possible to indicate with a considerable degree of precision the source of the foreign blood in several of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-473" data-page="473" data-folio="425" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n472_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 425">[p. 425]</button> principal clans, and at the same time to recognize the closeness of their relationship with castes which occupy a social position lower than that of the Rājpūts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Sakas and Yüe-chi.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The earliest foreign immigration within the limits of the historical period which can be verified is that of the Sakas in the second century <span class="sc">b. c.</span> (<em>ante</em>, pp. 240, 265); and the next is that of the Yüe-chi or Kushāns in the first century after Christ (<em>ante</em>, p. 267). Probably none of the existing Rājpšt clans can carry back their genuine pedigrees nearly so far. I have no doubt that the ruling families of both the Sakas and the Kushāns when they became Hinduized were admitted to rank as Kshatriyas in the Hindu caste system, but the fact can be inferred only from the analogy of what is ascertained to have happened in later ages—it cannot be proved.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Hūnas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The third recorded great irruption of foreign barbarians occurred during the fifth century and the early part of the sixth. There are indications that the immigration from Central Asia had continued during the third century (<em>ante</em>, p. 289), but, if it did, no distinct record of the event has been preserved, and, so far as positive knowledge goes, only three certain irruptions of foreigners on a large scale through the northern and north-western passes can be proved to have taken place within the historical period anterior to the Muhammadan invasions of the tenth and eleventh centuries. The first and second, as above observed, were those of the Sakas and Yüe-chi respectively, and the third was that of the Hūnas, or White Huns. These names, Saka, Yüe-chi, and Hūna, merely indicate the predominant elements in the invading swarms, which included many various races. The tradition of descent from the first and second swarms has been lost for ages. The Turkī Shāhiya kings of Kabul, who were displaced by the Hindu Shāhiyas in the ninth century, boasted their descent from the great Kushān king, Kanishka, but I do not know of any later claim on the part of an Indian ruling family to relationship with the Yüe-chi.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Effects of Hūna invasions.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The break in tradition seems to be due in large measure <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-474" data-page="474" data-folio="426" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n473_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 426">[p. 426]</button> to the far-reaching effects of the third barbarian irruption, to which the name of Hūna is given. The meagre literary record of the Hun invasion is supplemented by so many miscellaneous observations in the domains of ethnology, archaeology, and numismatics, that a strong impression is produced on the mind of the student that the Hun invasions disturbed Hindu institutions and polity much more deeply than would be supposed from perusal of the <em>Purānas</em>, and other literary works. The Hindu writers display great unwillingness to dwell upon ‘barbarian’ invasions, uniting in ‘a conspiracy of silence’. They never allude to the existence of Alexander the Great, and the Gujarāt historians similarly ignore the sack of Somnāth by Mahmūd of Ghaznī.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-117" href="#sec-31-fn-117" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-117" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> If Muhammadan authors had not related in detail the story of that famous raid, no record of it would have been found in Indian literature or inscriptions. There is, therefore, no reason for surprise that the Hindu record of the Hun deluge is meagre, and that recognition of its importance has had to be won laboriously by the patient researches of modern archaeologists. It is impossible to set forth the complicated evidence in this place, and the reader must be asked to accept the assertion that the series of invasions by the Huns and associated foreign tribes in the fifth and sixth centuries shook Indian society in Northern India to its foundations, severed the chain of tradition, and brought about a rearrangement of both castes and ruling families. The effects of the Hun cataclysm are obscured partially by the brilliant achievement of Harsha in establishing for thirty-five years (612–47) a strong paramount power able to control the conflicting interests of the various races, clans, and creeds subject to his temporary sway.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-118" href="#sec-31-fn-118" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-118" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> When his heavy hand was removed all those elements broke loose, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-475" data-page="475" data-folio="427" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n474_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 427">[p. 427]</button> and, after a period of unrecorded anarchy, produced in the domain of politics the new grouping of states described in its leading features in this chapter.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Gurjaras.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">It seems to be clearly established that the Hun group of tribes or hordes made their principal permanent settlements in Rājputāna and the Panjāb. The most important element in the group, after the Huns themselves, was that of the Gurjaras, whose name still survives in the spoken form Gūjar as the designation of a widely diffused middle-class caste in North-Western India. The Gūjars, primarily a pastoral people, are, of course, like almost all Indian castes, largely engaged in agriculture. The Jāts or Jats, more exclusively agricultural, are recognized universally to be akin to the Gūjars, although it is impossible to define the relationship. Neither Jats or Gūjars are accounted to rank as Rājpūts or Kshatriyas, but most of the Panjāb Jats claim Rājpūt descent.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-119" href="#sec-31-fn-119" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-119" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gurjara kingdoms.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The prominent position occupied by Gurjara kingdoms in early mediaeval times is a recent discovery. The existence of a small Gurjara principality at Bharōch (Broach), and of a larger state in Rājputāna, had been known to archaeologists for many years, but the recognition of the fact that Bhoja, and the other kings of the powerful Kanauj dynasty in the ninth, tenth, and eleventh centuries were Gurjaras is of recent date. Certain misreadings of epigraphic dates had obscured the true history of that dynasty, until the correct readings were established. It is now definitely proved that Bhoja (<em>c.</em> <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 840–90), his predecessors and successors, belonged to the Pratihāra (Parihār) clan of the Gurjara tribe or caste, and, consequently, that the well-known clan of Parihār Rājpūts is a branch of the Gurjara or Gūjar stock.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-120" href="#sec-31-fn-120" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-120" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-476"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="476" data-folio="428" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n475_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 428">[p. 428]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The ‘fire-born’ clans.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">A familiar legend appearing in the <em>Chand Rāisā</em> and other late documents in variant forms groups together four Rājpūt clans—the Pawār (Pramāra), Parihār (Pratihāra), Chauhān (Chāhumāna), and Solankī or Chaulukya—as being <em>Agnikula</em>, or ‘fire-born’, originating from a sacrificial fire-pit at Mount Ābū in Southern Rājputāna. The myth seems to express the historical truths that the four clans named are related, and all arose in Southern Rājputāna; and further, as Mr. Crooke justly observes, it ‘represents a rite of purgation by fire, the scene of which was in Southern Rājputāna, whereby the impurity of the foreigners was removed and they became fitted to enter the Hindu caste system’.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-121" href="#sec-31-fn-121" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-121" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Parihārs.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The fact that one of the four clans, namely, the Parihār, undoubtedly is of the Gūjar stock, raises a strong presumption that the three others also are descended from Gurjaras or similar foreign immigrants. In this way the origin of some of the most notable of the Rājpūt clans is accounted for. The Gurjaras are believed to have entered India either along with or soon after the White Huns, and to have settled in large numbers in Rājputāna; but there is nothing to show what part of Asia they came from, or to what race they belonged.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-122" href="#sec-31-fn-122" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-122" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The Pawār head-quarters were at Chandrāvatī and Achalgarh, near Mount Ābū, and in the seventh century the Parihārs ruled a large part of Rājputāna from Bhinmāl, some 50 miles to the north-west of Mount Ābū. About <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 800 Nāgabhata, king of the Gurjara country, conquered Kanauj on the Ganges, to which city he shifted his capital, and so founded the long line of Kanauj kings who ruled there until the advent of Mahmūd of Ghaznī at the beginning of <span class="sc">A. D.</span> 1019 (<em>ante</em>, p. 404). The discovery that the Rājas of Kanauj <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-477" data-page="477" data-folio="429" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n476_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 429">[p. 429]</button> from 800 to 1018, some of whom enjoyed the rank of paramount sovereigns of Northern India, really were the descendants of ‘barbarian’ foreign immigrants into Rājputāna in the fifth or sixth century and cousins of the Gūjars, though recognized as high-class Rājpūts, is one of the most notable additions made to Indian historical knowledge for many years past. Although the history of the other Rājpūt clans of the north has not been worked out with equal fullness, a fair presumption arises that many of them were of similar origin. The truth seems to be that when a foreign clan or tribe became Hinduized the ruling families were readily recognized as Kshatriyas or Rājpūts, while the rank and file gradually lost their tribal organization, and developed into an Indian caste not regarded as aristocratic.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Indigenous origin of the southern clans.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Some of the principal clans farther south spring from a different source, and apparently are descended from the so-called aboriginal tribes, Gonds, Bhars, Kols, and the like, whom Sir Herbert Risley designated by the singularly inappropriate generic name of ‘Dravidians’, one of the most misleading terms ever introduced.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-123" href="#sec-31-fn-123" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-123" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The evidence of a close connexion between the Chandēls and the Gonds, who, again, were associated with the Bhars, is particularly strong; and the inference is fully justified that the Chandēl Rājpūts were originally Hinduized Bhars or Gonds, or both, who attained recognition as Kshatriyas or Rājpūts, when they acquired power and took up the business of kingship for which the Kshatriya group of castes was appropriated. The Gaharwārs similarly are associated with the Bhars; the Bundēlas and the northern Rāthōrs are offshoots of the Gaharwārs, and so on. The name of the great Rāshtrakūta clan of the Deccan, the political history of which will be treated in the next chapter, is etymologically identical with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-478" data-page="478" data-folio="430" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n477_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 430">[p. 430]</button> Rāthōr, but there is not, so far as I am aware, evidence of any racial connexion between the Rāshtrakūtas of the Deccan and the Rāthōrs of Hindustan. The former seem to have originated among some one or other of the indigenous tribes of the Deccan in much the same way as the Chandēls became differentiated from the Gonds of the territory which is now the Chhatarpur State.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-124" href="#sec-31-fn-124" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-124" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Struggle between northern and southern clans.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The unceasing wars of the mediaeval period become a little more intelligible and interesting when they are regarded as being in large part a secular struggle between the foreign Rājpūts of the north and the indigenous Rājpūts of the south. Of course, this arrangement of the sides did not always hold good, and powers normally at feud sometimes made friends and contracted alliances one with the other, or all parties momentarily combined against the Muhammadans. But I think it is true that, as a general rule, the Rājpūts formed by the social promotion of ‘aborigines’ were inimical to the Rājpūts descended from ‘barbarian’ immigrants. In the northern group the clans most conspicuous in the historical field are the Chauhāns, Parihārs, Tomaras, and Pawārs; in the southern group the principal clans are the Chandēls, Kalachuris, or Haihayas, Gaharwārs, and Rāshtrakūtas. The origin of the Solankis or Chalukyas (Chaulukya, &amp;c.) is disputed. They claim to come from Oudh, but it is more probable that they are really of foreign origin, like the three other clans with which they are associated in the ‘fire-pit’ story.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-125" href="#sec-31-fn-125" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-125" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Summary.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The main points to remember are that the Kshatriya or Rājpūt group of castes is essentially an occupational group, composed of all clans following the Hindu ritual who actually <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-479" data-page="479" data-folio="431" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n478_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 431">[p. 431]</button> undertook the work of government<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-31-fn-126" href="#sec-31-fn-126" data-fn-target="sec-31-fn-126" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>; that, consequently, people of most diverse races were and are lumped together as Rājpūts; and that most of the great clans now in existence are descended either from foreign immigrants of the fifth or sixth century of the Christian era, or from indigenous races such as the Gonds and Bhars. This finding will, I fear, be displeasing to many families of Indian gentry, who naturally prefer to believe in orthodox Brahman-made pedigrees going back to the sun, moon, or fire-pit; but I am convinced that it is substantially true, although the evidence is of a kind difficult to grasp, and incapable of brief presentation.
