<span id="page-263" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX I</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>The Invasion of Menander, and the Date of Patañjali</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Authorities.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The authorities for the invasion of Menander are Strabo, who alone gives the Greek king's name (Bk. xi, sec. xi, 1; xv, sec. ii, 3); Patañjali, the contemporary Hindu grammarian; the Sanskrit astronomical work, the <em>Gārgī Saṁhitā</em>, of uncertain date; and Tāranāth, the Tibetan historian of Buddhism.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Strabo.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Strabo's informant, Apollodoros of Artemita, testifies that Menander crossed the Hypanis (Hyphasis, Biās) river, at which Alexander's advance had been arrested; penetrated to the Isamus (τοῦ Ἰσάμου), which has not been identified; and ultimately subjugated Patalēnē, or the Indus delta, the kingdom of Saraostos (Surāshtra, or Kāthiāwāṛ), and a territory on the western coast named Sigerdis. This statement is supported by the observation of the writer of the <em>Periplus</em>, who noticed, probably towards the close of the first century after Christ, that Greek coins of Apollodotos and Menander were still current at the port of Barygaza (Broach, Bharōch). This curious observation suggests the inference, that although Menander was compelled to retire quickly from the Gangetic valley, his rule must have continued for a considerable number of years in the territories on the western coast.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Madhyamikā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The sieges of Sākētam and Madhyamikā by the Yavana, that is to say presumably Menander, are referred to by the grammarian Patañjali in terms which necessarily imply that those events occurred during the writer's lifetime. The proof that Madhyamikā is the correct reading and to be interpreted as the name of a city is due to Prof. Kielhorn (<em>Ind. Ant.</em> vii, 266). The identity of Madhyamikā with the ancient town of Nagarī, or Tambavatī Nagarī, one of the oldest sites in India, about 8 miles to the north of Chitōr in Rājputāna, is established by the coins found at Nagarī, and rarely elsewhere, with the legend <em>Majhimikāya śibijanapadasa</em>, '[Coin] of the Śibi people in Majhimikā (Madhyamikā) city' (D. R. Bhandarkar in <em>Prog. Rep. A. S. W. I.</em>, 1915–16, p. 52. See also Cunningham, <em>Reports</em>, vi, 201; xiv, 146, pl. xxxi). The Śibis of Madhyamikā probably emigrated from the Panjāb. Madhyamikā was a place of great importance which the Greek army could not neglect. Prof. D. R. Bhandarkar has <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-264" data-page="264" data-folio="228" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n263_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 228">[p. 228]</button> found two inscriptions of the second century <span class="sc">b. c.</span> recording the performance of the <em>Aśvamedha</em> and <em>Vājapeya</em> sacrifices. The coins are rightly assigned to the middle of the second century <span class="sc">b. c.</span> The ruins at Nagarī include a square brick <em>stūpa</em> (converted into a Saiva temple) with remarkable tiles and terra cottas, and a <em>toraṇa</em> of Gupta date. Chitōr was largely built out of the ruins of Nagarī.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Sākētam.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Sākētam (Sākēta) probably was a town in Southern Oudh, but not identical with Ajodhya, as it is often asserted to be. There seem to have been several places of the name (Weber, in <em>Ind. Ant.</em>, ii, 208). The identifications of the Shā-che of Fa-hien with the Viśākhā of Hiuen Tsang and with Sākētam, as made by Cunningham, are equally unsound (<em>J. R. A. S.</em>, 1898, p. 522 ; 1900, p. 3). At present the position of Sākētam cannot be determined precisely.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Date of Patañjali.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The words of Patañjali in which he alludes to the horse-sacrifice of Pushyamitra (<em>iha Pushpamitram yājayāmaḥ</em>), when read with other relevant passages, permit of no doubt that the grammarian was the contemporary of that king as well as of the Greek invader presumed to be Menander. The question of Patañjali's date was the subject of prolonged controversy between Weber on one side and Goldstücker and Bhandarkar on the other. Ultimately Weber was constrained to admit the substantial validity of his opponents' arguments (<em>Hist. Ind. Lit.</em>, 2nd ed., Trübner, 1882, p. 224, note) ; and no doubt now remains that the date of Patañjali is fixed to 150–140 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> in round numbers. References are : Goldstücker, <em>Pāṇini, His Place in Sanskrit Literature</em>, pp. 228–38 ; <em>Ind. Ant.</em> i, 299–302 ; ii, 57, 69, 94, 206–10, 238, 362 ; xv, 80–4 ; xvi, 156, 172 (the Maurya passage).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Gārgī Saṁhitā.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The statement in the <em>Gārgī Saṁhitā</em>, a work ascribed by Max Müller to the second or third century after Christ, is to the following effect :— ‘After speaking of the kings of Pāṭaliputra (mentioning Śāliśūka, the fourth successor of Asoka [c. 200 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>] by name), the author adds : “That when the viciously valiant Greeks, after reducing Sāketa (Oude), the Pañchāla country [probably the Doāb between the Jumna and Ganges], and Mathurā, will reach Kusumadhvaja, that is, the royal residence of Pāṭaliputra, and that then all provinces will be in disorder”’ (Max Müller, <em>India, What can it Teach us ?</em>, p. 298, ed. 1883 ; and Cunningham, <em>Num. Chron.</em>, 1890, p. 224).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-001" href="#sec-20-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-265"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="265" data-folio="229" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n264_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 229">[p. 229]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tāranāth.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The evidence of Tāranāth (A. D. 1608, resting on old works), as correctly translated by Schiefner, agrees with that of the <em>Divyāvadāna</em> (Burnouf, <em>Introd.</em>, 2nd ed., p. 384) in stating that Pushyamitra was the ally of unbelievers, and himself burnt monasteries and slew monks :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>‘ Es erhob der Brahmanenkönig Puschjamitra sammt den übrigen Tirthja’s Krieg, verbrannte von Madhyadeça bis Dschalamdhara eine Menge von Vihâra’s,’ &amp;c. (p. 81).</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The historian adds that, five years later, Pushyamitra died in the north.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Assuming that Pushyamitra died in 149 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, after a reign of thirty-six years, as stated in the best Purānic texts, the invasion of Menander may be assigned to the years 156–153 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, a date fully in accordance with the numismatic evidence.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-002" href="#sec-20-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Coins of Menander are common in India, both in the Panjāb and farther east and south. Forty of his coins were found in the Hamīrpur district to the south of the Jumna in 1877, and brought to the author, then on duty in that district. They were associated with coins of Eukratides, Apollodotos Soter, and Antimachos Nikēphoros, and were in good condition (<em>Ind.
