<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-13"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="13" data-folio="v" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n12_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page v">[p. v]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> task of revising this history for a fourth edition was entrusted to me in accordance with the wish of the author, who realized that he would not be spared to complete the work himself. For one who has not devoted the greater portion of a lifetime to careful study of the problems involved, the task has been by no means easy. Fortunately, however, I was furnished with notes on various points recorded by Dr. Vincent A. Smith before his death; and with these and the help of reports, papers and essays on Indian history and antiquities, which have appeared in various publications since 1914, I have endeavoured to the best of my ability to bring the work up to date.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The excavations carried out at Taxila by the Archaeological Department of the Government of India, and the researches of Indian scholars like Messrs. R. D. Banerji, K. P. Jayaswal, D. R. Bhandarkar and others, have necessitated the preparation of several additional notes, as well as chronological and other amendments in the text and appendices of various chapters.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As regards the problem of the origin of the Pallavas, which the author perforce left unsolved in the third edition, I have included in the text a theory advanced by a scholar of Colombo, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-14" data-page="14" data-folio="vi" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n13_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page vi">[p. vi]</button> based upon a tradition embodied in ancient Tamil literature. His suggestion as to the original connexion of the dynasty with Ceylon, which formed the gist of an article in the <em>Indian Antiquary</em> of April 1923, should be compared with a valuable paper on ‘The Origin and Early History of the Pallavas of Kanchi’ by Professor S. Krishnaswami Aiyangar, which appeared in the <em>Journal of Indian History</em> (Vol. II, Part I) for November 1922. I much regret that a copy of the article, which Professor Aiyangar kindly sent to me, arrived too late to admit of my quoting or embodying his conclusions in this edition. Those, however, who are interested in the early history of South India will do well to acquaint themselves with the Professor’s view of the problem, based as it is upon prolonged research and careful reasoning.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It remains to add that the Index of the work has been revised and slightly enlarged, and that a few necessary alterations have been made in the maps and illustrations.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">S. M. E.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-15"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="15" data-folio="vii" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n14_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page vii">[p. vii]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">EXTRACT FROM PREFACE TO FIRST EDITION</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> plan and limitations of this book have been explained so fully in the Introduction that little more need be said by way of preface. The room for difference of opinion on many of the subjects treated is so great that I cannot expect my views on controverted points to meet with universal acceptance; and the complexity of my undertaking forbids me to hope that positive errors, justly open to censure, have been avoided altogether; but I trust that critics will be prepared to concede the amount of indulgence which may be granted legitimately to the work of a pioneer.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The devotion of a disproportionately large space to the memorable invasion of Alexander the Great is due to the exceptional interest of the subject, which, so far as I know, has not been treated adequately in any modern book.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The presentation of cumbrous and unfamiliar Oriental names must always be a difficulty for a writer on Indian history. I have endeavoured to secure reasonable uniformity of spelling without pedantry. The system of transliteration followed in the notes and appendices is substantially that used in the <em>Indian Antiquary</em>; while in the text long vowels only are marked where necessary, and all other diacritical signs are discarded.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-16"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="16" data-folio="viii" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n15_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page viii">[p. viii]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Vowels have values as in Italian; except the short <em>a</em>, which is pronounced like <em>u</em> in <em>but</em>, when with stress, and like <em>A</em> in <em>America</em>, when without stress. The consonants are to be pronounced as in English; and <em>ch</em>, consequently, is represented in French by <em>tch</em>, and in German by <em>tsch</em>; similarly, <em>j</em> is equivalent to the French <em>dj</em> and the German <em>dsch</em>. The international symbol <em>c</em> for the English <em>ch</em>, as in <em>church</em>, which has been adopted by the Asiatic Societies, may have some advantages in purely technical publications; but its use results in such <em>monstra horrenda</em> as <em>Cac</em> for <em>Chach</em>, and is unsuitable in a work intended primarily for English and Indian readers.</p></div>
