<span id="page-70" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX C</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>Chronology of the Śaiśunāga and Nanda Dynasties.</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Fixed data.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although the discrepant traditionary materials available do not permit the determination with accuracy of the chronology of the Śaiśunāga and Nanda dynasties, it is, I venture to think, possible to attain a tolerably close approximation to the truth,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">‘Nine months have over us<br>
passed since that sad day<br>
My father perished.’</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-71"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="71" data-folio="47" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n70_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 47">[p. 47]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">and to reconcile some of the traditions. The fixed point from which to reckon backwards is the year 322 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, the date for the accession of Chandragupta Maurya, which is approximately correct, with a possible error not exceeding three years. The second principal datum is the list of ten kings of the Śaiśunāga dynasty as given in the oldest historical entries in the Purāṇas, namely, those in the <em>Matsya</em> and the <em>Vāyu</em>, the general correctness of which is confirmed by several lines of evidence; the third is the revised reading (1917) of the Khāravela inscription, referred to in note 2 on p. 44 <em>supra</em>; and the fourth is the probable date of the death of Buddha.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Duration of reigns.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Although the fact that the Śaiśunāga dynasty consisted of ten kings may be admitted, neither the duration assigned by the Purāṇas to the dynasty as a whole, nor that allotted to certain reigns, can be accepted. Experience proves that in a long series an average of twenty-five years to a generation is rarely attained, and that this average is still more rarely exceeded in a series of reigns as distinguished from generations.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The English series of ten reigns from Charles II to Victoria inclusive, 1649–1901 (reckoning the accession of Charles II from the death of his father in 1649), occupied 252 years, and included the two exceptionally long reigns of George III and Victoria, aggregating 124 years. The resultant average, 25·2 years per reign, may be taken as the maximum possible, and consequently 252 years are the maximum allowable for the ten Śaiśunāga reigns. The Purāṇic figures of 321 (<em>Matsya</em>) and 332 (<em>Vāyu</em>) years, obtained by adding together the durations of the several reigns, may be rejected without hesitation as being incredible. The <em>Matsya</em> account concludes with the statement, ‘These will be the ten Śaiśunāga kings. The Śaiśunāgas will endure 360 years, being kings with Kshatriya kinsfolk.’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-001" href="#sec-08-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Mr. Pargiter suggests that the figures ‘360’ should be interpreted as ‘163’. If that interpretation be accepted the average length of reign would be only 16·3, and it would be difficult to make the death of Buddha (<em>c.</em> 543 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>) synchronize with the dates of his contemporaries, Bimbisāra and Ajātaśatru. It is probable that the dynasty lasted for more than two centuries.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Anterior limit of date.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">As stated in the text, the traditional periods assigned to the Nanda dynasty of either 100 or 155 years for two generations cannot be accepted. The A-nanda mode of reckoning, used by the poet Chand, suggests 90 or 91 years as the true period. We thus get 342 (252 + 90) as the maximum admissible period for the Śaiśunāga and Nanda dynasties combined; and, reckoning backwards from the fixed point, 322 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, <em>the year 664</em> <span class="sc">b. c.</span> <em>is found to</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-72" data-page="72" data-folio="48" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n71_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 48">[p. 48]</button> be the <em>earliest possible</em> date for Śiśunāga, <em>the first king</em>. But of course the true date may be, and probably is, somewhat later, because it is extremely unlikely that twelve reigns (ten Śaiśunāga and two Nanda) should have attained an average of 25·16 years.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Probable actual reigns.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The reigns of the fifth and sixth kings, Bimbisāra, or Śrēnika, and Ajātaśatru or Kūṇika, were well remembered owing to the wars and events in religious history which marked them. We may therefore assume that the lengths of those reigns were known more or less accurately, and are justified in accepting the concurrent testimony of the <em>Vāyu</em> and <em>Matsya</em> Purāṇas, that Bimbisāra reigned for twenty-eight years.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Ajātaśatru is assigned twenty-five, or twenty-seven years by different Purāṇas, and thirty-two years by Tibetan and Ceylonese Buddhist tradition. I assume the correctness of the oldest Purāṇic list, that of the <em>Matsya</em>, and take his reign to have been twenty-seven years. The real existence of Darśaka (erroneously called Vaṁśaka by the <em>Matsya</em>) having been established by Bhāsa's <em>Svapna-Vāsavadattā</em>, his reign may be assigned twenty-four years, as in the <em>Matsya</em>. Udaya, who is mentioned in the Buddhist books, and is said to have built Pāṭaliputra, is assigned thirty-three years by the Purāṇas, which may pass.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-002" href="#sec-08-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Vāyu</em> and <em>Matsya</em> Purāṇas respectively assign eighty-five and eighty-three years to the sum of the reigns of kings n<sup>os</sup>. 9 and 10 together. These figures are improbably high, and it is unlikely that the two reigns actually occupied much more than fifty years. The figure 57 is assumed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The evidence as far as it goes, and at best it does not amount to much, indicates that the average length of the later reigns was in excess of the normal figure. We may assume, therefore, that the first four reigns, about which nothing is known, must have been comparatively short, and did not exceed some sixty years collectively. An assumption that these reigns were longer would unduly prolong the total duration of the dynasty, the beginning of which must be dated about 642 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Traditional dates of Mahāvīra and Gautama.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The existence of a great body of detailed traditions, which are not mere mythological legends, sufficiently establishes the facts that both Mahāvīra, the Jain leader, and Gautama Buddha were contemporary to a considerable extent with one another and with the kings Bimbisāra and Ajātaśatru.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-003" href="#sec-08-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-73"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="73" data-folio="49" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n72_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 49">[p. 49]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The deaths of these saints form well-marked epochs in the history of Indian religion, and are constantly referred to by ecclesiastical writers for chronological purposes. It might therefore be expected that the traditional dates of the two events would supply at once the desired clue to the dynastic chronology. But close examination of conflicting traditions raises difficulties. According to Pāli tradition Mahāvīra predeceased Buddha. But other reasons support the date 467 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, as advocated by Charpentier, and this fits in with the traditional date of Bhadrabāhu, who was the contemporary of Chandragupta Maurya.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-004" href="#sec-08-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> The year 527 (528–7) <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, the most commonly quoted date for the death of Mahāvīra, is merely one of several traditionary dates,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-005" href="#sec-08-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> but it is supported by the evidence of the Khāravela inscription. It is impossible to reconcile wholly the Jain traditions either among themselves or with the known approximate date of Chandragupta.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Death of Buddha, 543 <span class="sc">b. c.</span></aside></div><p class="prose-p">The variety of dates assigned for the death of Buddha is almost past counting.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-006" href="#sec-08-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Diwan Bahadur L. D. Swamikannu Pillai, working from the week-days recorded for events of Buddha's life, as given by Bigaudet, finds that they suit Tuesday, April 1, 478 <span class="sc">b. c.</span>, which, consequently, he regards as the true date of Buddha's death (<em>Ind. Ant.</em>, Oct. 1914 (vol. xliii), pp. 197–204). Three other arguments confirm the approximate date as being 487 or 486 <span class="sc">b. c.</span> :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(1) The ‘dotted record’ kept up at Canton until <span class="sc">a. d.</span> 489 showed 975 dots up to that year; 975 − 489 = 486 (Takakusu, <em>J. R. A. S.</em>, 1905, p. 51).</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-74"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="74" data-folio="50" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n73_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 50">[p. 50]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(2) Paramārtha, author of the <em>Life of Vasubandhu</em>, places the teachers Vrisha-gaṇa and Vindhya-vāsa, who flourished in the fifth century after Christ, as living in the tenth century after the Nirvāna (487 + 413 = 900).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(3) One form of the Khotan tradition places Dharma Asoka 250 years after the Nirvāna of Buddha, and makes him contemporary with the Chinese emperor, She-hwang-ti, the builder of the Great Wall, who came to the throne in 246 <span class="sc">B. C.</span>, became ‘universal emperor’ in 221, and reigned until 210 (Sarat Chandra Dās, <em>J. A. S. B.</em>, part 1, 1886, pp. 193–203; Tchang, <em>Synchronismes chinois</em>).<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-08-fn-007" href="#sec-08-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-08-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I do not believe that the date can be fixed with anything like certainty, and in opposition to the arguments in favour of 487 or 486 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> we now have the new reading of the Khāravela inscription which, if correct, obliges us to move back all the Saisunāga dates more than 50 years and therefore supports the Ceylon date for the death of Buddha, viz. 544 or 543 <span class="sc">B. C.</span> It may be argued that traditions preserved in Magadha should be more trustworthy than those recorded at a later date by monks in distant Ceylon : but there is ample evidence of the fact that Gautama Buddha was contemporary with both Bimbisāra or Srēnika and his son Ajātasatru or Kunika, and this being so, I feel compelled, until further light is thrown on the subject, to accept tentatively the earlier date, 543 <span class="sc">B. C.</span>, based on the chronology disclosed by the Khāravela inscription.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is impossible to fix precise dates for the pre-Maurya kings. The following table assumes the correctness of their names and order as given in the oldest Purānic lists, those of the <em>Matsya</em> and <em>Vāyu</em>, but no reliance can be placed on the recorded length of the reigns. Some may be correct, while it is certain that some are erroneous.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-75"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="75" data-folio="51" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/earlyhistoryofin0000smit_e6k6/page/n74_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 51">[p. 51]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHRONOLOGY (APPROXIMATE) OF ŚAIŚUNĀGA AND NANDA DYNASTIES</h2><div class="ledger-table-block" id="tbl-002"><div class="table-toolbar"><span class="table-id-badge">TBL-002 · FOLIO 75</span><span class="table-csv-links"><a href="/downloads/early-history-of-india-1924/datasets/tables/tbl-002_p0075.csv" download>Diplomatic CSV</a> · <a href="/downloads/early-history-of-india-1924/datasets/tidy/tbl-002_p0075.tidy.csv" download>Tidy CSV</a></span></div><div class="ledger-table-wrap" role="region" aria-label="Statistical table" tabindex="0"><table class="ledger-table"><caption>CHRONOLOGY (APPROXIMATE) OF ŚAIŚUNĀGA AND NANDA DYNASTIES</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col"><em>Serial No.</em></th><th scope="col"><em>King (Matsya Purāṇa).</em></th><th scope="col"><em>Length of Reign.
