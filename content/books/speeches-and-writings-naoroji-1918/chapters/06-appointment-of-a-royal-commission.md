<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-115"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="115" data-folio="101" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/speecheswritings00naor/page/n114_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 101">[p. 101]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">Appointment of a Royal Commission.</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">[<em>The following speech was delivered by Mr. Dadabhai Naoroji at the First Congress held in Bombay,</em> 1885.]</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I had no thought of speaking on this resolution,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-06-fn-001" href="#sec-06-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-06-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> but I see I must say something. There is a notion running under some remarks, that if a Conservative Government appoints a Committee, it will not be a good one. I do not think there is any good reason for that assumption. The Conservatives are not so bad that they will never do a good thing, nor are the Liberals so good that they never did a bad thing. In fact we owe good to both, and we have nothing to do with them yet as parties. We are thankful to either party that does us good. The Proclamation is the gift of a Conservative Government. I have some experience of a Parliamentary Committee and that Committee, a Liberal one; and yet under the Chairmanship of a gentleman like Mr. Ayrton, you cannot be sure of a fair hearing. On the other hand, a fair-minded Chairman and similar members, be they Conservatives or Liberals, would make a good Committee, and give a fair inquiry. Much depends upon the Secretary of State for India. If he is a fair-minded person and not biassed in any particular way, you will have a fair Committee. If we are asking for a Parliamentary Committee, we need not be afraid of asking one from a Conservative <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-116" data-page="116" data-folio="102" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/speecheswritings00naor/page/n115_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 102">[p. 102]</button> Government. A Secretary of State like Sir Stafford Northcote (Lord Iddesleigh) will give a fair one, and we should not assume that the present Secretary will not give a good one. We should only desire that Anglo-Indians may not be put in it, or only a few such in whom Natives have confidence. In such an inquiry Anglo-Indian officials are on their trial, and they should not be allowed to sit in judgment upon themselves.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">From the remarks already made, there appears to be an undecidedness, whether to ask for a Committee, or for a Royal Commission. And there seems also a notion underneath, that if we were not satisfied with the one we could ask for the other. Now we must bear in mind that it is not an easy thing to get a Parliamentary Committee or a Royal Commission, and that you cannot have either whenever you like. Do not suppose that if we have a Committee or a Commission and if we say we are dissatisfied with its results, we would at once get another for the asking. We must make up our minds definitely as to what we want and what would be the best thing for us. You should not leave it open whether there should be a Committee or Commission. Whichever you want, say it out once for all. In dealing with Englishmen, make up your minds deliberately, speak clearly, and work perseveringly. Then and then only can you hope to be listened to, and get your wishes. You must not show that you do not know your own mind. Therefore, know your own mind, and say clearly whether you desire a Parliamentary Committee, or a Royal Commission. It is evidently the desire here, that a full and impartial enquiry by fair and high-minded English statesmen, with an adequate number of Natives on the enquiring body, should be carried on in India. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-117" data-page="117" data-folio="103" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/speecheswritings00naor/page/n116_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 103">[p. 103]</button> itself. If so, then we must remember that a Parliamentary Committee can consist only of members of Parliament, and can sit in the Parliament House only. For our purpose to lay bare the actual conditions of India, an inquiry <em>in</em> India, in all departments and in the whole condition of India— material and moral—is absolutely necessary. No enquiry in England, and that with the evidence of Anglo-Indians chiefly—who themselves are on trial, and who would not naturally condemn their own doings and work—can ever bring out the truth about India's true condition and wants and necessary reforms. We, then irresistibly come to one conclusion, that an enquiry in India itself is absolutely necessary, and that such an enquiry can be conducted by a Royal Commission. Only let us clearly say our mind that we ask for a Royal Commission. Do not let there be any doubt about what we do really want. If I am right in interpreting your desire, then I say let there be no vague general resolution, but say clearly and distinctly that we require a Royal Commission.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-06-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-06-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">*.</a> <span class="fn-body"><em>Resolution.</em>—That this Congress earnestly recommends that the promised inquiry into the working of the Indian Administration here and in England should be entrusted to a Royal Commission, the people of India being adequately represented thereon, and evidence taken both in India and in England.</span></li></ol></section>
