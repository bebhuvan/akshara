DADABHAI’S CANDIDATURE IN THE PARLIAMENTARY ELECTION (1886).</span></h2><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>. . . , But he has far higher qualifications, whether for an Irish or an English seat. He is by far the ablest, the most high-principled, and the most accomplished native of India who has ever visited this country. He knows England, and English politics, and the English language, like an Englishman. And as a political official and administrator he has occupied some of the highest posts in his own country. His <em>forte</em> is finance—the rock on which the English rule of India may go to pieces some day or other. For the sake of India—which I know pretty well—and for the sake of English Imperialism, I should rejoice to see able natives of the stamp of the Hon. Mr. Dadabhai Naoroji in the English House of Commons.—<em>Weekly Times and Echo, London, May 30th</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Mr. Naoroji is another native of India whose presence in the House of Common would be of great advantage both to Englishmen and to our Indian fellow subjects. During forty years he has been to the front in every good movement in Bombay, where he is regarded by the natives with something like the same enthusiasm which Mr. Gladstone’s name inspires here. But more than half that time he has passed in England, moving to and fro, so that he is as familiar as any Englishman can be with our home politics, and in all respects he possesses rare intelligence and remarkable capacity for independent judgment and honest action. The Holborn Liberals will do as much honour to themselves as to Mr. Naoroji by doing their utmost to secure his election.—<em>Weekly Dispatch, 20th June</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>He has always taken a leading interest in the affairs of India and this country.—<em>Times, June 25th</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Like Ireland, India had grievances to be redressed, and it was right that her representatives should have an opportunity of bringing those grievances before the people of England. Apart from the special knowledge which he would bring to bear upon the Indian question, Mr. Naoroji, by 20 years’ residence in England, had made himself fully acquainted with English political questions, and by returning him to the House of Commons, Holborn would prove itself one of the grandest constituencies in Great Britain. (Cheers.)—<em>Times, 26th June</em> 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>His candidature is exciting great interest among the natives of India in the Metropolis, by whom he is regarded with the deepest respect and admiration.— <em>York Herald, June 25th</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Although in the estimation of his countrymen he is a great authority on all questions affecting the interests of India, he is also intimately acquainted with the politics of this country, having resided here at one period of his life for many years, and taken an active part in public matters.—<em>Rochdale Observer, 29th June</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>. . . And ruled with great distinction for some time over two millions of people (cheers). In fact, his administration was so beneficent that it called forth from the British Governors in India the warmest testimony of admiration. Well, he contended that a gentleman who had struggled so long for the rights of his own country, and who had shown such distinguished capacity as a statesman, ought to prove himself a worthy representative of Holborn.—<em>Freeman’s Journal, Dublin, 26th June</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The electors of the division have an opportunity of returning a sound Radical in place of Colonel Duncan, their late Tory member, and at the same time of giving a graceful example of the broad tolerance which is a feature of the democracy. It is to be hoped that our Indian fellow-citizen, stamped with the approval of the Holborn Liberal Association, will receive the support of all the workingmen voters.—<em>Reynolds, June 27th</em>, 1886.</p></blockquote></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-18"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="18" data-folio="iv" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/essaysspeechesa00paregoog/page/n17_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page iv">[p. iv]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He is a “Gladstonite.” . . . Apart altogether from party politics, we think the House of Commons would gain by his election, and are gratified by the cordial welcome Mr. Naoroji received from the electors of Holborn.—<em>Christian Million, July 1st</em>, 1886.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Holborn electors are in a dilemma. They rejoice in a Liberal candidate (the Hon. D. Naoroji). We hope he will get in. Although it is not an easy matter to articulate his name, we wish him a <em>pronounced</em> success.—<em>Funny Folks, Fleet Street, 3rd July</em>, 1886.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Mr. Naoroji has received a generous reception even from voters who do not approve of his Irish politics, and who, but for the fact that the only issue now before the country is that of Home Rule for Ireland, with its unknown consequences, would accord to him their hearty support.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“The friend of India and of Ireland,” as he was called at the meeting, was received with loud and long continued cheering.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is well that able and honourable men of the stamp of Mr. Naoroji and Mr. Ghose should obtain a hearing, not only before English constituencies, but before the English Parliament.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">. . . For example, it will be a good day for London when it can boast of a Municipal Government like that of Bombay—of which, by the way, Mr. Naoroji himself was a distinguished ornament. It is as the candidates for India that he and Mr. Ghose richly deserve the confidence of English constituencies.—<em>Echo, Strand, 3rd July</em>, 1886.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In Holborn the ex-Prime Minister of Baroda is creating a great impression on the constituency, and convincing many, both in and out of Holborn, that whoever is left out of Parliament, Dadabhai Naoroji ought to be inside.—<em>Pall Mall Gazette, Strand, 3rd July</em> 1886.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On Thursday night there was a grand Liberal demonstration at the Holborn Town Hall, in favour of the candidature of Mr. D. Naoroji, and we venture to say that a meeting of such dimensions and enthusiastic unanimity has never taken place in the Holborn Town Hall before.—<em>Holborn Guardian, 26th June </em> 1886.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">And holds such Radical opinions that he should make a good member for Holborn. Besides, it would be of great Imperial advantage if we had a real Indian to make us fully acquainted with Indian affairs at first hand in the House. Good luck to Naoroji!—<em>Penny Illustrated Paper</em>, 26th June 1886.</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">MR. DADABHAI’S APPOINTMENT IN THE BOMBAY LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“Mr. Dadabhai Naoroji has been offered, and has accepted, a seat in the Local Legislative Council, vacated by Sir Jamsetjee Jejeebhoy. The appointment is a most popular one. His services in the cause of this country point him out as the most fitting recipient of the honour.”—<em>Madras Times</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“The Native Press is gratified at such recognition, for in Mr. Dadabhai Naorojee is realized the popular ideal of a people’s man, quite as much as a colleague and counsellor for the head of Government.”—<em>Indian Spectator</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“In appointing Mr. Dadabhai Naoroji to a seat in the Legislative Council, the Government of Lord Reay has honoured itself, and honoured the native community more than it has honoured Mr. Dadabhai personally. Mr. Dadabhai’s ability, earnestness, and public spirit had long marked him out as the people’s champion and representative, but it was reserved to Lord Reay to reward those services, and to mark his approval of a career of disinterested patriotism.”— <em>Indu Prakash</em>.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-19"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="19" data-folio="v" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/essaysspeechesa00paregoog/page/n18_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page v">[p. v]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“No more competent person can be chosen from the Native community of Bombay. He is a close student of Indian economical problems, and is well known to leading men in England as well as in India. In choosing him as a member of the Legislative Council, the Governor of Bombay has exercised rare discretion, and has conferred honour on his Legislative Council as well as on the Native community of Bombay.”—<em>Indu, Madras.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“Bombay could not have produced a more able and experienced person to represent it in the local legislature, and the Government could not have selected a better man.”—<em>Hindoo Patriot.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“We may not hesitate to say that Mr. Dadabhai will prove an accession to the enlightened Government of Lord Reay.”—<em>Native Opinion.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“The appointment of Mr. Dadabhai Naoroji as a member of the Bombay Legislative Council has given universal satisfaction. Mr. (D.) Naoroji has been known for a long series of years as a remarkably able man, and the recognition of his claims has been a bare act of justice.”—<em>Indian Nation.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“The appointment of Mr. Dadabhai Naoroji, though it comes very late in the day, is a fitting recognition of the services rendered to his country by this most selfless of Indian patriots.”—<em>Indian Echo, Calcutta.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This our “Master” of former times was reckoned as the gem of the educated Natives. By fawning or sacrificing the independence of his opinion, he could have worked himself up to a high Government post, and would have by this time secured a pension. But Professor Dadabhai preferred the interests of the people to his own. . . . But Dame Fortune never favoured him, nor did Government give him his due, because high officials never recognized the worth of the “Good Dadabhai.” But what does a patriot care for Government recognition? . . . Really, no other Governor has brought together such solid pillars—such gems of the native community—as Messrs. Dadabhai, Badrudin, Telang, and Ranade, in the Legislative Council.—<em>Jam-e-Jamshed.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>There is no doubt that Mr. Dadabhai will prove himself a real representative of the people in the Legislative Council, and an excellent adviser of the Government. His complete knowledge of the political questions of India, and of the condition of the native public, his high political experience, acquired during a prolonged stay in England, are sufficient to prove his fitness for the post. We believe that, with his zeal for the interests of the public good, he will prove himself more useful than before to his fellow countrymen while in the Council. . . His Excellency Lord Reay has, so to say, made a new epoch by the nominations of Messrs. Dadabhai and Ranade to the Legislative Council. . . .—<em>Bombay Samachar.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>His Excellency the Governor and his councillors have made a really proper appointment in nominating Mr. Dadabhai to the Legislative Council in succession to Sir Jamsetjee, and the public of Bombay are doubly pleased at the recognition of the worth of a worthy gentleman.—<em>Akhbar-e-Soudagur.</em></p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The accession of this Master-mind in the ranks of such cultured men as the Honourable Messrs. Badrudin, Telang and Ranade, has greatly strengthened the voice of the native community. . . .—<em>Kaiser-i-Hind.</em></p></blockquote></div>
