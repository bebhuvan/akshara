<span id="page-293" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><h3 class="sec-h2">REPLY TO CRITICISMS.</h3><h4 class="sec-h3">(July 1876.)</h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">(Before an adjourned Meeting of Bombay Branch of the E. I. Association, on Monday evening, 24th July 1876, in the Framjee Cowasjee Institute. Mr. R. N. Khote in the chair.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">I begin</span> with Mr. Maclean. He has very much misunderstood my papers. As a first instance, when he asks me to deduct the exports of India (less the exports from Native States) from my estimate of the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-294" data-page="294" data-folio="277" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/essaysspeechesa00paregoog/page/n293_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 277">[p. 277]</button> production of India, he does not see that my estimate is for the <em>total production</em> in India, and that what is exported is not to be deducted therefrom.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As a second instance, he asks me to add 15,000,000<em>l.</em> for cotton manufactures. My estimate for production <em>includes</em> all <em>raw</em> cotton of British India, and I have already included in my estimate the additional value the raw cotton acquires by the application of industry in its conversion into cloth. Coal and foreign stores that are used in the mills are paid for from, and are therefore <em>included</em> in, the production I have estimated. The only additional value is that of the labour employed. But even if we allow the <em>whole</em> additional value acquired by raw cotton in its conversion into cloth, what will it be? Mr. Maclean’s “Guide to Bombay” (1875) (which is much later than the time of my notes) gives the number of the then working spindles as about 6 lakhs in the whole of the Bombay Presidency. Taking 5 oz. per day per spindle and 340 working days in the year, the total quantity of raw cotton consumed will be about 81,300 candies, which, at Rs. 150 per candy, amounts to about 1,220,000<em>l.</em> The price of cloth is generally about double the price of raw cotton, as I have ascertained from the details of two or three mills of Bombay; so that the whole addition caused by the mills to the value of raw cotton is only 1,250,000<em>l.</em>, say 1,500,000<em>l.</em> sterling to leave a wide margin. Then, again, there are, about the time of my notes, yarn imports into India worth about 2,500,000<em>l.</em>, per annum. This, of course, is paid for from the production of the country. The value added to it is its conversion into cloth. Now the cost of weaving is about 25 per cent. of the value of yarn, so that the value thus added is about 600,000<em>l.</em>, say 1,000,000<em>l.</em>, to include any contingency, making the total value to be added to the raw production of about 2,500,000<em>l.</em> If deduction is made for coal and foreign stores, this amount will be much lessened. Again, we know that hand-spinning is much broken down, and there can be but a little quantity of cloth woven out of hand-spun yarn in India. Giving even 500,000<em>l.</em> more for that industry, the outside total of addition to the raw produce would come to, as a high estimate, 3,000,000<em>l.</em> instead of the 15,000,000<em>l.</em> which Mr. Maclean asks me to add without giving a single figure for his data. Let him give any reasonable data, and I shall gladly modify my figures so far.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As a third instance of misunderstanding my paper, when Mr. Maclean asks me to take 5,000,000<em>l.</em> for gold and silver ornaments made in this country, he forgets that gold and silver are not produced in this country. All bullion is <em>imported</em>, and is paid for from the produce of India; it therefore <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-295" data-page="295" data-folio="278" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/essaysspeechesa00paregoog/page/n294_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 278">[p. 278]</button> can add nothing to my estimate of production. The only addition is the industry employed on it to convert it int o ornaments. This industry for the ordinary native ornaments will be amply covered by taking on an average an eighth of the value of the metal, which will give about 625,000<em>l.</em>, or say 750,000<em>l.</em> sterling, or even 1,000,000<em>l.</em>, while Mr. Maclean wants me to take 5,000,000<em>l.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As a fourth instance : while Mr. Maclean tells me to add 15,000,000<em>l.</em> and 5,000,000<em>l.</em> when there should be hardly one-fifth of these amounts, he does not see that I have actually allowed in my paper for all manufacturing industrial value, to be added to that of raw produce, as 17,000,000<em>l.</em>; and, further, for any omissions 30,000,000<em>l.</em> more (<em>East India Association Journal</em>, vol. ix., No. 4, page 257).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These four instances, I think, would be enough to show the character of Mr. Maclean’s criticism, and I pass over several other similar mistakes and mis-statements. In India the exports are declared at the value at the port of export. It is the same with the United States. The imports in India are declared at the “<em>wholesale cash price less trade discount</em>”<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-003" href="#sec-15-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">†</a></sup> at the <em>port of import</em>, which means the value at the foreign port of export, <em>plus</em> freight, insurance, and other charges to the Indian port of import, and also <em>plus</em> 10 per cent. for profits. This is the principle on which the imports are declared in the Custom Houses in India when the tariff value is not already fixed, or the market price not agreed upon by the importer and the Custom House. But in the case of the United States the declared value<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-004" href="#sec-15-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">‡</a></sup> of imports is only the value declared at the <em>foreign port</em> from which the merchandize was <em>exported</em>, which means <em>without</em> adding the cost of freight, insurance and other charges, and 10 per cent. profits. In that case the actual excess of imports over exports in the United States will be $717,774,734 = 149,536,403<em>l</em>.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-15-fn-006" href="#sec-15-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-15-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">†</a></sup> for the five years, 1869 to 1873, or $143,554,947 = 29,907,280<em>l</em>. per annum. Thus the correct result about the United States (on the principle of the Indian Custom House) is that, instead of there being an excess of <em>exports</em> of 15,000,000<em>l</em>. sterling per annum, there is actually an excess of <em>imports of double that amount</em>, or nearly 30,000,000<em>l</em>. sterling; thus making a difference between Mr. Maclean's and the correct figures of nearly (“ Hear, hear,” and cheers.)</p></div><h3 class="sec-h2">THE REMEDY.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">When I wrote these notes in 1873, or read them in 1876, I little dreamt that they would so soon obtain such terrible confirmation as the present deplorable famines have given them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The chief cause of India’s poverty, misery, and all material evils, is the exhaustion of its previous wealth, the continuously increasing exhausting and weakening drain from its annual production by the very excessive expenditure on the European portion of all its services, and the burden of a large amount a year to be paid to <em>foreign</em> countries for interest on the public debt, which is chiefly caused by the British rule.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The obvious remedy is to allow India to keep what it produces, and to help it as much as it lay in the power of the British nation to reduce her burden of the interest on the public debt; with a reasonable provision for the means absolutely necessary for the maintenance of the British rule.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">For this purpose it is necessary, on the one hand, to <em>limit within a certain amount</em>, the total of <em>every</em> kind of expenditure (pay, pensions and every possible kind of allowance), for the <em>European</em> portion of <em>all</em> the services both in England and India, directly or indirectly connected with or under the control of Government (including, therefore, guaranteed railways or other works, manufactures, local funds, &amp;c.); and to guarantee the public debt; and on the other hand, for the important political object of maintaining the British rule, to reserve by law for <em>Europeans alone</em>, such places of power as may be absolutely necessary for the purpose with a fair proportion of the army, within the limited amount of expenditure for the European portion of all the services.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Under some judicious arrangement of the kind I propose, the people of India, being allowed to keep most of what they produced, will rise in material prosperity under, what is upon the whole, a good system of administration, blessing the hand that gave such prosperity, and <em>increasing <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-308" data-page="308" data-folio="291" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/essaysspeechesa00paregoog/page/n307_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 291">[p. 291]</button> the benefit to the English people also manifold</em>, by the extensive commercial relations that must necessarily be then developed between England and India: and all fears of any danger to the British rule will be dispelled, both from the gratitude, loyalty, and <em>self-interest</em> of the people of India, and from the possession of important posts of power and a fair portion of the army by Europeans alone. Then will Macaulay's words be verified to the <em>glory of England</em>, as also to her <em>benefit</em>:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>“We shall never consent to administer the <em>pousta</em> to a whole community, to stupefy andalyse a great people whom God has committed to our charge;” and we shall not “keep a hundred millions (two hundred millions), of men from being our customers, in order that they might continue to be our slaves.”</p></blockquote></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-15-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-15-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">*.</a> <span class="fn-body">Monthly Reports on the Commerce and Navigation of the United States, by the Chief of the Bureau of Statistics, for the year ending 30th June 1871, p. 386.</span></li><li id="sec-15-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-15-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">*.</a> <span class="fn-body">Monthly Reports on Commerce and Navigation of the United States, by Edward Young, Ph.
