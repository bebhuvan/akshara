<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-35"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="35" data-folio="25" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/mughaladministra00sarkuoft/page/n34_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 25">[p. 25]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1"><strong>LECTURE III.</strong></h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><strong>The Treasury and Household Departments.</strong></h3><h4 class="sec-h3">§ 1. <em>Diwan or Chancellor of the Exchequer.</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Exchequer was presided over by the High Chancellor (<em>Diwan-i-ala</em>), who bore the courtesy title of <em>wazir</em>, and had two assistants called the <em>Diwan-i-tan</em> (or Diwan of Salaries) and the <em>Diwan-i-khalsa</em> (or Diwan of Crownlands.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The term <em>diwan</em> has been derived by orientalists from a hypothetical Iranian word <em>diwan</em>, connected with <em>dabir</em>, meaning ‘writer’,—(like the Turkish official title <em>bitikchi</em> which means ‘writer.’) The first use of the word diwan was in the sense of the public <em>registers</em> of receipts and expenditure kept in Greek (in Syria and Egypt) and in Pahlavi (in Persia) in the early years of Arab conquest. The name next passed to the officers of the Treasury and thence extended to the <em>government</em> of the Abbasid Khalifs, and in Saladin’s time to the Khalif himself. (<em>Encyclopædia of Islam, i.,</em> 1979.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We can get a clear general idea of the High Diwan’s positionl and duties if we bear the following facts in our mind :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(<em>a</em>) He was the intermediary between the Emperor and the rest of the official world.</li><li>(<em>b</em>) Practically all official records (except those of a technical character or containing minute details) had to be sent to his office for his inspection and storage under his control. His was the Public Records Office.</li><li>(<em>c</em>) Abstracts of all transactions and payments in all departments (except the smallest) had to be inspected and criticised by him.</li><li>(<em>d</em>) No order for appointment (except that of menials, workmen and privates in the army), promotion or large payment could have effect without his written sanction.</li><li>(<em>e</em>) He kept in his own hands the threads of the revenue collection and expenditure in all parts of the empire and often in great detail, and the Emperor kept his finger on the pulse of State finance by means of the abstracts which the diwan had to submit to him almost daily, and also by frequently asking him to read the details out.</li></ul></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-36"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="36" data-folio="26" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/mughaladministra00sarkuoft/page/n35_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 26">[p. 26]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>(<em>f</em>) Within certain limits he was given full discretion as to what matters he should report to the Emperor and take his orders on them, and what matters not.</li><li>(<em>g</em>) He was the embodiment of the Government on the administrative side and had to give formal leave (<em>rukhsat</em>) to all high officers on their appointment, charge them solemnly with their duties, and receive regular reports from them on the state of their provinces.</li><li>(<em>h</em>) The provincial diwans were constantly and minutely controlled and guided by him from the imperial Court. Revenue was his own special department, and these diwans and their underlings stood in direct contact with him.</li><li>(<em>i</em>) His seal and signature were necessary for the validation of most papers (including copies of the Emperor’s writings or reports of his verbal orders) and the authentication of the true copies of nearly all official documents.</li></ul></div><h3 class="sec-h2">§ 2. <em>Records that had to reach the Diwan’s office.</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Funds (<em>tahvils</em>) the records of which must go to the office of the diwan :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">I. [Z. 13<em>b</em>—14<em>b</em>].</li></ul></div><h4 class="sec-h3">§ 3. <em>Individual diwans' modes of transacting business.</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">We can get a clear idea of the High Diwan's office-work and method of transacting business from the recorded practice of some of the famous diwans of the middle of the 17th century. (D. 101<em>a</em>—102<em>a</em>.) Sadullah Khan (the famous wazir of Shah Jahan) used to proceed in the following way :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">First he read the letters received and replied to them. Then he selected the applications of the <em>tankha</em> department which were fit to be accepted, and submitted them to the Emperor with reasons for their acceptance, after signing his name at the bottom. Next, the papers of the department of rent-free land grant (<em>aima</em>) were signed. Thereafter the abstract memos (<em>yaddasht</em>) of the amin's office were signed by him. Finally he gave a hearing to the plaintiffs. Before leaving office he used to listen to the agents (of the princes, governors and other nobles) who pressed their requests.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The distribution-list of the <em>chauki</em> (<em>i.e.</em>, mounting guard round the palace on different nights by different nobles), was signed by him in the morning before all other work.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He used to draft the royal letters in private, but urgent ones were composed by him in his office room.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-40"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="40" data-folio="30" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/mughaladministra00sarkuoft/page/n39_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 30">[p. 30]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Jafar Khan's office-procedure was the following :—First he drafted the farmans and urgent letters. Then he read the papers of the <em>tankha</em> office, applications (<em>arzi</em>) and orders (<em>parwánahs</em>.) Next he immediately took into consideration any paper of the <em>Khalsa</em> department, or any <em>naqdi</em> or abstract-memo, etc., that any one submitted to him, disposed of it, and applied himself to other business.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Between the death of Sadullah Khan (7th April, 1656, O.S.), and the installation of Mir Jumla as his successor (July, 1656), Raja Raghunath was the acting <em>diwan</em>. He used to conduct the work of the Exchequer thus :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He reported to the Emperor the purport of the petitions received, in the same manner as the High Diwan, and then placed the papers before His Majesty. After drafting the royal letters (<em>farmáns</em>) he submitted them to the Emperor, after whose approval they were written out fair. On the attested copies (<em>tasdiqát</em>) of the letters (<em>risálah</em>) of the [provincial ?] diwan,— according to which the abstract memo was drawn up,—he used to write, ‘ Incorporate with the report of events (<em>wáqia</em>.)’ On the abstract-memos of the <em>waqia</em> he wrote ‘ Bring to His Majesty’s ears a second time (<em>arz-i-mukarrar</em>, for confirmation) and compare with original <em>waqia</em>. ’</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On the abstracts of <em>waqia</em>,—according to which the <em>farmáns</em> were written,—he wrote ‘ Write an imperial farman [on the subject.] ’ He put his signature on the <em>siáha</em> of jagirs and the <em>daul</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-004" href="#sec-05-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> of cash <em>tankha</em>, on the settlements of accounts (<em>muhásibat</em>), on the parwanahs of all offices, on the attested copies of the <em>aima</em> of the Crownland office, on the letters (<em>arzi</em>) from the treasuries, on the permits (<em>dastaks</em>) for <em>tankha</em> in cash : and also affixed his seal to the last-mentioned papers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">His seal was impressed on the rooms of the Public Treasury and the bags of money [in them], on the abstracts of the reports (<em>waqia</em>), on the acknowledgments (<em>qabz</em>) of the monthly salary of the <em>ahadis barqandazes</em> and menial servants, on the sheets of the reports from the provinces which the news-letter-reader at the imperial Court after reading them to His Majesty used to send to the imperial Record Office, on the slips (<em>nuskha</em>)<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-005" href="#sec-05-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">†</a></sup> of the provinces sent by the [provincial] diwan or other officers.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-41"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="41" data-folio="31" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/mughaladministra00sarkuoft/page/n40_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 31">[p. 31]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On <em>farmáns</em> he wrote ‘Enter in the book’ (<em>sabat numáid</em>.)</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">§ 4. <em>Duties of the Diwan of Crown lands.</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">So much for the High Diwan. The duties of the Diwan of Crownlands (<em>khalsa</em>) were the following [D. 87 <em>b</em>; Z. 30 <em>b</em>] :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The posting of the subahdars, faujdars, amins, diwani officers, kroris,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-006" href="#sec-05-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> and daroghas of the provinces,—of the amins, mushrifs and tahvildars of mahals,—of the fotahdars, clerks of issue (<em>bar-ámad-navisán</em>), daroghas, amins, mushrifs and khazanchis of [provincial] treasuries,—of the sazawals of papers,—the amins and kroris of arrears,—the collectors of the <em>mutáliba</em> (recovery of loans or advances made by Government) and [the dues from] the zamindars.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On parwanahs the High Diwan should write ‘correct’ (<em>sad</em>) and the Diwan of Khalsa should write ‘seen’ (<em>muláhiza shud</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Among the duties of the Diwan of Khalsa were to answer the inquiries of the lower officials (<em>‘amál</em>); to issue letters-patent (<em>sanad</em>) for services; to issue orders (parwanahs) on the parganahs assigning the cash salary (<em>tankha</em>) of the Emperor’s sons and grandsons, according to the <em>daul</em> prepared by the cash department [of the Treasury];—also parwanahs for the recovery of Government advances, for the payment of the fixed salaries of the servants (<em>áhal-i-khidmat</em>), the customary commission of the fotahdars, and the collection fee of the kroris;—parwanahs on complaints,—for the attachment [of property or crops] for unpaid arrears,—for calling up money from the [subordinate] treasuries,— for cash assignment in payment of things ordered to be manufactured [for the State, <em>farmáish</em>] and whatever is ordered on the provinces and troops,—for investigating any matter reported in the news-letters;—to write letters by order (<em>hasb-ul-hukm</em>) on any subject as desired by the Emperor;—to issue permits (<em>dasta</em>) addressed to the clerks (<em>mutasaddi</em>) of the treasuries about the fixed <em>tankha</em> which might be ordered to be paid out of the Public Treasury (<em>i.e.</em>, the Central Treasury ?),—permits addressed to the khazanchis about the <em>tankha</em> that may be ordered on [sub] treasuries and the troops,—passports for roads, passes for workmen.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-42"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="42" data-folio="32" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/mughaladministra00sarkuoft/page/n41_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 32">[p. 32]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Diwan of Khaisa was left free to report to the Emperor the summary of the despatches of the lower diwani officers at his discretion, and to reply to those that required reply. The other despatches were to be initialled by him without reporting to the Emperor.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Then we have lists of the papers which had to be read to the Emperor by this Diwan and of those that had to be merely initialled by him without submission to his master. Even in the case of the former class, the Diwan could withhold unimportant papers if he thought fit. He had also to report to His Majesty the cash balances of the treasuries very frequently and the doing of the zamindars.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">All parwanahs for cash payment in the imperial Household department were issued by the Khan-i-saman, and the Diwan merely endorsed them.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Diwan of Khalsa had to endorse the security-bond (<em>tamassuk-i-zámini</em>) of the workmen and for the repayment of State loans (<em>mutáliba</em>), and the indemnity-bonds (<em>muchilka</em>) signed by certain officers binding themselves to carry out certain specified tasks on pain of fine.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Reports from the fotahdars and khazanchis of the parganah and provinces stating the amounts they had recovered from the loans or advances made by Government, [had to be read by the Diwan of Khalsa.]</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The High Diwan should write ‘sanctioned’ (<em>manzur shud</em>) and the Diwan of Khalsa ‘seen’ on the <em>badar-navisi</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-007" href="#sec-05-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> of the <em>amlas</em> in the Khalsa office.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The High Diwan should write ‘entrust to such and such a fund’ (<em>tahvil-i-falan numáid</em>) on the audit-reports that are brought [to him] for signature by the <em>mustaufis</em>, <em>viz.</em>, the mustaufis of revenue [or of the <em>amlas</em>, variant], the mustaufi of <em>rás-ul-mál</em> (=trading stock or the original prices of articles), the mustaufi of treasuries, the mustaufis of arrears, the mustaufi of <em>ámwál</em> [attached property of officers who died indebted to Government], and the mustaufi of <em>jaziya</em>. He should also sign the attestation copy of the fixed salaries of workmen.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-43"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="43" data-folio="33" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/mughaladministra00sarkuoft/page/n42_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 33">[p. 33]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The High Diwan should sign the sheets of the abstracts (<em>zimn</em>)<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-05-fn-008" href="#sec-05-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-05-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">*</a></sup> of imperial farmans and the Diwan of Khalsa should sign the sheets of the abstract of Diwani, faujdari and amini works (<em>khidmát</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>siaha</em> of <em>ahkam</em> (imperial orders in writing) should be sent directly to the offices of the High Bakhshis and the Khan-i-saman.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The duties of the Diwan of Khalsa include—investigating into the notes (<em>nuskha</em>) of the revenue department,—correction of the <em>tumar-i-jama</em> (record of total standard assessment) of the Crownlands, estimating (<em>baráwardan</em>) the expenditure of the troops accompanying the Emperor.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The facts about dismissed officers, compiled from the office records should be initialled by the High Diwan. An attested copy of it should be sent to the officer concerned and the <em>siaha</em> to the offices of the High Bakhshi, etc.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Diwan of Khalsa drew up the statement of the income and disbursement of the imperial camp and of all the subahs, and kept the records of the <em>tankha</em> (allowance) of the Begams and lists of the <em>mahals</em> (villages) of the Crownlands, workmen and annual lists (?-statistical abstracts ?)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The High Diwan’s office kept copies of all sheets signed by the Emperor.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Then we have a long list of the papers which the Diwan of Khalsa had to secure from different classes of officials, such as revenue officers, amins, kroris, collectors of <em>sair</em> mahals, clerks of the treasuries, as well as reports of <em>pandit-khánah</em>, etc.</p></div><h4 class="sec-h3">§ 5. <em>Duties of the Diwan of Tankha.</em></h4><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Diwan-i-tan</em> dealt with the following matters [Z. 34<em>b</em> —35 <em>a</em>; If the requisite stores are wanting, write out a statement of the expenditure (<em>saranjám</em>) necessary for supplying the want, apply to the proper authority, take from him a <em>tankha</em> on the diwan for the <em>saranjam</em> and make the things.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“In order that the Khan-i-saman may be in a position to supply all articles that may be required, it is his business to buy them with Government money or on credit from traders, and keep them ready and deliver them to Government at need at the market price. If he is a rich man himself, he ought to buy and stock the things, so that he may not have to beg of others at the time of need, but issue them after valuation at the market rate. Thus his master will have no occasion to be displeased at delay in supplying. If the Khan-i-saman supplies his own things at the market rate, most probably Government will make a saving by the transaction; but the clerks of the Government will allege that he is selling things to State at profit; therefore he ought to ask the Emperor beforehand to advance him money to buy everything and stock it for supply when needed.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">“Old and secondhand stores should be sold to the army, after taking permission [of the Emperor] and learning their current prices from a <em>muqim</em> [broker or appraiser.] Keep the
