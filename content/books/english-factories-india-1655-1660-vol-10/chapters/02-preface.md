<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-9"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="9" data-folio="9" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n8_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 9">[p. 9]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> changed plan on which this series has been resumed, after the pause imposed by the war, requires a few words of explanation. In the earlier volumes the method followed was that of the calendars prepared by the Public Record Office, that is to say, a detailed analysis was given of every document available, and these summaries were arranged in strict chronological order, a unifying element being supplied in the shape of an introduction. Now, however, a point has been reached at which the materials rapidly increase; for example, the documents available for the year 1659 number nearly five hundred and those for 1660 about three hundred, as compared with a yearly average of seventy-five in the volumes already published. At the same time, as the increase is mainly due to the survival of certain letter-books, &amp;c., transcribed from the records kept at particular factories, many of the documents are concerned with commercial details which have lost their importance, and there is a considerable amount of repetition. In these circumstances, to persevere on the old lines would have greatly retarded progress and would have entailed an expenditure difficult to justify. It was decided, therefore, to change the method of procedure: to extract merely those passages which seemed to merit preservation, and to connect them by a narrative which would at the same time embody the information obtained from other documents which it was not thought necessary to quote in full. This plan admits of an arrangement, partly chronological and partly geographical, which makes it easier for the reader to follow the course of events. The outcome of the change is that the present volume covers a period of six years instead of four (the average hitherto), and contains the results of an examination of over eleven hundred documents, few of which have been utilized before.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A full calendar of all the documents available for 1655–9, which had been prepared before the new system was adopted, has been <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-10" data-page="10" data-folio="6" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n9_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 6">[p. 6]</button> placed among the <em>Factory Records, Miscellaneous</em>, series at the India Office, as vols. 28–31.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Since extracts from the same document often appear on different pages, it has been thought advisable to concentrate the references in a table at the end of the volume. This not only saves a large number of footnotes, but also avoids distracting the reader’s attention with information probably not wanted at the moment.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The materials for the volume have been found mostly amongst the India Office records, particularly the <em>Letter Books</em>, the <em>Original Correspondence</em>, and the various sections of the <em>Factory Records</em>. Use has also been made of a volume containing copies of letters received at Madras, 1658–60, which is now among the Rawlinson MSS. in the Bodleian Library. The only Indian Record Office possessing documents of the period is that at Bombay; and the Bombay Government kindly furnished transcripts of these for the purpose of the present work. The transcripts have been added to the Surat section of the <em>Factory Records</em> as vol. 102 B.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Throughout the volume the factories in Persia receive almost as much attention as those in India. This is in accordance with the practice in the previous volumes; and in fact English trade in the former country was so closely bound up with the fortunes of the Surat Presidency (of which those factories formed part) that no other course would be satisfactory.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In making quotations, contractions have been extended and modern practice has been followed in regard to punctuation, capital letters, and the use of <em>u</em> for <em>v</em> and of <em>j</em> for <em>i</em>. In the spelling of names the method of the <em>Imperial Gazetteer of India</em> has been adopted.</p></div>
