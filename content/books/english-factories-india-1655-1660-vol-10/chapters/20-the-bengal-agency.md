<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-200"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="200" data-folio="188" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n199_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 188">[p. 188]</button></div><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">THE BENGAL AGENCY, 1658</span></h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">When</span>, on the last day of 1657, ‘the Governour, Deputie, and Committees for the new Joint Stock for India’ penned their first letter to Bengal, the only Englishmen in that country were a few private adventurers and the merchants who had been sent out by the syndicate promoted by Maurice Thomson (now the Governor of the resuscitated East India Company). It was therefore to a number of these merchants, viz. George Gawton, Thomas Billidge, William Blake, Thomas Hopkins, Richard Chamberlain, and Ion Ken, that the letter was addressed. It informed them of the grant of an exclusive charter and the subscription of a new stock; announced the dispatch of the <em>Blackamoor</em> and other ships; and empowered them to dispose of the cargoes of these vessels, and to arrange for their relading, ‘without having any subordinacy to our Agent etc. at the Coast’. The goods to be sent home were to consist of saltpetre, sugar, raw silk, cotton yarn, turmeric, ‘bowgees’ [cowries: Port. <em>buzios</em>], gumlac, taffetas, ‘sannoes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-001" href="#sec-20-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> adaties’, and any cinnamon the factors could procure (from the Dutch or other merchants). Gawton and his colleagues were warned that ‘the whole trade aperteyneth singly unto us, exclusive unto all others’, and were charged not only to refuse any commissions from private adventurers, but also to hinder any investment being made on behalf of such interlopers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These instructions were sent by the <em>Blackamoor</em>, which, after calling at Madras, appears to have reached Balasore towards the end of July, 1658. Another copy went by the <em>Anne</em>, which quickly followed her consort from the Coast to Bengal. The <em>Love</em>, which was the next vessel to arrive from England, brought out the list of the new establishment, as settled by the Committees in the middle of January, 1658. As already noted, the factories in Bengal were formed into an Agency, independent of Madras, but under the control of the President and Council at Surat. Of the merchants available in the country George Gawton was chosen to be Agent, at 100<em>l.</em> per annum, with his head-quarters at Hūglī; Matthias Halstead to be Third there (30<em>l.</em>), and Thomas Davies Fifth <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-201" data-page="201" data-folio="189" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n200_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 189">[p. 189]</button> (20<em>l.</em>). At Balasore Thomas Hopkins was to be Chief (40<em>l.</em>) and William Daniel Third (30<em>l.</em>). At Kāsimbāzār Ion Ken was made Chief (40<em>l.</em>) and Job Charnock Fourth (20<em>l.</em>). At Patna the post of Chief was given to Richard Chamberlain (40<em>l.</em>). To fill the remaining posts, the Company dispatched in the <em>Love</em> William Ragdell to be Fourth at Hūgli (20<em>l.</em>), Walter Rogers to be Second at Balasore (30<em>l.</em>), Joshua Wright to be Fourth there (20<em>l.</em>), Daniel Sheldon to be Second at Kāsimbāzār (30<em>l.</em>), and Samuel Cutler (a youth) to serve at Balasore. William Vassall was also sent in the <em>Persia Merchant</em> to be Third at Patna (30<em>l.</em>), and John Priddy, intended for Third at Kāsimbāzār (30<em>l.</em>), went out by some ship not named. For the post of Second at Hūgli (40<em>l.</em>), William à Court was at first nominated; but a dispute over his taking a servant with him caused the Company to cancel his appointment. They were induced, however, to reconsider the matter, and on 8 March, 1658, appointed him to be Chief at Vīravāsaram, and nominated Jonathan Trevisa to go out to Bengal in his place. Trevisa, as already mentioned, embarked in the <em>Persia Merchant</em>, and was much delayed by the shipwreck of that vessel. Even when he reached Madras, he was in no hurry to proceed to his post at Hūgli; and since Gawton was already dead when the <em>Love</em> arrived, the Bengal factors were left without a head for more than a year.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The appointment of Trevisa was notified in a letter of 27 March, 1658, sent in the <em>Merchant’s Delight</em>. This made certain other changes. Vassall was promoted to be Second at Patna, Samuel Bayley (now sent out) was appointed Third there, and Henry Aldworth (already in Bengal) was made Fourth. Daniel was transferred to Petapoli, Wright succeeding him as Third at Balasore, and John Offley being appointed Fourth. A postscript authorized the engagement, for any vacancy, of ‘one Mr. Pickring, of whom wee have had some good report’, and who was understood to be on the spot. The list of factors, as thus altered, had suffered considerable changes by the end of the year. Apart from the substitution of Trevisa for Gawton, there were gaps caused by the non-arrival of Vassall (now a prisoner in Ceylon), by the transfer of Daniel to Petapoli, and the deaths of Ragdell, Wright, Offley, and Bayley. A letter from Balasore to Madras of 3 December, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-202" data-page="202" data-folio="190" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n201_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 190">[p. 190]</button> 1658, gives the actual arrangement as follows : at Hūgli, Hopkins, Rogers, Charnock, and Thomas Gifford ; at Balasore, Ken, Sheldon, and Cutler ; at Kāsimbāzār, Halstead, Davies, and Priddy ; and at Patna, Chamberlain, Aldworth, and James Pickering. Of these, Gifford and Pickering had been recruited on the spot, the latter in consequence of the permission received from the Company.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is curious to notice that two of these factors were closely related to prominent ecclesiastics. Ion Ken (whose Christian name is often given wrongly as John) was the elder brother of the celebrated bishop. After his return from India, he was employed in the home service of the Company, beginning as an assistant to the auditor (Oct. 1673) and rising to the post of Cashier-General (April, 1682) ; in the following year, however, he got into trouble and was dismissed.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-002" href="#sec-20-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Sheldon was a son of Ralph Sheldon (see a pedigree in Le Neve's <em>Knights</em>), and nephew of Gilbert Sheldon, the well-known Archbishop of Canterbury. Daniel and his brother Joseph (knighted in 1666 and Lord Mayor ten years later) were the archbishop's executors and residuary legatees (as Professor Oman has kindly informed me). On Daniel's arrival in India he was introduced to Pickering, in a letter from Thomas Otway at Masulipatam (<em>O.C.</em> 2664), as 'nephew to the learned and loyall Dr. Sheldon, and cannot bee imagined but richly stored with ingenuity and honesty'. He returned to England (doubtless with a fortune) in 1666, and served as a Committee of the East India Company in 1672–4, 1676–8, and 1680. He married Judith, daughter of George Rose and relict of Sir Maurice Diggs, and settled at Ham Court in Surrey. Evelyn visited him there on 25 August, 1678, and noticed the 'many curiosities of India' in his house. At the end of 1697 Sheldon became one of the securities for Thomas Pitt, on the latter's appointment as Governor of Fort St. George.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Reverting to the letter (dated 27 Feb., 1658) carried out by the <em>Love</em>, in which the new establishment was fixed, we note <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-203" data-page="203" data-folio="191" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n202_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 191">[p. 191]</button> that the allowance for housekeeping (for all four factories) was 300<em>l.</em> per annum, with an addition of 15<em>l.</em> for each factor above the number of fourteen. Rules were laid down as to the giving of security, the signing of covenants, &amp;c., on similar lines to those prescribed to Surat and Madras; and instructions were given for valuing and turning over to the New General Stock any remaining goods, &amp;c., of the United Joint Stock. Of the directions concerning merchandize we need only notice those relating to the purchase of silk:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>In our preceeding letter you will perceive that wee gave you order to furnish us with Bengala silke to the vallue of 3000<em>l.</em> sterling, and desired that it might be sent us in long skaynes, if soe procureable. But since the dispeeding of those advices, wee have had some conference and received direction from a workeman, who from experience adviseth us to contradict that our order of having the silke in long skaynes, because their lengths causeth them in the winding very much to breake, and will not part without much trouble. Wee therefore herewith send you a skayne of silke for a patterne, of which length wee desire that our parcell may be procured; and entreate you to take espetiall care that it be well sorted and chosen, that the insides of the skaynes may be answerable in goodnesse to the outsides, and that it may be made a perfect thred, singly reeled, and not two threads together. And if you shall send us any of that sort of silke which usually goes here under the names of Piggtailes, alter not the lengths of the skaynes from what formerlie, but take a more then ordinarie inspection into this sort, which useth to come very artificially covered with fine silke, and the middle of the skaynes extraordinarie course and fowle, to the great disparagment of the commoditie when it comes to sale.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Love</em> reached Balasore on 22 September, 1658, and the <em>Merchant’s Delight</em> on 11 October. The money and goods brought by these two vessels, added to what had been consigned by the <em>Blackamoor</em> and <em>Anne</em>, made up a stock of just over 23,000<em>l.</em> (<em>O.C.</em> 2665). Of this stock William Blake (both Gawton and Billidge being dead) took possession, on the strength of the Company’s letter of 31 December, 1657, although later dispatches showed that he was not included in the new establishment. On discovering this, Hopkins and the other factors protested, with the result that Blake yielded to them the management of the stock of the <em>Merchant’s Delight</em>, retaining, however, that of the preceding three <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-204" data-page="204" data-folio="192" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n203_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 192">[p. 192]</button> vessels. This was reported to Madras in a letter of 7 November, 1658, which also announced that</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Wee have now a mint in Ballasore; which doth somewhat advance the price of silver in reference to sale; so that now a piece of eight will yeild (if it be weighty) rupees chellena [<em>chalani</em>, current] two and one anna.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The <em>Merchant's Delight</em> was sent back to the Coromandel Coast on 15 November, 1658, carrying a letter from Hopkins, Halstead, Rogers, and Davies (of that date), in which complaint was made against her commander (Thomas Bell) for not sailing nine days earlier. A fear was expressed that anchorage dues would have to be paid for that vessel; but the factors intended to evade this, if possible, and the next year, by addresses to the prince, &amp;c., ' to breake this new custome, at first brought on perticuler ships and now demanded of all '.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-003" href="#sec-20-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> With this went a private letter from Hopkins and Ken, complaining of Blake's action in excluding them from the control of the cargoes of the three remaining ships. He had now, however, promised to give them an account of all his transactions in the matter and to hand over any balance remaining. This being so, they deemed it advisable to maintain a show of friendship with him for the present season, ' that the natives might not take notice of any alteracions and so expect double visitacions with presents '.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The next of the Company's ships to quit Balasore was the <em>Blackamoor</em>, which sailed on 5 December, with two letters for Madras (dated two days earlier), one from Blake and the other from Hopkins and his colleagues. The former reported that the governor, Malik Beg, had been ' very abusive, exacting what presents he pleases; if not, hinders all proceedings '. The latter told a similar tale of trouble, but added that they hoped to be allowed to trade the following year, ' by complying somewhat more then ordinary with the governours in these parts '. Blake had attributed the short supply of saltpetre sent in the <em>Love</em> to an unwillingness on their parts to assist; but the real reasons were (according to the factors) <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-205" data-page="205" data-folio="193" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n204_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 193">[p. 193]</button> that ‘ the last yeare his brother Billedge (deceased) and himselfe sent hence a great part of their estates ’; that what Blake had left was in broadcloth, &amp;c., which could not be trucked for saltpetre ; and that he had borrowed so freely before that he could not get credit ‘ in these troublesome tymes ’. With a view to the next season’s shipping, Hopkins and his colleagues proposed to give out orders at Patna for 25,000 maunds of saltpetre, and ‘ about Hughly or Chandrecone ’ <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-004" href="#sec-20-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> for 3,000 bales of sugar. They added :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Captain John Durson and Mr. William Pitts, wee feare, will be somwhat troublesome to us ; and how to convey them away perforce wee know not, the one [i.e. Pitt] being married here to one accompted a Moorish woman, and the other being enterteyned in the Prince of Bengala’s service.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Durson had some sixteen years earlier commanded one of the vessels sent out by Courteen’s Association, and had now been for a long period trading on his own account in India. He had a standing grievance against the Company’s factors on account of some chank shells of his which had been seized and sent to Bengal for sale. In a letter of 20 December, 1658, the Balasore factors reported that Durson had complained to the Governor about the matter, but that functionary had refused to interfere. They on their side had exhibited the Company’s letter, ordering the withdrawal of all unauthorized Englishmen, and had asked the Governor to seize and deliver Durson to them, to be sent home. Malik Beg declined to do this, though he assured the factors that he would not hinder them from seizing Durson themselves. However, they feared lest in that case they would be made responsible for the money he owed in Bengal ; besides, his funds were running low and his credit was declining, and so he was not likely to give much further trouble. If the Madras Agent thought differently, they suggested that he should write himself to Malik Beg, desiring him to arrest Durson and send him to the Coast ; or else a power of attorney should be obtained from some of Durson’s creditors, authorizing the Bengal factors to demand an account from him. The same letter announced that the remains of the United Joint Stock at Hūgli, Balasore, and Kāsimbāzār had been duly valued <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-206" data-page="206" data-folio="194" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n205_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 194">[p. 194]</button> and bills drawn on the New General Stock for the amount. Mention was also made that Blake was preparing to send a sloop to Pegu, carrying chiefly freighted goods, but also certain prohibited commodities of his own; but the factors would endeavour to frighten the Moors from lading any goods, by warning them that these would probably be seized on arrival.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On 29 December, 1658, Blake managed to dispatch the <em>Anne</em> to the Coast, with a cargo to the value of 34,832 rupees. The <em>Love</em> must have quitted Balasore a few days later, as we find that she reached Masulipatam on 9 January, 1659, and Madras on the 14th.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The documents of the year include a further batch (ten in all) of private letters addressed by various individuals to James Pickering, mostly from Masulipatam or the neighbouring factories. To the contents of one or two of these letters allusion has already been made. Others are taken up with matters of private trade and the sad condition of his correspondent of 1657, George Bradshaw, who was reported, in a letter of 16 August, 1658, to be lying at Winter's house in Madapollam 'mad as any in Bedlam', though some of the later communications gave hopes of his recovery. An extract from one of these epistles is worth quoting as reflecting the convivial habits of the factors. It is from a certain Thomas Bateman at Balasore, and bears no date except August, 1658.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-20-fn-005" href="#sec-20-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-20-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> It is addressed to Kāsimbāzār.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>You have been frequently remembred here by your freinds when upon the bubbing [i.e. drinking] designe, which since your absence is not so well carried on as it ought to bee. Wee are all generally so sensible of the want of your company that you have been often wisht for. Your freind Mr. Ken is not yet recovered, but has every other day his wonted fitts; and poore Job [Charnock] begins to droope and simpathize with Ions sicknes. I hope by this time you are acquainted with the carowsing Dutchmen, that you may bee able to beare up against those melancholy thoughts that assault the solitary. This day I meane to drinke your health, with a <em>Vivat Jacobus Pickering</em>, that hee may suddainly and safely returne to exhilerate the hearts of those that love him.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A subsequent letter of 14 October from the same correspondent (then at Hūgli) is in a different vein. Bateman had evidently <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-207" data-page="207" data-folio="195" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n206_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 195">[p. 195]</button> received one from Pickering making some display of erudition, and he retorted in kind, as follows :</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Ciceronian Sir, Your elaborate lines have so puzled my incultivated intellect that it was some time ere I resolv[ed] whether by silence to incurr the censure of uncivill negligence, or by writing to discover my foolish impertinence. At length, because therby I shall least wrong my freind, and <em>ex malis minimum eligendum</em>, I fell on the latter, choosing rather to render my selfe rediculous then my freind displeased. But from a chip of a rough hew'n logg hee that looks for better then wooden phrases will starve his expectation ; <em>ex quovis enim ligno non fit Mercurius</em>. I was never yet so good a proficient in the schoole of litterature as to know what deity to invoke for those Heliconian irrigations which should so sublimate my thoughts as to make mee capable of returning an answer suitable to the sublimity of your style ; but in their stead Heaven has been so propitious as to afford mee some divine irrorations, sufficient to advise you that hereafter you accompt not your solitude a curse, since it produced such rare effects in our late soveraigne. Remember Dr. Browne : <em>nunquam minus solus quam cum solus</em>. Now is the only time for you to commune with your owne heart, and by the publishing of your pious soliloquies there may bee hope in time you may bee preferr'd to bee one of Maurice Thomsons planters and propagatours of the Gospell in these heathenish parts ; which that you may so proove, you shall not want the prayers of him who is Pathetically yours, <span class="sc">Thomas Bateman</span>.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As Sir Henry Yule remarks, this letter shows recent familiarity with the works of Sir Thomas Browne ; and we are not surprised to find that in January, 1659, Bateman sent to Pickering copies of the <em>Religio Medici</em> and of <em>Enquiries into Vulgar Errors</em>, as also a <em>Treatise of Bodies</em>.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-20-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-20-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">A kind of white cotton goods (<em>sānu</em>).</span></li><li id="sec-20-fn-002" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-20-fn-002" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">Ken, by the way, was evidently a musician.
