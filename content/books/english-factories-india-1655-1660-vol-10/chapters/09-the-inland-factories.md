<span id="page-73" class="sec-page-anchor" aria-hidden="true"></span><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Owing</span> to the survival at home of a ‘copie booke’ of letters received at Surat from December, 1655, to November, 1656 (<em>Surat Factory Records</em> at the India Office, vol. 103), and of a similar volume at Bombay (transcripts from which form vol. 102 <span class="sc">b</span> of the India Office Series) continuing the series for a year longer, we learn much about what was happening at the various factories subordinate to Surat. Pre-eminent in interest are the letters <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-74" data-page="74" data-folio="62" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n73_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 62">[p. 62]</button> received from Jesson at Agra, of which the first, dated 6 February, 1656, contained the intelligence that a new Governor had been appointed to Surat.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Hoffus Nossur, Governour of Surrat, is tageered, and one formerly called Sheak Boodun, now new named Sheak Mahmud Ummee,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-001" href="#sec-09-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> apointed to succeed him; who is already departed from Dilly [Delhi] towards Surrat. Hee was three yeares agoe some few months Duan of Agra, and for his covetuousnesse was removed and made Amin of Somber; which office he hath ever since continued, till now that hee was (to take upon him this goverment) called to courte, and uppon his arrivall presented by Sadula Ckawne (whose favourite hee is) to the King, vested with a sirpaw, and the next day dispeeded. Soe that Annuntidas<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-002" href="#sec-09-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> had not time to speake with him about the insureance businesse; but Sadula Ckawne promiseth, when hee is assured of his arrivall to Surrat, to write to him, either to make the shroff[s] pay the mony there, or send them to courte, which voyage, I beleive, they will hardly undertake.... The premencioned new Governour is held to bee a very covetuous man; one that will doe nothing without, but any thing with, money. Of this I thought good to give you notice, that you may knowe how to deale with him.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sadullah Khān, as already mentioned, was at this time Wazīr at Delhi and had great influence with Shāh Jahān. He now desired (Jesson reported)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Two excellent English swords of this country fashion, large, with broad backs. If you will please to furnish mee with such to present him, theire cost would happely not bee lost, for hee would more esteeme of them then 20 times the valew. And if hee bee not pleasured, hee may perhaps doe the Company a discourtesie, in forceing payment of the hassell [<em>hāsil</em>, tax] of broadcloth in Jehanabaud,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-003" href="#sec-09-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> which is 1½ per cent., pretended not to bee pardoned by the firmaund,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-004" href="#sec-09-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> because Sha Jahanabaud was builded since the firmaund was given; soe that Anuntidas hath much adoe to avoide its payment, and that by the favour of Sadula Ckawne.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The latter went on to say that The reports runs that this King will send an army against Golcunda and Decan [i.e. Bījāpur] in favour of Meire Jimla, lately <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-75" data-page="75" data-folio="63" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n74_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 63">[p. 63]</button> generall of the King of Gulcondah[s] forces, who, haveing subdued the large territory of Ram Rajah,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-005" href="#sec-09-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> is reported now to bee in rebellion, profferring Sha Jehan to doe his best to subject the whole country of Gulcondah to his more absolute comand. If this warr goe forwards, it may perhaps bee as dangerous to send the saltpeeter by the way of Brampore [Burhānpur] as Ahmahdabad, for if the Prince Oran Zeab have occasion for any, noe doubt but hee will stop it, and then it may bee a businesse very troublesome to gett mony for it.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On this point Jesson desired early instructions, as he already had 120 maunds in hand, and expected to make the amount up to about 200 bales. He had heard from Ananti Dās that most of the broadcloth at Delhi had already been sold. Part of the proceeds would be absorbed in paying for the saltpetre; the rest ‘shalbee disposed of as you please to commaund’, but Jesson deprecated its being handed over to the broker, Morār Dās, ‘hee being a man well enough knowne to bee none of the wisest or ablest’. The Dutch factors, having provided 450 bales of indigo at Khurja and Bīana, had now returned to Agra; but ‘Franq Vanderburgh’ had been sent back to buy 100 more. The price at Hindaun was 33 rupees per maund, and would have fallen lower but for this and other fresh demands. On the question when he would be able to close the factory and proceed to Surat, Jesson was significantly silent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This letter was speedily followed by another (dated 23 Feb.), in which Jesson announced that he had made an agreement for the transportation of the saltpetre to Surat by</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>A parcell of Decan oxen bound back to Brampore, willing to accept of a freight to Surratt at a very cheap rate. I caused the owners to bee called before mee, and have come to an agreement with them for transporte of our saltpeter at 11½ rupees for 4 maunds 8 seare, they paying the jaggatt<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-006" href="#sec-09-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> and the Company to allowe onely two peons to accompanie it; covenanting that they shall not come within 40 course [<em>kos</em>] of Brampore, whereby our feare expressed in our last of the Princes seizure of [it] is removed, and a great deale of mony saved of what the freight or jaggatt, either upon camells or carts, would have amounted unto. . . . Your order I cannot with safety stay for, in reguard I dare not <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-76" data-page="76" data-folio="64" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n75_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 64">[p. 64]</button> lett the saltpeter ly soe long, either where it is made or in Agra, for feare the Kings officers should seize it for theire masters occasions, as they have heeretofore done; which to avoid, as any quantetie is ready, I cause it on the same oxen to bee removed to Ibramabaud,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-007" href="#sec-09-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> where it may lie more secure untill the remainder of what wee are to have of the saltpetermen bee gotten in, and then from thence it shalbee laden away altogeather.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">With regard to the war in the Deccan, Jesson supposed that more was known at Surat than at Agra.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">All that wee know is that Raja Chittersall<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-008" href="#sec-09-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> is already speeded that wayes. The King hath alsoe sent Cossum Ckawne,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-009" href="#sec-09-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> generall of artillery, and 22 other umbrawes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-010" href="#sec-09-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> against the Rajah of Serunger,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-011" href="#sec-09-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> commonly called Nuckterrannee. His Majestie is very jealous of his eldest sonn, Dorrasha, who (if reporte speaks truth) would gladly have his father removed out of this world, that hee might enjoy his throne; and some (tis fained) for that purpose imployed, miscarrying in the action, have lost theire lives for it. I wish the old King long to live, for noe doubt upon his death unspeakable troubles will arrise by the dissentions of the four princes; there being hardly one of the umbrawes that cares for the eldest prince, but hates him deadly, and hee them as much.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The references in these letters to the ambitious rivalry of Shāh Jahān’s four sons and the defection of the Nawāb Mīr Jumla from his suspicious master, the King of Golconda, have already been explained. A definite rupture had occurred between the two latter. Incensed by an act of presumption on the part of Mīr Jumla’s son, Muhammad Amīn, who was being held at Golconda as a hostage for his father’s good behaviour, the King in November, 1655, threw the young man into prison and seized his property. Thereupon the Nawāb, who had been for some time secretly in correspondence with Prince Aurangzeb, invoked the Emperor’s protection, and a letter was sent from Court to the King of Golconda, ordering the release of Muhammad Amīn. Aurangzeb, who had been longing for a pretext for conquering Golconda, now dispatched his eldest son, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-77" data-page="77" data-folio="65" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n76_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 65">[p. 65]</button> Muhammad Sultān, with an army to Hyderābād, following himself towards the end of January, 1656. The Golconda monarch had already released his prisoner and returned a humble reply to the imperial letter; but Muhammad Sultān pressed on, captured and looted Hyderābād, and blockaded the King in his fortress-palace at Golconda. On the arrival of Aurangzeb (early in February), the siege was commenced; and the operations were continued until the end of March, when, in consequence of the receipt of peremptory orders from the emperor, the Golconda monarch’s offers of submission were accepted. The latter was forced to agree to pay an indemnity, to cede certain territory, and to give his second daughter in marriage to Muhammad Sultān, to whom the succession to the throne is said to have been promised at the same time. Aurangzeb, who had been sharply censured by his father for the deception he had practised in suppressing the offers received from the King, withdrew in dudgeon to his post in the Deccan.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-012" href="#sec-09-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Mīr Jumla, who had joined the Prince’s camp before the raising of the siege, accompanied him part of the way, and then proceeded to the imperial Court, to which he had been summoned by Shāh Jahān.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At Delhi the great topic of interest was the mortal sickness of the Wazīr and the changes his death was likely to produce. Writing from Agra on 9 April, 1656, Jesson says:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Sadula Ckawne hath bin many dayes very sick, and twice even at the last gaspe, soe that a generall reporte gave him for dead; in soe much that the gomors [villagers: <em>gāonwār</em>] of severall his townes neare Agra rose in armes. But it proved to theire own distruction; for they were suddainely surprized by Abdall Nubby,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-013" href="#sec-09-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> his fouzdarr, theire townes sacked, and such as escaped not by flight either slaine or imprisoned. Wee heare hee is now reasonable well recovered, and that Christopher St. Jaquees<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-014" href="#sec-09-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> in the extremety of his sicknesse by the Kings command administred unto him the phisick that gave him ease; if soe, hee wilbee in favour both with <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-78" data-page="78" data-folio="66" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n77_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 66">[p. 66]</button> His Majestie and Sadula Ckawne, and by his meanes wee may happelie procure better writeings about the insureance businesse upon your new Governour then wee could upon the old. . . . Twice Sha Jehaun went to visitt him, and shed teares to see him (as hee supposed) past recovery, neare his end. How hee came by his sicknesse there runs severall reports. Most men will have him poisoned by the Prince; and Robert Smith<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-015" href="#sec-09-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> undertakes to sett downe the day the poison was given him, to witt, the 2d March. On Annuntidas his arriveall I may happely learne the truth.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As regards commercial matters, the letter announces the dispatch of part of the saltpetre from ‘Sadabaud’, and adds that the caravan will be joined at ‘Caggarowle’<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-016" href="#sec-09-fn-016" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-016" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> by sixty more oxen carrying the portion</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Formerly housed at Ibramabad to avoid the search of the Kings officers, who had command for the makeing 1000 maunds powder, besides the provision of a great quantety of saltpeter for the cooleing the Kings drinking water; yett Sadabaud being Sadula Ckawnes jaggeere, the Kings officers presume not as yett to molest our businesse. Soe that I hope wee may have 400 maunds more. . . . Indicoe there is little left, the greatest parte of the abundance this yeares cropp produced being bought up and sent downe by merchants to Surrat, and some by the planters themselves adventured thither, rather then they would sell at such extreame under rates. Deryabauds<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-017" href="#sec-09-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> are [cheap?], there being as yett but few buyers.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At the time when the letter was sent off, Sadullah Khān was in fact dead. The news reached Agra just as the messenger was starting, and Jesson scrawled an intimation of it on the outside of the packet. A month later (7 May) he wrote to Surat, confirming the intelligence:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">On Twesday the 8th ditto [April]<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-018" href="#sec-09-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> in the morneing, to the Kings great greife and the Princes noe lesse content, hee deceased, leaveing to bee accompt [<em>sic</em>] of men and mony in this world to goe and give an accompt in the other of his life and actions to a Judg that knowes better how to judg of them then Sha Jehan did; who (if reportes speakes not a lye) hath since his death acknowledged himselfe much <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-79" data-page="79" data-folio="67" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n78_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 67">[p. 67]</button> mistaken in his opinion of him. And though some suppose the Kings speech to have beene uttered in that particuler onely to please the Prince, in whose eares hee knew his dispraise would sound sweetly, yett seemeth it not altogeather falce; for His Majestie sheweth not noe such extraordinary favours to his young children as was by all men expected; and tis generally known hee was extreeme negligent in gathering in the Kings mutalba [<em>mutālaba</em>, ‘demand’] (which are moneyes lent out of the Kings cussanah [<em>khazāna</em>, treasury] to umbrawes when they are imployed in any warr, to bee repaid out of theire jaggeeres); which upon his death was made knowne unto him [i.e. the King] by Ra Rugnate,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-019" href="#sec-09-fn-019" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-019" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> a Hendoe dewan under Sadula Ckawne, the man that now agitates all businesse; while the cheife place is (as tis beleived) reserved for Meire Jimla, an umbraw that hath left the King of Golcundahs service to come to him; whose discontent with his former master was cause of the late warr. But if hee, on his arriveall to court, bee not judged by the King capeable to manage soe great and troublesome an office, tis then supposed the Duanshipp wilbe conferred upon Shasta Ckawne.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-020" href="#sec-09-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> In the meane time writeings of greatest consequence are signed by the King himselfe.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Funds being available from the proceeds of broadcloth sold at Delhi, Jesson was arranging to buy indigo and piece-goods, but he could not hope to dispatch these before the rains set in. The saltpetre caravan had started for Surat in the middle of April, under the charge of two house-servants and an escort of six soldiers:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Jaggatt of the way is to bee defreyed by the oxen men, all excepting one place belonging to Brampore, which is, upon the Companies accompt, reported to bee <sup>10</sup>/<sub>16</sub> rupees per oxe. . . . Had Sadula Ckawne lived but 20 dayes longer, I had recovered Mockoos<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-021" href="#sec-09-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> whole debt; whereas now there will remaine upwards of 400 rupees, besides what is owing from others, who as yett, for feare of the Kings officers, playes least in sight. Those pergannahs<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-022" href="#sec-09-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> that were Sadula Ckawnes neare Agra the King hath bestowed upon the Prince, whose fouzdar is on[e] Doudee Ckawne,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-023" href="#sec-09-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> former[ly] under Gyratt Ckawne, Sooba of Amudabad, a very courteous man to our nation; by whose assistance Mockoos remaines might <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-80" data-page="80" data-folio="68" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n79_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 68">[p. 68]</button> quickly bee gotten in, were not the raines soe neare. What saltpeter was upon the ground when news arrived of Sadula Ckawnes death, the gomors carryed away.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">With regard to his leaving Agra himself Jesson said nothing definite, but he promised to take advantage of the approaching departure of some Dutch factors to send down William Bell, the junior factor (Andrews being no longer recognized as in the Company’s service):</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Wee that stay untill dispeed of our next caphila will lessen our expences as much more as possible, keeping onely 8 or 10 peons and a water oxe. The coach shall serve to transporte William Bell, by whome such letters of the Companies as are found heere shall alsoe bee remitted you.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">With the letter was sent an account of the broadcloth sold at Delhi. This contains the names of many of the principal nobles, and mentions the delivery of cloth ‘to the Kings botade’ [household: <em>būtāt</em> is an account of household expense]. From the gross prices of such as was sold either to the king or to the prince, a deduction of about 20 per cent. appears to have been made for ‘duties of the sircar’ (see note on p. 160 of <em>English Factories</em>, 1618–21).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Jesson’s next letter (3 July) advised the postponement of Bell’s departure, and the results of visits paid to Mīr Jumla (then on his way to Delhi) and to the late Governor of Surat:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">William Bell was intended to have accompanied the Cheife of the Dutch, who, wee were informed, was in few dayes to journy to Surrat. At lenght (though later by much then was expected) arrived hither Signor Peter de Bee,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-024" href="#sec-09-fn-024" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-024" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> with an assistant and a chirurgion; and Signor John Tack, with two assistants, prepared to departe, makeing theire fairewell feast on Satturday the 21th passed, resolveing to sett out the Tewsday following. But that day, after theire things were laden upon carts, arrived two expresses from theire Comandore<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-025" href="#sec-09-fn-025" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-025" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> etc. commanding John Tacks stay untill the raines bee over, and in the meane time with all expedicion to repaire for Dilly, and there (on theire arrivall) to present to the King certaine horses which are upon the way. In complyance to <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-81" data-page="81" data-folio="69" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n80_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 69">[p. 69]</button> which theire order, hee the 30th June, accompanied with one assistant and theire broker, comenced his journy towards the court; where what hee agitates you shalbee advised heereafter. By this meanes William Bell, disapointed, is forced to stay against his will till the raines bee expired, the way of Seroy<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-026" href="#sec-09-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> being extreeme daingerous to bee travelled with soe few men as hee is to bee allowed, and the way of Brampore utterly unpassable by reason of the many rivers and nollaes.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-027" href="#sec-09-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> The 27th, being seven dayes after his arriveall to Agra, I visitted Meere Joomla, presenting him the value of 150 rupees; which every man beleived hee would not accept of, because hee received noe piscashes of any man; but wee found the contrary, for hee received ours very courteously (if it may bee soe tearmed), promiseing to doe our nation all the courtesie that lyes in his power. The day following hee proceeded for Dilly, intending to see the King the 8th July, which is (it seems) his sait.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-028" href="#sec-09-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> Hee came with very few people, his laskar<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-029" href="#sec-09-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> and treasure (which is thought to bee as great as the Kings) is behind with his sonne. When hee will arrive, it is not knowne. On the 29th June wee vissited Hoffus Nassur, cheaper then Meire Joomla, for it cost us nothing; yett had wee as much from him as the other, to witt, faire words and kinde promises of his assistance in any affaire wee should have with the King, especially in the insureance businesse. How hee will performe wee knowe not. Hee is by this time advanced halfe way to Dilly.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Bell did not quit Agra until 5 August. Jesson, in a letter of that date carried by Bell, explained that the latter had been unable to find suitable travelling companions</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Untill now that severall Banian merchants of quallity, which came up to Matra and Gocall<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-030" href="#sec-09-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> to worship, are retourning to Amahdabad, soe well attended with souldiers that it is conceived he may with them travaile safely, and therefor is with his good will dismissed, receiveing what was judged necessary for his accomodation, vizt., two oxon and the howse coach; one clock, given the factory by Mr. Knipe;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-031" href="#sec-09-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">6</a></sup> one silver spoone, poiz 3 tol[as] 2½ vals; 12 parcell citching ware, poiz 15 seers copper; one paule;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-032" href="#sec-09-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">7</a></sup> cash in species, 171½ rupees.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Ten days later, Jesson addressed a long letter to Surat, detailing his proceedings in the purchase and sale of goods, explaining why the Agra factory had not been closed, and incidentally describing <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-82" data-page="82" data-folio="70" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n81_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 70">[p. 70]</button> Mīr Jumla’s reception at Court. The following extracts may be given:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Cudioo Meerah is in provision of 3,000 pieces deryabauds at Lucknow; which hee is enordered to send away suddainely cuddied,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-033" href="#sec-09-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> which is onelie the stifning stuff washed out, whereby they become 5 or 6 seares upon a corge <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-034" href="#sec-09-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> lighter, and, if they bee wett, will not soone receive dammage. If wee can gett the remaines from Dilly (which is 7,300 rupees) time enough, soe many more shalbee provided and sent you as that sum will acquire; for as for the 50 bales indicoe I writt you I was intended to buy, the bargaine went not forward for want of mony; which had I not needed, I could have furnished you with as many deriabauds as you could have desired from Lucknow; for though they were deare and scarce heere, they might have beene procured there cheap and good, by giveing out a little money to the weavers, who, findeing bad bought up by other men at an inconsiderable difference, will make noe good; soe that what deryabauds come thence this yeare into the Jagegundy <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-035" href="#sec-09-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup> are such base trash that I never saw the like, nor can conceive they will sell in England to any proffitt; and yett are the open traders like to gett noe better, soe long as they provide theire goods, or by Bannyans heere in Agra. Mercooles there comes none of the demencions wee used to buy for the Company, but a sort of narrow slight cloth and shorte, not worth the buying; nor doe the weavers of Lucknowe or Corsett <sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-036" href="#sec-09-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">4</a></sup> make any but when wee bespeake them. . . . Meir Jimla, upon his arrivall into the presence of the King, was very courteously by him entertained and made Dewon,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-037" href="#sec-09-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">5</a></sup> haveing given him by His Majestie two ellephants, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-83" data-page="83" data-folio="71" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n82_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 71">[p. 71]</button> 202 lackeys, and five lacks of rupees for his expences; hee presenting the King with a dyamond uncutt weighing 160 ruttees, besides other precious stones of great value; which hee still continues to doe. Tis reported hee does good justice; but I feare hee will doe us none in the insureance businesse, for when hee was in Agra I spoke to him of it, and hee, as if hee had beene before informed thereof (as tis likely hee was by Hoffuz Nasser about it, and intreated him well to consider that businesse) made answer that hee thought there was little reason that the shrofs should make satisfaction, seeing the goods were lost upon our owne ships, given up by our owne people to the Dutch, with whome wee were at peace when the ship departed from Swally. Besides, Anuntidas writes that hee spake with Hoffuz Nassur about it and intreated him to move the businesse in our behalfe to Meire Jimla (new named by the King Nuwab Mayzumckawne [Muazzam Khān]); but hee replyed hee could not, for that in his opinion our demand was unjust. Soe that tis to bee feared nothing wilbee gotten but by such a course as the Dutch tooke about the robberry of theire house, when theire complaints were (as ours now are) nothing reguarded.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-038" href="#sec-09-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> . . . Thomas Andrews is very sorry to heare the Company soe much resent his mishap in comeing theire debtor, calling it an act of dishonestie. It is his cheifest care now how to gett out of it, and hopes that, with what hee expects from England this yeare and the next, to make an end thereof. . . . I am sorry to see that Agra and its factors must bee made the odium of India, and every of theire actions presented to the Company attended with such a censure as both place and persons are not thought worthy to bee continued; whenas, if it please God our masters can reobtaine theire pattent and just previledges (which I heartelie wish them), they and theire servants in Surrat will finde theire occasions cannot dispence with the want of Agra, and that theire businesse will never bee carryed on well by brokers, as it was the aime perchance of some now deprived to have it done. . . . For any thing that [has] befalne in Agra, it may with a little charity bee imputed to the misfortune of humaine affaires; and if things bee well examined, they will finde that the Company have had very ill successe in theire businesse in all places since [the] departure of President Merry. God graunt them better in the future. I perceive it is the desire and your intent to disolve this factorie. All that wee now have heere to doe is to gett in the broadcloth money and remitt it you in such goods as you desire; which being done as soone as possible I can, the books shalbee closed and sent you; which effected, wee would gladlie, according to your command, come away, but (not to hold you longer in suspence) wee are necessitated now to tell you that <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-84" data-page="84" data-folio="72" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n83_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 72">[p. 72]</button> wee cannot, as well my selfe as Mr. Andrews . . . being to deep indebted soe as to thinke it safe (considering how of late some English have departed India, heere well knowne) to desire our creditors to licence our departure for Surrat; whenas they are willing, whilest wee motion noe such thing, to awaite for theire money untill wee are able to pay them. And therefore you are humblie intreated not to impute our stay to disobeidiance. . . . There is nothing in the world which I more desire then to leave this place; but yett I would doe it with the conservation of my reputacion, which I would not have blemished by a base way of departeing, upon any feare or condicion whatsoever. Besides, it wilbee for the Companies credit and our nations that wee, that have served in such qualetie, doe come of honestly with all men; and therefore wee rather hope for your help then hinderance, seeing that neither the Company nor your selfe may receive any prejudice or trouble through our meanes, wee haveing made our creditors acquainted that the debt is our owne, and noe man any wayes ingaged to answer for a penny thereof; with which they rest well satisfied. And now, seeing wee must remaine heere, though upon our owne charge, it is my humble request that you would permitt us to live in the Companies house, untill that either (in case they desert theire trade) they inorder it to bee sould, or that (renewing it) there occasions move you to send up some other English to take possession thereof. And as for the plate and householdstuff, if you please to permitt it to bee charged to my accompt at as much as it wilbee really worth, at ballance of our books I will allowe it out of my sallarey . . . Eighteene dayes agoe departed from Agra, by the way of Brampore, one Mirza Mahmud Cossum, apointed Fousedar of Surratt.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Then came a long silence, until on 4 December Jesson wrote once more to Surat, forwarding the final accounts of the factory, and admitting himself indebted to the Company for the balance shown therein. He declared that his difficulties had been rendered acute by the action of the Surat factors in protesting his bill of exchange, as this led Vīrji Vora, his chief creditor, to press him for payment of 20,000 rupees. This sum he could only raise by borrowing a portion from a Delhi shroff on the security of the Company’s broadcloth—an expedient of which he had availed himself in the assured belief that he would, by the time the cloth was sold, have sufficient funds of his own to clear off the liability. Unhappily this expectation had not been fulfilled; but he was still confident of being able in time to repay the Company both principal <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-85" data-page="85" data-folio="73" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n84_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 73">[p. 73]</button> and interest. Meanwhile he besought the Surat factors to ‘take little notice thereof’, lest his position should be still further prejudiced. With regard to the insurance business, he was unwilling to employ at Court Ananti Dās, who had now been discharged.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But heere is in Agra on[e] Tulcidas [Tulsi Dās], a Hindoo, a man in greate favour with all the umbrawes and in especiall manner with Hooree Konnum [Hūri Khānum], Begum Sahabs nurse, who can doe in court what she pleaseth. He, if the Companies occassions had any neede of a broker, being imployed (without wages), would, I veryly beleive, be a meanes to recover that mony in a very little time and with little charge.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">As for himself and Andrews, the factory being closed,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>We must now live upon our owne charge, and finde our selves victualls as well as we cann; for wee cannott in conscience live upon the Company, unless we had imployment sufficient to deserve it, which we knowe we are not to hope for. I render you humble thanks for permitting us to have the use of the Companies house.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The sensation of the moment was the abandonment of a projected expedition against Kandahār, in favour of an attack upon Bījāpur, the king of which had recently died. This new war was instigated by Prince Aurangzeb, who was still set upon the subjugation of both Bījāpur and Golconda, and had found a pretext in the alleged right of Shāh Jahān to settle the disputed question of the succession to the throne of the former.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The King, being resolved to renue the warr against Candahor, speeded Cossom Ckawn [see p. 64] with the tobeconah [<em>top-khāna</em>, artillery] for Lahore, and with him went all the druncken English that entertained themselves at Surratt into the Kings service, being of seamen turned gunners. The Prince [Dāra] was to have followed, but he would not stirr without the King, though his father were very urgent with him. While this dispute past betwixt father and sonn, the King goeing one day abroad a hunting with a hauke upon his fist, his horse, frighted with the noise of the bells, started, and rising up threw the King to the ground. This was taken to be an ill omen, at leastwise so interpreted by those that liked not of the Candahore warr; and it wrought somuch with the King as to alter his resolution and call back Cossom Ckawn, who is returned to Dilly. And now an army is raised to goe against Decann (whose King is reported to be dead), the generall wherof is Maizum Ckawn or Mere Jimla, with whom goes 22 umbrawes and 20,000 horse, under the comand of Calleeloola Ckawn [Khalīlullah Khān].</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-86"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="86" data-folio="74" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n85_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 74">[p. 74]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Should Mīr Jumla pass through Agra (as expected), Jesson would endeavour to find out whether he was really displeased with the English, as advised by the Madras Agent, for taking a ship or town of his near Fort St. George.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Allabux [Allah Bakhsh] writes that Meire Jimla at his departure presented the King with a crower of rupees worth of pretious stones, a lack of sherafeens,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-039" href="#sec-09-fn-039" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-039" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> and 112 elephants. Methinkes it is to much to be beleived.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Reverting to the question of the insurance claim, Jesson reported that Tulsi Dās had just brought to him one of Hūri Khānum’s servants, who was bound for Surat on his mistress’s business:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The man is a very understanding bould man, and promiseth, if you will imploy him in the insureance buisnesse, he will dispute our right with the Governor against the sherroffs untill such time as Begum Sahabs neshan [<em>nishān</em>, order], which Hooree Connum hath promised to procure when Tulcidas comes to Dilly (whether he is suddainely intended), arrives; and then the sherroffs shall be forced to pay downe the mony, or he will bring them bound to court; and, rather then be soe used, I beleive they will yeild to any thing.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Nothing seems to have come of the claim for the insurance money, though its non-payment remained for some years a shadowy grievance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is now time to review the course of events at the other factories in India dependent on Surat. It had been intended to close the one at Ahmadābād as soon as the indigo was cleared from thence; but when, on 25 February, 1656, Richard Craddock, the only factor left there, attempted to start for Surat, he was prevented by the indigo makers, who insisted upon his first weighing what he had received from them:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Notwithstanding I told them that the brokers, after my departure, would have order to weigh it, yett they were not content therewith, saying that, whilest there was an Englishman heere, there was some hopes, but if I went, they then knew not what to trust to.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He was accordingly forced to write to Surat for permission to remain until the indigo was weighed and packed. As a matter of fact he did not quit Ahmadābād until the middle of June, when he was relieved by Anthony Smith, whose principal business on behalf <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-87" data-page="87" data-folio="75" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n86_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 75">[p. 75]</button> of the Company was to recover debts due from Hardeo Pāndi and others for advances made for the provision of saltpetre (Ahmadābād to Surat, 18 June). Smith was shortly to quit the Company’s service in consequence of the orders for reduction of staff, and he was now at Ahmadābād chiefly on his private business. Writing on 6 September, 1656, he said that he had done his best to make the desired recoveries, but the debtors</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Being exceeding poore, it is impossible for them to pay, either in species or comodities. I will (no other remedy being left unattempted) therefore, if you approove of it, give one-fourth parte to the Durbarr (as accustomary in such desperate debts); and then the Catwall [<em>Kotwāl</em>] will tare it out of the sides of them, or sell their howse, wife, and children to pay it. And I thinke it is far better to recover three fourths, though by this rigorous meanes, then to loose the whole debt.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Evidently the Surat factors had asked Smith to arrange for the purchase of a quantity of indigo, for he goes on to say that, although it involves his remaining until the middle or end of November, he will undertake the commission,</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>The Company beareing my charges for dyett and accomodation whilst I am here in Amah dabad; which is no more then just and reasonable. And for the threescore and two daies time that Mr. Young was here with me, I put them not to a pice charges, more then for the porter of their howse. I doubt not but the Companies coach and oxen which belonged to this factory you have safely received and in good condition, being sent per Mr. Richard Craddock. . . . The Persian horse which was in this factory I sent downe with Mr. Henry Young.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Illness prevented Smith from writing again until 22 October. He then announced that he had procured the imprisonment of Hardeo Pāndi (who was preparing to abscond) and had thus obliged him to pay half the amount due. Answering an inquiry from Surat as to the possibility of procuring saltpetre from the <em>banjāras</em> (itinerant dealers) who came to Ahmadābād from time to time, he said:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>As for our bying saltpeeter of the Bunjaries, either now or in the future, there is no hope of it; for since they have bin soe plagued by the Dewan and this Prince [i.e. Murād Bakhsh] his officers, they have quite left the trade and bring no more of that comodity.</p></blockquote></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-88"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="88" data-folio="76" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n87_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 76">[p. 76]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There were, however, some ‘Borahs’ (trader: <em>bohra</em>) who had a considerable quantity, though it was not refined to the degree usually required for England; but the price demanded was so high that he would not give it without instructions from Surat.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There is this yeare a plentifull crop of indico leafe, and it is by report very good; . . . but to this day there is not a sear made of either sort, except of the green leafe, of which herewith I send you a sample. It is very good, but not such as is usually sent home nor bought by other merchants to export, it being onely for dyers, beaters, and chinters<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-040" href="#sec-09-fn-040" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-040" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> use here. . . . It is reasonably prized, being 17½ or 18 rupees per maund. And now to tell you true, the time being soe farr spent in which the indico makers use annually to be at worke, and yett the leafe not bought, I feare this yeares indico wilbe very late, yea, too late to accomodate the Company or any other of the English occasions to be sent for Europe this yeare. The reason is, the Prince haveing a great quantety of leafe, the Dewan etc. officers would force the indico makers to take it at a very unreasonable price, or otherwise will not give them leave to buy any leafe nor to make indico. Soe that 15 daies of the principall time for makeing the comodity here and in Surqueez is now lost and nothing donn. If they make not an end of the busines and fall to worke in lesse then these 10 or 12 daies more, there will not bee any new indico here untill towards the end of December; and then to be weighed off before it be drie will loose 4 or 5 sear in a maund.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He promised, however, to be on the watch for the earliest and best parcels, should any be made in time. The letter notices the arrival of several private ships at Swally, and concludes:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>I am hartely sorry for the sad and fatall accident that happened to our new come English freinds in Swally Hole, and congratulate Agent Bornfords fortunate escape.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This incident (which is not referred to elsewhere in the records) is mentioned in two passages in Peter Mundy’s journal (now in course of publication by the Hakluyt Society). My attention was called to them by Miss Anstey, and they are now quoted by the permission of the editor, Sir Richard Temple, C.I.E.:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Aboutt this tyme [Sept. 1657] came sad tidings from India: thatt Captaine Bayly, comander of the <em>William</em> . . . with nine or ten more were drowned in Swally Hole. Coming from the shore late in the barge, [they] fell thwart of the shipps hause and were <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-89" data-page="89" data-folio="77" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n88_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 77">[p. 77]</button> unfortunately oversett and the company all drowned, excepting two or three that caughtt hold of the long boate asterne. Among the rest that perished was one Slade; whose widdow Mr. Nathaniell Wyche married, who went with him for India this last spring, hee beeing chosen President for those parts.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This summer [1658] wee had newes from East India … of the losse of sundry East India shipps, viz. the <em>William</em>, the <em>Persian Merchant</em>, the <em>Allum Frigat</em>, the <em>Freindshippe</em>, the <em>Dove</em>, and <em>Expedition</em> in the East Indies (the <em>William</em> near the Manillas in sight of her port). The men saved themselves in their boates, and arriving on shore made a little fortification and planted theron six peeces of ordnance; butt were cutt of by the inhabitants; among the rest Mr. William Burnford, supercargo or cape merchant (with whome I was well acquainted in London). Hee was one of the three that escaped drowning in Swally Hole, having then lost his hatte and cloke; yet at length both hee with ship, goods, and company came to their appointed end.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The dispatch of the <em>William</em> to Surat by a private syndicate has been mentioned on p. 49. Her supercargo, Henry (not William) Bornford, had been in the service of the Company from 1634 to 1643.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At Tatta Nicholas Scrivener remained throughout the year, though probably, in the absence of sufficient employment, he was working more on his own account than on the Company’s. A letter from him to Surat dated 21 February, 1656, refers to the sale of wheat and lead, and the purchase of cotton goods and saltpetre. Its most interesting passage is the following:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>This Bucksee [<em>Bakhshī</em>] haveing abused a cheife man of the Curmuttes<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-041" href="#sec-09-fn-041" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-041" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> or Bulchees [Baluchis], he getting free hath raised what force hee could make of his owne men and turned rebell, and to his aid hath caled anothers compainy of Blochees from about Corah Bollah;<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-042" href="#sec-09-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> soe both togeather make a considerable army. They have not left a towne betweene Bunder [i.e. Lahrībandar] and this place unplundered, carrying cattle, people, and all away; and yesterday they robbed a towne on this side the river. And yett I see noe order taken to withstand them; soe that, if they doe noe more mischeife yett, it is there owne fault.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Scrivener’s next letter (3 March) mentions that the Dutch merchants have finished their investments and are expecting a ship.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-90"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="90" data-folio="78" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/englishfactories10fost/page/n89_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 78">[p. 78]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">He acknowledges the receipt of a sum of money, but considers it insufficient to provide the piece-goods desired, for the purchase of which he proposes to go to Nasarpur and Kandiāro, remaining at one or other of these places until the end of September. About five weeks later (8 April) he writes again, announcing that he is about to depart for Nasarpur, and adding:</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>Heere is lately t[w]o Portugall vessells arrived from Cocheene and Goa, whose people reporte that the Portugalls have well beaten the Dutch; but how true this news is I am not certaine. This day is news come that Cossee Abraham [Kāzi Ibrāhīm], who comes Ameine of this place for the Prince,<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-09-fn-043" href="#sec-09-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-09-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> is arrived at Nasserpore; but as yett noe news of the new Governour.</p></blockquote></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A further letter (24 April) to Surat shows how traders were inconvenienced by the arbitrary actions of the Mogul officials.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><blockquote><p>You may peradventure at receipt of these admire to finde mee still in Tuttah; but … Jeffar Ckawne [Jafar Khān] being displaced and goeing for Lahore, hath seased on all the boates hee could light on for his accomidacion, not leaveing one behinde him fitt for service; soe that my stay thus long heere hath beene much against my will.
