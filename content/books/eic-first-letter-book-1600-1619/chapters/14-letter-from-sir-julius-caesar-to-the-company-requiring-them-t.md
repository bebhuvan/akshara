Theis are therefore to praie &amp; requier you to make staie of the said attached goode in yo<sup>r</sup> hande vntill such time as the cause shall be examined by me accordinge to his ma<sup>ties</sup> Comaundm<sup>t</sup> or that you shall receiue further order from me touchinge the same Whereof requireing you not to faile I hartilie bid you ffarewell from the Courte of Wilton this 27<sup>th</sup> of October 1603.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">yo<sup>r</sup> verie loueing ffreind</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Julius Cæsar.</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-14-fn-001" href="#sec-14-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-14-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup></p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-14-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-14-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">2.</a> <span class="fn-body">Mr., afterwards Sir, Julius Cæsar, the son of a doctor of Italian extraction, who had settled in London, and had risen to notice as a Court physician, was appointed in 1583 judge of the Admiralty Court. Five years later he was admitted master of the chancery in ordinary, and in 1591 Elizabeth conferred on him the post of master <span class="folio-turn" id="page-118">[p. 26]</span> of requests. He was knighted by James I. on the 20th May, 1603. In 1606 he was made chancellor and under-treasurer of the exchequer, and in the following year was sworn of the Privy Council. Seven years later, he succeeded to the post of master of the rolls, for which he had long been waiting; and the rest of his life is one unbroken record of active state employment. He died 18th April, 1636, and was buried in the church of Great St. Helen's, Bishopsgate, where his monument may yet be seen. His legal knowledge appears to have been hardly adequate to the important posts he was called upon to fill, and this point did not escape the notice of his enemies; but even they were forced to admit the excellence of his private character. His charity was unbounded, and during his early career his generosity to impecunious suitors in his court was carried to an extent that threatened seriously to embarrass his own resources. He was Cecil's right-hand man at a time of great difficulty in regard to the national finances, and his whole career seems to have been that of a useful public servant.</span></li></ol></section>
