<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-195"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="195" data-folio="157" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n194_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 157">[p. 157]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">CHAPTER IV</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><span class="sc">botany</span><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-001" href="#sec-07-fn-001" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-001" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Introductory.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> term British India as employed in this chapter embraces, over and above the vast territory controlled by the Government of India, some independent countries, of which Nepāl and the Himālayas east of Sikkim are the chief, together with Ceylon and the Malayan Peninsula, which are in great part under the Colonial Office.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The geographical and climatal features of India, upon which the distribution of its Flora so much depends, can be here introduced only incidentally. They will be found to be fully discussed in chap. i of this volume<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-002" href="#sec-07-fn-002" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-002" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The term Flora applies in this sketch to native Flowering plants, Ferns and their allies. Collected materials do not exist for discussing the distribution of Mosses, Hepaticae, Lichens, and Fungi, which abound in most parts of India, or of the Algae in its seas and fresh waters. On the other hand, such extensive herbaria of the higher Orders of plants have, during the last century especially, been made over most parts of British India, that the study of their contents may be assumed to provide sufficient materials for a review of its Flora.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Flora of British India is more varied than that of any other country of equal area in the eastern hemisphere, if not in the globe. This is due to its geographical extension, embracing so many degrees of latitude, temperate and tropical ; to its surface, rising from the level of the sea to heights above the limits of vegetation ; to its climates, varying from torrid to arctic, and from almost absolute aridity to a maximum of humidity ; and to the immigration of plants from widely different bordering countries, notably of Chinese and Malayan <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-196" data-page="196" data-folio="158" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n195_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 158">[p. 158]</button> on the east and south, of Oriental<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-003" href="#sec-07-fn-003" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-003" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, European, and African on the west, and of Tibetan and Siberian on the north. Whether India is richer in number of genera and species than any other area on the globe of equal dimensions is doubtful; it is certainly far poorer in endemic genera and species than many others, especially China, Australia, and South Africa.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of the elements of the Indian Flora the Malayan is the dominant; but until the Floras of Sumatra, Tongking, and South China are better known, it is not possible to estimate its comparative strength. The Oriental and European elements can be approximately estimated. About 570 European genera and 760 species are indigenous in India, of which about 430 genera and over 400 species are British; and if the Oriental genera and species be added to the European, these figures would probably be doubled. The African element, which includes the Arabian, is third in amount, and it will no doubt be augmented as the Flora of Equatorial Africa becomes better known. The Tibetan and Siberian elements, which include an Arctic, are all but confined to the alpine regions of the Himālayas. Lastly, the Chinese and Japanese Floras are strongly represented throughout the temperate Himālayas and in Burma.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of the Natural Orders of Flowering plants, Ferns and their allies comprised in the Flora of British India, not one is peculiar to it; and if the genera common to it and to one or more of the adjacent countries be excluded, few endemic genera remain, and such of them as are endemic are local, and with few exceptions are restricted to one or few species<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-004" href="#sec-07-fn-004" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-004" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>. It may hence be affirmed that in a large sense there is no Indian Flora proper<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-005" href="#sec-07-fn-005" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-005" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-197"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="197" data-folio="159" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n196_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 159">[p. 159]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The British Indian Flora, though so various as to its elements, presents few anomalies in a phytogeographic point of view. The most remarkable instances of such anomalies are the presence in it of one or a few species of what are very large and all but endemic genera in Australia: namely, <em>Baeckea</em>, <em>Leptospermum</em>, <em>Melaleuca</em>, <em>Leucopogon</em>, <em>Stylidium</em>, <em>Helicia</em>, and <em>Casuarina</em>. Others are <em>Oxybaphus himalaicus</em>, the solitary extra-American species of the genus; <em>Pyrularia edulis</em>, the only congeners of which are a Javan and a North American; and <em>Vogelia</em>, which is limited to three species, an Indian, South African, and Sokotran. Of absentee Natural Orders of the Old World, the most notable are <em>Myoporineae</em>, which, though mainly Australian, has Chinese, Japanese, and Mascarene species; <em>Empetraceae</em>, one species of which girdles the globe in the north temperate hemisphere and reappears in Chili (the rarity of bog-land in the Himālayas must be the cause of its absence); and <em>Cistineae</em>, an Order containing upwards of 100 European and Oriental species, of which one only (a Persian) reaches Native Baluchistān. The absence of any indigenous Lime (<em>Tilia</em>) or Beech (<em>Fagus</em>) or Chestnut (<em>Castanea</em>) in the temperate Himālayas is remarkable, all three being European, Oriental, and Japanese genera. The Chestnut, which has been introduced into NW. India from Europe, ripens its fruit in the Western Himālayas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">With the exception of the Rhododendron belt in the high Eastern Himālayas, there are in India few assemblages of species of peculiar or conspicuous plants giving a character to the landscape over wide areas, as do the Heaths in Britain, the Heaths and succulent plants in South Africa, the <em>Eucalypti</em>, <em>Epacrideae</em>, and <em>Proteaceae</em> in Australia, the <em>Cacti</em> in America, or the great Aloes and Euphorbias in East Tropical and South Africa; nor are there representatives of the Pampas, Catingas, Savannahs, or Prairie vegetation of America. The Coniferous forests of the Himālayas resemble those of other northern countries, and the great Teak forests have no peculiar features<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-006" href="#sec-07-fn-006" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-006" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. The wood-oil trees (<em>Dipterocarpi</em>) in Burma form an exception, towering over the forests of Arakan and Tenasserim. Of gregarious trees, some of the most conspicuous are the Sāl (<em>Shorea robusta</em>), <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-198" data-page="198" data-folio="160" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n197_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 160">[p. 160]</button> Eng or In (<em>Dipterocarpus tuberculatus</em>), Sissū (<em>Dalbergia Sissoo</em>), Khair (<em>Acacia Catechu</em>), and Babūl (<em>A. arabica</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Indigenous Palms are few compared with many regions in tropical America, and are comparatively unobtrusive. The Talipot Palms (species of <em>Corypha</em>) are the most majestic palms in India, in stature, foliage, and inflorescence, but they are exceedingly rare and local. The Indian Date (<em>Phœnix sylvestris</em>), the Fan-Palm or Palmyra (<em>Borassus flabellifer</em>), and the Coco-nut near the sea, are the only palms that may be said to be conspicuous in the landscape of the plains of India. On the other hand, graceful erect or climbing Palms with pinnate or fan-shaped leaves frequent the humid evergreen forests, where the Rattans (<em>Calami</em>) ascend the trees by their hooked spines and expose their feathery crowns to the light. Bamboos, of which there are more than 120 kinds in India, form, as elsewhere in the tropics, an important feature, whether as clumps growing in the open, or forming in association all but impenetrable jungles; the taller kinds monopolize large areas in the hot lower regions, and the smaller clothe mountain slopes up to 10,000 feet in the Himālayas. Tree-ferns, of which there are about twenty (?) species, frequent the deepest forests of the Eastern Himālayas, Central India and Vizagapatam, Burma, Malabar, the Malay Peninsula, and Ceylon.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of shrubs that form a feature in the landscape from their gregarious habits, the most conspicuous examples are the Rhododendrons of the temperate regions of the Himālayas, and the genus <em>Strobilanthes</em> in the western hills of the Peninsula. Many species of the latter genus do not flower till they have arrived at a certain period of growth, and then, after simultaneously flowering, seed profusely and die. Some Bamboos, also gregarious, display the same habit, which they retain under cultivation in Europe. Three local all but stemless Palms are eminently gregarious: <em>Phœnix farinifera</em> of the Coromandel coast, <em>Nannorrhops Ritchieana</em> of extreme Western India, and <em>Phœnix paludosa</em> and <em>Nipa fruticans</em> of the Sundarbans. Among herbaceous plants the beautiful genus <em>Impatiens</em> takes the first place, from abounding in all humid districts except the Malay Peninsula, and from its numerous species being (with hardly an exception) endemic; added to which is the fact that, though profuse in individuals, the species are remarkably local, those of the Eastern Himālayas differing from those of the Western, these again from the Burmese, and all from those of the Eastern and Western Peninsulas and Ceylon; and most of these two last from one another.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-199"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="199" data-folio="161" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n198_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 161">[p. 161]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of fresh-water flowering plants, floating or wholly or partially submerged, there are many kinds in India. They include the beautiful white, red, and blue <em>Nymphaeas</em>, <em>Nelumbium speciosum</em>, and <em>Euryale ferox</em>, the latter a near ally of the <em>Victoria Regina</em> of South American waters; also many carnivorous Bladderworts (<em>Utricularia</em>), and the curious <em>Aldrovanda</em> with leaves like those of the Venus Fly-trap, a South European plant, hitherto found nowhere in India except in some saline ponds near Calcutta. The most remarkable among the Indian fresh-water plants are the Podostemonads, which clothe rocks and stones in rapid streams with submerged spreading fronds, resembling green Lichens more than flowering plants. They are most common in Malabar and Ceylon, and are never found in rivers that have glacial sources. Marine flowering plants are few indeed, and are mostly of wide Oceanic distribution. Of peculiar littoral sand-hill plants there are few, the most notable being the above-mentioned <em>Phoenix farinifera</em>, <em>Ipomoea biloba</em>, <em>Vigna lutea</em>, <em>Canavalia lineata</em>, <em>Launaea pinnatifida</em>, and a curious grass, <em>Spinifex</em>, of Australian affinity. The estuarial plants will be enumerated when describing the tidal flora of the Sundarbans.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The number of recorded species of Flowering plants in India approaches 17,000<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-007" href="#sec-07-fn-007" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-007" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, under 174 Natural Orders<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-008" href="#sec-07-fn-008" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-008" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>; and there are probably 600 species of Ferns and their allies.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The largest Order of Flowering plants in all India is <em>Orchideae</em>, of which more than 1,600 species are recorded and additions are constantly being discovered. The greater number of these are tropical and epiphytic, and with comparatively few exceptions all are endemic. Ten are European, and they are British<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-009" href="#sec-07-fn-009" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-009" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>. It is only in the Eastern Himālayas, Burma, and the Malay Peninsula that the Order predominates; in other parts of India <em>Leguminosae</em>, <em>Gramineae</em>, and <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> outnumber them.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-200"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="200" data-folio="162" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n199_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 162">[p. 162]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The ten dominant Orders of Flowering plants in all British India are in numerical sequence :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1. <em>Orchideae.</em> 6. <em>Acanthaceae.</em> 2. <em>Leguminosae.</em> 7. <em>Compositae.</em> 3. <em>Gramineae.</em> 8. <em>Cyperaceae.</em> 4. <em>Rubiaceae.</em> 9. <em>Labiatae.</em> 5. <em>Euphorbiaceae.</em> 10. <em>Urticaceae.</em></p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of these all but <em>Labiatae</em> and <em>Compositae</em> are more tropical than temperate. <em>Compositae</em> take a very low place, and would, but for the temperate and alpine Himālayan species, take a very much lower. In this respect India shares, with the whole Malayan Archipelago, an exceptional poverty in what is not only the largest of all the Orders of Flowering plants in the world, but the one that heads the list in most other parts of the globe.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The following data<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-010" href="#sec-07-fn-010" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-010" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, deduced from the whole Indian Flora, are of use for comparison with those of its several botanical areas. The proportion of Monocotyledons to Dicotyledons is approximately as 1 to 2·3; of genera to species as about 1 to 7. Of Palms there are more than 230 recorded species ; of Bamboos, 120 ; of Conifers, only twenty-two ; of <em>Cycadeae</em>, five. Of genera with 100 or more species there are ten, of which four are Orchids, headed by 200 of <em>Dendrobium</em>; the others are <em>Impatiens</em> (which will probably prove to be the largest genus of Flowering plants in India), <em>Eugenia</em>, <em>Pedicularis</em>, <em>Strobilanthes</em>, <em>Ficus</em>, <em>Bulbophyllum</em>, <em>Eria</em>, <em>Habenaria</em>, and <em>Carex</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Botanical Regions of British India.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">British India is primarily divisible into three botanical areas : a Himālayan, an Eastern, and a Western. The two latter are roughly limited by a line drawn meridionally from the Himālayas to the Bay of Bengal. The prominent characters of the three are—that the Himālayan area presents a rich tropical, temperate, and alpine Flora, with forests of Conifers, many Oaks, and a profusion of Orchids ; that the Eastern has no alpine Flora, a very restricted temperate one, few Conifers, many Oaks and Palms, and a great preponderance of Orchids ; that the Western has only one (very local) Conifer, no Oaks, few Palms, and comparatively few Orchids. Further, the Himālayan Flora abounds in European and Siberian genera ; the Eastern in Chinese and Malayan ; the Western in European, Oriental, and African. These three botanical areas are divisible into nine botanical Regions, for the determination of which I have, after long deliberation, resorted to the number of species of the ten <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-201" data-page="201" data-folio="163" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n200_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 163">[p. 163]</button> largest Natural Orders in each Region as the leading exponent of their botanical differences. The nine Regions are :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. The Eastern Himālayas, extending from Sikkim to the Mishmi mountains in Upper Assam.</li><li>2. The Western Himālayas<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-011" href="#sec-07-fn-011" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-011" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, extending from Kumaun to Chitrāl.</li><li>3. The Indus Plain, including the Punjab, Sind, and Rājputāna west of the Arāvalli range and Jumna river, Cutch, and Northern Gujarāt.</li><li>4. The Gangetic Plain, from the Arāvalli hills and Jumna river to Bengal, the Sundarbans, the plains of Assam and Sylhet, and the low country of Orissa north of the Mahānadī river. This Region is divisible into three Sub-regions : an upper dry, a lower humid, and the Sundarbans.</li><li>5. Malabar in a very extended sense—the humid belt of hilly or mountainous country extending along the western side of the Peninsula from Southern Gujarāt to Cape Comorin. It includes Southern Gujarāt, the Southern half of Kāthiāwār, the Konkan, Kanara, Malabar proper, Cochin, Travancore, and the Laccadive Islands.</li><li>6. The Deccan in a very broad sense : that is, the whole comparatively dry elevated table-land of the Peninsula east of Malabar and south of the Gangetic and Indus Plains, together with, as a Sub-region, the low-lying strip of coast land extending from Orissa to Tinnevelly, known as the Coromandel coast.</li><li>7. Ceylon and the Maldive Islands.</li><li>8. Burma, bounded on the N. and NE. by the flanking mountains to the south of the Assam valley and China, on the east by China and Siam, on the west by Bengal and the Indian Ocean, and on the south by the State of Khedah in the Malay Peninsula. The Andaman Islands, and possibly the Nicobar, belong to the Burmese Region.</li><li>9. The Malay Peninsula, from Khedah to Singapore, including the British protected States in this Peninsula. The British Provinces proper are Wellesley, the Island of Penang, Malacca, and Singapore. The Nicobar Islands may belong to this Region.</li></ul></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-202"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="202" data-folio="164" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n201_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 164">[p. 164]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A glance at the map of India shows that, in the attempt to delimit these botanical Regions geographically, large areas are in some cases difficult to apportion : as, for example, Kāthiāwār, of which the NW. half is probably referable botanically to Sind, the SE. to the Konkan. It is not possible to draw a bounding line between the Flora of the Indus and of the Gangetic Plains : that is, between the Flora of the affluents (in the plains) of the Sutlej and of the Jumna rivers. Extensive tracts of land with characteristic Upper Gangetic plants intrude into the Indus Plain, and desert areas of Rājputāna intrude into the Gangetic Plain. The eastern limit of the Malabar Region is undefinable, because of the number of spurs and valleys from its hills which project far into the Deccan Region, sometimes almost crossing it, carrying with them types of the Malabar Flora, which towards its northern limit mingles with those of the Deccan and of the Indus and Gangetic Plains. The Flora of the trans-Indus mountains bounding the Indus Plain Region on the west, of which the eastern flanks are British Indian, is known botanically in one valley only, the Kurram. To have referred this either to the Western Himālayan Region, or to Afghānistān, would be premature. It is therefore treated of in an Appendix (A) to this Sketch; as is also the Flora of British Baluchistān (Appendix B), which differs considerably from that of any other botanical Region of India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These Regions coincide roughly with the areas of comparative humidity or dryness, indicated by Major Prain in his <em>Plants of Bengal</em> (Introduction, p. 2) as follows :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">India Deserta ; Sind, Rājputāna, and the Punjab. (The Indus Plain Region.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">India Diluvia ; with its chief development in the Gangetic Plain, comprising much of the territory that constitutes politically the United Provinces and Bengal. (The Gangetic Plain Region.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">India Aquosa ; the wet forest tracts along the Western Ghāts from Gujarāt to Travancore, which receive all the force of the SW. monsoon. (The Malabar Region.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">India Vera ; the dry but not desert triangle between the Western and the Eastern Ghāts of the Peninsula, with its apex in Tinnevelly, and its base skirting the Gangetic Plain. (The Deccan Region.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">India Subaquosa ; the Eastern Ghāts and the strip between them and the sea. (The Coromandel Sub-region.) <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-203" data-page="203" data-folio="165" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n202_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 165">[p. 165]</button> India Littorea; most highly developed in the Gangetic delta. (The Sundarbans Sub-region.)</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">They also approximate to the botanical Sub-areas of British India drawn up by Mr. C. B. Clarke in his instructive Essay in the <em>Journal of the Linnean Society</em> (vol. xxxiv (1898), p. 142), with an excellent map. The principal differences between his Sub-areas and my Regions lie in his inclusion of Central Nepāl in the Eastern Himālayan Region, and of the Afghān E. boundary mountains, all Baluchistān, SE. Rājputāna, and Central India in the Indus Plain Region; in his treatment of N. and NE. Burma with the Assam Valley as a separate Sub-area (Assam), and of Eastern and Southern Burma as another (Pegu); and in his inclusion of all Ceylon in the Deccan Region.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Flora of British India has been described at much greater length than in this Sketch in the Introductory Essay to Dr. Thomson's and my <em>Flora Indica</em> (see footnote, p. 157). In that work three primary divisions are recognized<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-012" href="#sec-07-fn-012" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-012" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>: namely, I. Hindustān, including the Western Peninsula from the base of the Himālaya to Cape Comorin; II. The Himālayas; III. India beyond the Ganges. These primary divisions are subdivided into sixty-four Provinces, the botanical characters of which, as far as they were then known, were delimited in relation to their climate, geographical position, elevation above the sea, and other physical conditions; to which are added references to many of the botanists who had explored them, their collections and their works. These sixty-four Provinces will, I believe, all prove to be deserving of detailed botanical treatment when sufficient materials shall have been obtained to effect this. The following is a list of them, arranged under the Regions adopted in this Sketch:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. Eastern Himālayas: Mishmi, Abor, Bhutān, Sikkim, Central Nepāl.</li><li>2. Western Himālayas, under three groups: I. Kumaun, Garhwāl, Simla, Kulū, Chamba, Jammu, Rajaori; II. Kanāwar, Lāhul, Kishtwar, Kashmīr, Murree; III. Gugi, Piti and Parang, Zāskār, Dras, Nari, Ladākh, Bālti, Nubra.</li><li>3. Indus Plain, which includes the Punjab, Sind, Cutch, Northern Gujarāt, and Rājputāna west of the Arāvalli Hills.</li><li>4. Gangetic Plain, under two groups: I. Upper, including Rājputāna east of the Arāvalli Hills, Bundelkhand, and Mālwā <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-204" data-page="204" data-folio="166" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n203_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 166">[p. 166]</button> north of the Vindhya range; II. Lower, including Bengal south of the Himālayas, Orissa north of the Mahānadī, the Assam, Sylhet, Cāchār, and Tippera plains.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">5. Malabar, including Khāndesh, the Konkan, Kanara, Coorg, Malabar proper, Travancore.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">6. The Deccan, including Mālwā, Bihār, Berār, Central India, the Central Provinces, Chotā Nāgpur, Orissa south of the Mahānadī, the Deccan proper, Mysore, and the Coromandel coast.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">7. Ceylon.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">8. Burma, including the Assam, Gāro, Pātkai, Nāgā, Khāsi, Manipur, Cāchār, and Sylhet Hills, Chittagong, Tippera, Arakan, Pegu, and Tenasserim, together with the Shan and other States bordering China and Siam.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">9. The Malayan Peninsula, including the British and Siamese States therein.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Eastern Himālayan Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The only botanically well-known portion of the first Region is its western end: Sikkim, an oblong section of the Himālayas about 100 miles long from north to south, and forty from east to west. An analysis of its Flora may be presumed to give an adequate idea of the general features of the unexplored Himālayas to the eastward of it. This is indeed proved by such materials as have been procured from the latter<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-013" href="#sec-07-fn-013" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-013" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A comparison of its vegetation with that of the Western Himālayas will be found under that Region.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sikkim is the most humid district in the whole range of the Himālayas, because of its proximity to the Bay of Bengal and direct exposure to the effects of the moisture-laden south-west monsoon, from which the ranges east of Sikkim are partially screened by the mountains on the south flank of the Assam valley. It is estimated to contain about 4,000 species of Flowering plants under 160 Natural Orders; also 250 Ferns <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-205" data-page="205" data-folio="167" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n204_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 167">[p. 167]</button> and their allies, of which eight are Tree-ferns. The ten dominant Orders are the following, to which are appended, in brackets, their relative places in the Western Himālayan decad:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Orchideae</em> (7).</li><li>6. <em>Urticaceae</em>.</li><li>2. <em>Gramineae</em> (1).</li><li>7. <em>Scrophularineae</em> (10).</li><li>3. <em>Leguminosae</em> (3).</li><li>8. <em>Rosaceae</em> (8).</li><li>4. <em>Compositae</em> (2).</li><li>9. <em>Rubiaceae</em>.</li><li>5. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (5).</li><li>10. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The proportion of Monocotyledons to Dicotyledons in Sikkim is about 1 to 2·5. Its Flora is disposed in three altitudinal zones: tropical, temperate, and alpine. It is difficult to limit, even approximately, the elevation of these zones above the sea, many tropical species ascending far into the temperate, and temperate species descending into the tropical. So too with the alpine zone, many of its species descend far into the temperate, and temperate species ascend far into the alpine. Assuming the normal tropical Flora to ascend from the level of the plains of India to 6,500 feet, and the normal alpine to descend from 18,000 to 12,000 feet, the number of species normal to each zone may possibly be found to approximate to 2,000, 1,500, and 500. The total number of recorded species of Orchids in Sikkim is 440; of Palms, twenty; of <em>Bambusae</em>, about twenty-three; and of Ferns and their allies, 280, of which eight are Tree-ferns.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tropical zone of Sikkim.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The tropical zone of Sikkim is skirted at its base by a low belt about 20 miles broad, which gradually slopes upwards from the level of the plains of Bengal to the foothills of the zone at 1,000 feet elevation, from which the ascent is rapid to 6,500 feet and upwards. This belt was, when Sikkim was first botanically explored (in 1848), a deadly, unhealthy tarai<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-014" href="#sec-07-fn-014" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-014" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, covered with a loose forest (now for the most part cleared away) of trees common in the hotter parts of India, especially the Sāl (<em>Shorea robusta</em>), together with a rich undergrowth of shrubs, coarse grasses, and the herbaceous plants of the Gangetic Plain. Among them a few species of the temperate zone occur, brought down by the streams from higher levels. The foothills and spurs of the tropical zone are (or were before the introduction of tea cultivation) clothed throughout with a dense <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-206" data-page="206" data-folio="168" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n205_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 168">[p. 168]</button> forest, Malayan in general character. The ten dominant Orders of the tropical zone are, in numerical sequence :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Orchideae.</em></li><li>6. <em>Cyperaceae.</em></li><li>2. <em>Leguminosae.</em></li><li>7. <em>Rubiaceae.</em></li><li>3. <em>Gramineae.</em></li><li>8. <em>Compositae.</em></li><li>4. <em>Urticaceae.</em></li><li>9. <em>Asclepiadeae.</em></li><li>5. <em>Euphorbiaceae.</em></li><li>10. <em>Acanthaceae.</em></li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are in this zone about 850 trees and shrubs, many of them timber trees, among the most conspicuous of which are (beside the Sāl), species of <em>Magnoliaceae</em>, <em>Anonaceae</em>, <em>Guttiferae</em>, <em>Sterculiaceae</em>, <em>Tiliaceae</em>, <em>Meliaceae</em>, <em>Sapindaceae</em>, <em>Anacardiaceae</em>, <em>Leguminosae</em>, <em>Combretaceae</em>, <em>Myrtaceae</em>, <em>Lythraceae</em>, <em>Rubiaceae</em>, <em>Bignoniaceae</em>, <em>Laurineae</em>, <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>, <em>Urticaceae</em>, and <em>Myristicaceae</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">There are four species of Oak, as many of <em>Castanopsis</em> (a genus allied to <em>Castanea</em>), <em>Luculia gratissima</em>, the two paper-yielding shrubs, <em>Daphne cannabina</em> and <em>Edgeworthia Gardneri</em>, a Poplar, a Willow (<em>Salix tetrasperma</em>), one Pine, in the inner valleys only (<em>P. longifolia</em>), a <em>Cycas</em> and two species of <em>Musa</em>, eighteen indigenous Palms, a dwarf and a tall <em>Pandanus</em>, and twelve arborescent or frutescent Bamboos. Ferns abound. Of shrubs, <em>Acanthaceae</em> and <em>Melastomaceae</em>, together with others of the above arboreous Orders, are among the most frequent. Of climbers, there are many species of <em>Ampelideae</em>, <em>Cucurbitaceae</em>, <em>Convolvulaceae</em>, <em>Apocyneae</em>, <em>Asclepiadeae</em>, <em>Smilax</em>, <em>Dioscorea</em>, and <em>Aroideae</em>. Herbaceous plants are well represented by <em>Malvaceae</em>, Balsams, Orchids, and <em>Scitamineae</em>, together with many species of other ubiquitous tropical Indian Orders.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Temperate zone of Sikkim.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The temperate zone of Sikkim, from 6,500 to 11,500 feet, is roughly divisible into a lower non-coniferous and an upper coniferous and Rhododendron belt; but the line of demarcation between these varies so greatly with the exposure and humidity of the locality that they cannot be dealt with apart. Of about 100 Natural Orders of Flowering plants that occur in this zone the following ten dominate, the figure in brackets after each Order denoting its relative position in the tropical zone :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Orchideae</em> (1).</li><li>6. <em>Geraniaceae.</em></li><li>2. <em>Compositae</em> (8).</li><li>7. <em>Ericaceae.</em></li><li>3. <em>Gramineae</em> (3).</li><li>8. <em>Liliaceae.</em></li><li>4. <em>Rosaceae.</em></li><li>9. <em>Labiatae.</em></li><li>5. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (6).</li><li>10. <em>Umbelliferae.</em></li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of the above Orders the <em>Orchideae</em> alone are strongly <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-207" data-page="207" data-folio="169" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n206_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 169">[p. 169]</button> Malayan in character, the others are mostly European, Central Asian, Japanese, or Chinese. The most conspicuous trees are <em>Magnoliaceae</em> (five species), of which one (<em>Magnolia Campbellii</em>), before the working of the forest began, clothed the slopes around Darjeeling, starring them in spring, when still leafless, with its magnificent flowers. Other conspicuous trees of this region are Oaks, Laurels, Maples, Birches, Alder, <em>Bucklandia</em>, <em>Pyrus</em>, and Conifers. Of these the Conifers are chiefly confined to a belt from 9,000 to 12,000 feet in elevation. The monarch and most common of them is a Silver Fir (<em>Abies Webbiana</em>), which is also the most gregarious; others are the English Yew, a Spruce (<em>Picea Morinda</em>), a Larch (<em>Larix Griffithii</em>, the only deciduous Conifer in the Himālayas), the weeping <em>Tsuga Brunoniana</em>, and two species of Juniper, both of which, in dwarf forms, ascend high into the alpine zone. The absence of any true Pine or Cypress in the forests of this region of the Himālayas is notable, in contrast with similar elevations in the Western Himālayas. Of shrubs, the most conspicuous are the Rhododendrons (twenty-five species), which abound between 9,000 and 12,000 feet elevation, some of them forming impenetrable thickets; a few of these are arboreous, though never attaining any considerable height. Other shrubs are species of <em>Clematis</em>, <em>Ternstroemiaceae</em>, <em>Berberideae</em>, <em>Ilex</em>, <em>Rosa</em>, <em>Rubus</em>, <em>Cotoneaster</em>, <em>Spiraea</em>, <em>Hydrangea</em>, <em>Aucuba</em>, <em>Lonicera</em>, <em>Leycesteria</em>, <em>Osmanthus</em>, <em>Osbeckia</em>, <em>Buddleia</em>, <em>Vacciniaceae</em> (some epiphytic), <em>Ericaceae</em>, Elder, <em>Viburnum</em>, <em>Polygonum</em>, Ivy, &amp;c. Beautiful herbaceous plants abound—Anemones, Aconites, Violets, many species of Balsam, <em>Potentilla</em>, <em>Fragaria</em>, <em>Gentianeae</em>, <em>Campanulaceae</em>, <em>Gesneraceae</em>, <em>Scrophularineae</em>, <em>Orchideae</em> (<em>Coelogyne</em>, eight species), <em>Cypripedium</em>, <em>Polygonatum</em>, <em>Smilacina</em>, <em>Lilium</em>, <em>Fritillaria</em>, <em>Arisaema</em>. Only two Palms inhabit this zone, a scandent Rattan (<em>Plectocomia himalaica</em>), and very rarely a Fan-Palm (<em>Trachycarpus Martiana</em>). Dwarf Bamboos, of which there are six species, abound, some of them forming impervious thickets infested with leeches and large ticks. Ferns are characteristic of this zone.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Alpine zone of Sikkim.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The alpine zone of Sikkim descends to about 12,000 feet from the upper limit of the existence of Flowering plants. It presents two climates with conforming differences in their vegetation: a lower or outer humid, and an upper or inner dry Tibetan region. But the limits of these climates are not clearly definable either topographically or botanically; for whereas in some cases the passes between Sikkim and Tibet are abrupt and lofty, in others the valleys expand widely and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-208" data-page="208" data-folio="170" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n207_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 170">[p. 170]</button> become gradually Tibetan in climate and features, the pass proper being a political boundary. Further, some valleys run up from south to north, others from west to east. The number of species of Flowering plants recorded for this zone is about 380, no doubt far below what future collectors will raise it to. They are included under forty-six Orders, of which the ten dominant are as follows, their corresponding position in the Western Himālayan decad being given in brackets :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Compositae</em> (1).</li><li>2. <em>Scrophularineae</em>.</li><li>3. <em>Primulaceae</em>.</li><li>4. <em>Saxifragaceae</em> (9).</li><li>5. <em>Cruciferae</em> (5).</li><li>6. <em>Caryophylleae</em> (8).</li><li>7. <em>Ranunculaceae</em> (6).</li><li>8. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (4).</li><li>9. <em>Gramineae</em> (2).</li><li>10. <em>Fumariaceae</em> (10).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of the above, the first three greatly outnumber the others, some of which may have to give place to <em>Rosaceae</em>, <em>Gentianaceae</em>, or <em>Umbelliferae</em>. The largest genera are <em>Pedicularis</em>, <em>Primula</em>, <em>Corydalis</em>, and <em>Saxifraga</em>. The low position of <em>Cyperaceae</em> and <em>Gramineae</em> in the decad is notable, remarkably so in contrast to the Western Himālayan decad; but future herborizations may bring them up higher. The few trees, to be found only on the lower skirts of this zone, are scattered Birches and <em>Pyri</em>. The principal bushes are Rhododendrons (of which several species attain 14,000 feet elevation, and three dwarf ones 16,000 feet), two Junipers, species of <em>Ephedra</em>, <em>Berberis</em>, <em>Lonicera</em>, <em>Caragana</em>, <em>Rosa</em>, <em>Cotoneaster</em>, <em>Spiraea</em>, and dwarf Willows. Of Ferns there are very few. About thirty species reach 18,000 feet elevation, some of them a little higher. The highest recorded Flowering plant is a <em>Festuca</em> (not found in flower) at about 18,300 feet. In drier valleys above 15,000 feet elevation several species of <em>Arenaria</em> occur, which form hard hemispheric or globose white balls and are a characteristic feature in the desolate landscape. By far the most striking plants of this zone are the species of <em>Meconopsis</em>, <em>Rheum nobile</em>, the Edelweiss, many Primulas, <em>Tanacetum gossypinum</em>, <em>Saussurea obvallata</em> and <em>gossypifera</em>, and the odorous <em>Rhododendron Anthopogon</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Western Himālayan Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Western Himālayan ranges differ greatly from the Eastern, in orientation, in greater length, higher latitude, cooler drier climate, and in the far greater breadth of the mountain mass west of the Sutlej. A transverse section drawn through the valley of Kashmīr, from the plain of the Punjab to the Kārakoram range, is three times as long as one drawn anywhere <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-209" data-page="209" data-folio="171" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n208_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 171">[p. 171]</button> transversely across the Eastern Himālayas, and, unlike the latter, it presents a series of parallel snow-clad ranges, which have a general direction from SW. to NE. Of the valleys enclosed by these ranges, those towards the plains are very narrow, tortuous, and steep; the rearward, on the contrary, are more open, with elevated, often saline floors, and owing to the dryness of the air are either sterile or support a Tibetan vegetation. The latter valleys constitute Little Tibet, forming the western termination of the Great Tibetan plateau. It would appear from the above that the Western Himālayan Flora should greatly outnumber the Eastern in genera and species, and but for the dryness and reduced temperature of its tropical and temperate zones it would doubtless do so. But if it is borne in mind that no area in the Western Region of the dimensions of Sikkim is nearly so rich as the latter, and that the Flora of the Western is fairly well explored, while the Eastern, except in Sikkim, is all but unexplored, the conclusion must be that the latter will prove to be far the richer botanically.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Upwards of 4,000 Flowering plants are recorded as Western Himālayan, comprised in 147<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-015" href="#sec-07-fn-015" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-015" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Natural Orders, and there are also 230 Ferns and their allies. Of the former, the following ten are the dominant, the number in brackets indicating their corresponding positions in the Eastern Himālayan decad:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Gramineae</em> (2).</li><li>6. <em>Ranunculaceae</em>.</li><li>2. <em>Compositae</em> (4).</li><li>7. <em>Orchideae</em> (1).</li><li>3. <em>Leguminosae</em> (3).</li><li>8. <em>Cruciferae</em>.</li><li>4. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (5).</li><li>9. <em>Rosaceae</em> (8)</li><li>5. <em>Labiatae</em>.</li><li>10. <em>Scrophulariaceae</em> (7).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The proportion of Monocotyledons to Dicotyledons is about 1 to 3. Twelve Eastern Himālayan Orders here disappear: namely, <em>Dilleniaceae</em>, <em>Guttiferae</em>, <em>Passifloraceae</em>, <em>Stylidieae</em>, <em>Vacciniaceae</em>, <em>Diapensiaceae</em>, <em>Myristicaceae</em>, <em>Proteaceae</em>, <em>Cycadeae</em>, <em>Burmanniaceae</em>, <em>Xyrideae</em>, and <em>Pandaneae</em>, together with Tree-ferns and many Palms. With the exception of <em>Vacciniaceae</em> and <em>Diapensiaceae</em>, all are tropical. The following Orders that are absent in the Eastern Himālayas are found in the Western: <em>Moringeae</em>, <em>Polemoniaceae</em>, <em>Illecebraceae</em>, of which <em>Polemonium</em> alone is strictly temperate or sub-alpine. Of even greater significance is the removal of Orchids from the first to the seventh place in the above decad, and the replacement of the tropical Orders of <em>Urticaceae</em>, <em>Rubiaceae</em>, and <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> of the Eastern Region <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-210" data-page="210" data-folio="172" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n209_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 172">[p. 172]</button> by the temperate <em>Ranunculaceae</em>, <em>Cruciferae</em>, and <em>Labiatae</em> of the Western. Finally, an instructive example of the difference between the Eastern and Western Floras is afforded by a reference to the recently published <em>Flora Simlensis</em> by the late Colonel Sir Henry Collett. In that work, 1,326 Flowering plants are described, of which nearly one-third are absent from Sikkim.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The number of genera in the Western Himālayan Region (about 1,220) does not greatly exceed that in Sikkim, but no fewer than 250 of them are absent from the latter; almost all of them are European, thus demonstrating the preponderance of this element in the West. Of the disappearance of trees prevalent almost throughout the tropical zone of the Himālayas, that of the Sāl between the Rāvi and Chenāb rivers is a very notable instance.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Orchids of this zone, especially the epiphytic, are few, and almost confined to the Districts of Kumaun and Garhwāl. There are only fifteen Dendrobes, all but one or two of which are also natives of Sikkim, where about forty are known. Of <em>Bulbophyllum</em> and <em>Cirrhopetalum</em>, only six species are recorded. The Palms are <em>Wallichia densiflora</em>, <em>Phœnix sylvestris</em>, <em>acaulis</em>, and <em>humilis</em>, and <em>Calamus tenuis</em>. The only common Bamboo is <em>Dendrocalamus strictus</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Temperate zone of Western Himālayas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Owing to the complexity of the mountain ranges, outer and inner, and the differences of their climates, it is very difficult to assign altitudinal limits to the temperate zone of the Western Himālayas. In Kumaun and Garhwāl I have estimated it as perhaps 1,000 feet lower than in Sikkim, but farther to the west and north observations are wanting for fixing it. The vegetation of the outer ranges is in character on the whole that of Sikkim, the difference being due mainly to the greatly increased number of European genera. All the Conifers of the Eastern Himālayas are present, except the Larch; and to these are added forests of Deodar, <em>Pinus longifolia</em> (a comparatively rare plant in Sikkim and there only tropical), <em>P. excelsa</em>, <em>Abies Pindrow</em>, <em>Cupressus torulosa</em>, <em>Juniperus macropoda</em>, and (in dry regions) <em>Pinus Gerardiana</em>, the first and three last giving, where found, a character to the landscape. Of Oaks there are six, four of them Sikkim species, two only being Western, of which one is the European Holm Oak (<em>Quercus Ilex</em>), which extends eastward to Kumaun at 3,000 to 8,500 feet elevation, and westward to Spain. The Indian Horse-Chestnut (<em>Aesculus indica</em>) represents the Eastern <em>Ae. punduana</em>. Two Birches and two Hornbeams are common to both Regions, but the Eastern-nut (<em>Corylus ferox</em>) is replaced by the Oriental <em>C. Colurna</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Among other shrubs and small trees peculiar to the Western Himālayas are the Indian Bladder-nut and Lilac (<em>Staphylea Emodi</em> and <em>Syringa Emodi</em>), <em>Rosa Webbiana</em>, <em>moschata</em>, and <em>Eglanteria</em>, <em>Parrottia Jacquemontiana</em>, the Mountain <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-212" data-page="212" data-folio="174" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n211_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 174">[p. 174]</button> Ash (<em>Pyrus Aucuparia</em>), the Bullace (<em>Prunus insititia</em>), and the common Hawthorn. On the other hand, the most striking difference between the temperate Floras of the Eastern and Western Himālayas is the paucity of species of Rhododendron in the latter, where only four are found—all common, at their respective elevations, in Sikkim—<em>R. Anthopogon</em>, <em>barbatum</em>, <em>campanulatum</em>, and <em>arboreum</em>; the latter also inhabits the mountains of Southern Malabar, Burma, and Ceylon. Of European herbaceous plants there occur several hundreds unknown in Sikkim, as <em>Ranunculus aquatilis</em> and <em>R. Lingua</em>, <em>Caltha palustris</em>, <em>Nymphaea alba</em>, <em>Lythrum Salicaria</em>, <em>Menyanthes trifoliata</em>, and <em>Cladium Mariscus</em>, which all occur in the Lake of Kashmīr; also species of <em>Aquilegia</em> and <em>Paeonia</em>, <em>Parnassia palustris</em>, <em>Adoxa Moschatellina</em>, <em>Lysimachia vulgaris</em>, <em>Polemonium caeruleum</em>, <em>Eriophorum vaginatum</em>, and many Grasses, Rushes, and Sedges. The genus <em>Impatiens</em> abounds in the temperate zone, at all elevations except the highest, the species being with few exceptions endemic. The Orchids of this zone are almost uniformly terrestrial; they include several European species unknown in the Eastern Himālayas (see footnote, p. 161). The only Palm is a <em>Trachycarpus</em>, confined to and local in Kumaun and Garhwāl. Of Bamboos there are four, all dwarf and gregarious.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Alpine zone of Western Himālayas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Assuming 11,000 to 12,000 feet as the lower limit of the alpine zone of the Western Himālayas in the outer ranges, and 18,000 feet as the normal upper limit of Flowering plants, about 600 species belonging to forty-eight Natural Orders are recorded as indigenous. Of these Orders the dominant ten are the following, with their relative positions in the alpine Sikkim decad indicated in brackets:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Compositae</em> (1).</li><li>6. <em>Ranunculaceae</em> (7).</li><li>2. <em>Gramineae</em> (9).</li><li>7. <em>Gentianeae</em>.</li><li>3. <em>Leguminosae</em>.</li><li>8. <em>Caryophylleae</em> (6).</li><li>4. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (3).</li><li>9. <em>Saxifragaceae</em> (4).</li><li>5. <em>Cruciferae</em> (5).</li><li>10. <em>Fumariaceae</em> (10).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Two Orders of the above decad, <em>Leguminosae</em> and <em>Gentianeae</em>, are absent in the corresponding Eastern one, where they are replaced by <em>Primulaceae</em> and <em>Scrophularineae</em>. I doubt, however, whether this and other comparisons will hold good when the alpine zone of Sikkim shall have been fully explored. As was to be expected, the Western alpine Flora is much richer than the Eastern, due to its greater area. <em>Corydalis</em>, <em>Draba</em>, <em>Potentilla</em>, <em>Gentiana</em>, <em>Juncus</em>, <em>Carex</em>. The above data all point to a further great predominance of the Eastern Himālayan alpine Flora over the Western to be expected when the 500 miles of mountains east of Sikkim shall have been botanized<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-017" href="#sec-07-fn-017" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-017" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Two of the most conspicuous alpine plants of Sikkim are absent in the Western Himālayas, <em>Rheum nobile</em> and <em>Tanacetum gossypinum</em> ; the latter has, however, the remarkable <em>Saussurea obvallata</em> and <em>gossypiphora</em>, together with, in the driest regions, some cushion-formed species of <em>Arenaria</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Tibetan valleys of Western Himālayas.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Ascending the Indus river a few tropical plants extend up to Gilgit (alt. 4,000 to 5,000 feet). At greater elevations the full effects are experienced of a dry climate, great cold alternating with fierce sun-heat, and consequent aridity. Between 12,000 and 14,000 feet the principal indigenous trees are <em>Populus euphratica</em> and <em>P. balsamifera</em>, and of shrubs or small trees <em>Ulmus parvifolia</em> and species of <em>Tamarix</em>, <em>Caragana</em>, <em>Rosa</em>, <em>Lonicera</em>, <em>Hippophae</em>, <em>Myricaria</em>, <em>Elaeagnus</em>, and <em>Salix</em>. The cultivated trees are the fruit-bearing European ones, with <em>Populus alba</em> and <em>P. nigra</em>. Above 14,000 and up to 18,000 feet is a region of alpine perennials of European, Oriental, and Central Asian Orders and genera, as <em>Fumariaceae</em>, <em>Leguminosae</em> (<em>Astragalus</em> especially), <em>Compositae</em>, <em>Labiatae</em>, and <em>Stipaceae</em>. The only Orchids are a few species of <em>Orchis</em> and <em>Herminium</em>. Above 17,000 feet, twenty-five genera are recorded, all (except <em>Biebersteinia</em>) European, and many of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-214" data-page="214" data-folio="176" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n213_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 176">[p. 176]</button> them British, as <em>Potentilla Sibbaldi</em> and <em>anserina</em>, <em>Saxifraga cernua</em>, and <em>Lloydia serotina</em>. The most typical plant of this region is <em>Arenaria rupicola</em>, which forms hard white cushions or balls a foot in diameter, apparently the growth of centuries. The genera <em>Astragalus</em>, <em>Saussurea</em>, <em>Artemisia</em>, <em>Tanacetum</em>, and <em>Allardia</em> have many endemic species at these elevations. Where saline soils occur <em>Chenopodiaceae</em> abound, with two endemic Crucifers (<em>Dilophia</em> and <em>Christolea</em>), also <em>Sonchus maritimus</em>, <em>Glaux maritima</em>, and <em>Triglochin maritimum</em>. The fresh-water plants of this region include <em>Ranunculus aquatilis</em>, <em>Hippuris vulgaris</em>, <em>Limosella aquatica</em>, and species of <em>Utricularia</em>, <em>Potamogeton</em>, and <em>Zannichellia</em>. Ferns are all but absent.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Indus Plain Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Whether proceeding across the Indus Plain Region in a SW. direction from the Himālayas to Sind, or in a SE. from the Afghān border to Western Rājputāna, vegetation rapidly diminishes, approaching extinction in the Indian desert. Over the whole region a low, chiefly herbaceous vegetation of plants common to most parts of India, mixed with Oriental, African, and European types, is spread, with thickets of shrubs and a few trees. With few exceptions all have deciduous leaves, and most of the herbaceous are burnt or dried up in the hot season, the principal exceptions to the latter being <em>Chenopodiaceae</em>, which especially affect the frequent saline soils. On Mount Abu, an outlier of the Arāvalli Hills in the SE. of this region, 5,653 feet in elevation, more humid tropical types appear, with <em>Rosa Lyellii</em>, <em>Vogelia indica</em>, and an epiphytic orchid (<em>Aerides</em>); many cultivated fruits succeed in the villages, as the mulberry, tamarind, mango, guava, and custard apple.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">From the scattered materials available it may be assumed that the Flora of this region may comprise about 1,500 indigenous Flowering plants, under 112 Orders, of which the following are the dominant ten, with, in brackets, their corresponding positions in the Gangetic Plain decad:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Gramineae</em> (1).</li><li>2. <em>Leguminosae</em> (2).</li><li>3. <em>Compositae</em> (4).</li><li>4. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (3).</li><li>5. <em>Scrophularineae</em> (5).</li><li>6. <em>Labiatae</em> (10).</li><li>7. <em>Boragineae</em>.</li><li>8. <em>Malvaceae</em> (6).</li><li>9. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> (8).</li><li>10. <em>Convolvulaceae</em> (9).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of the 112 Orders about thirty-five are represented by a single genus only, and thirteen by a single species. The number of monotypic genera is also very large. The high position of <em>Boragineae</em> is a marked feature. The chief arboreous vegetation consists of isolated groups of trees in the outskirts <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-215" data-page="215" data-folio="177" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n214_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 177">[p. 177]</button> of the Western Himālayan Region, on the banks of the rivers, and in the Ajmer forest on the flanks of the Arāvalli hills in Rājputāna<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-018" href="#sec-07-fn-018" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-018" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. The only indigenous Palms are <em>Phoenix sylvestris</em> and <em>Nannorrhops Ritchieana</em>. The only Bamboo is <em>Dendrocalamus strictus</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of shrubs, among the most conspicuous are isolated clumps of the columnar, almost leafless <em>Euphorbia Royleana</em> and <em>neriifolia</em>. Other more or less prevalent shrubs and under-shrubs in certain districts are <em>Capparis aphylla, horrida,</em> and <em>spinosa, Flacourtia Ramontchi, Tamarix dioica</em> and <em>gallica, Grewia</em> (seven species), <em>Fagonia arabica, Rhamnus persica</em> and <em>virgata, Zizyphus nummularia, vulgaris,</em> and <em>Oenoplia, Dodonaea viscosa, Alhagi maurorum, Sophora mollis, Cassia auriculata, Tora,</em> and <em>obovata, Mimosa rubicaulis, Pluchea lanceolata, Reptonia buxifolia, Carissa diffusa, Rhazya stricta, Nerium odorum, Orthanthera viminea, Periploca aphylla, Calotropis procera</em> and <em>gigantea, Withania coagulans, Adhatoda Vasica, Calligonum polygonoides, Pteropyrum Olivieri, Salsola foetida</em>, and species of <em>Kochia, Suaeda, Anabasis</em>, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of special interest as shrubs of this region are three species of Cotton (<em>Gossypium</em>), a genus unknown as native in any other part of India. These are <em>G. Stocksii</em> in Sind, <em>G. Wightianum</em> in the Arāvalli forests, and <em>G. herbaceum</em> (?) in the Ambāla District of the Punjab. Having regard, however, to the many centuries during which cotton has been a staple crop in India, it may be doubted whether these three may not be the descendants of as many cultivated plants. It is a remarkable fact that one of these (<em>G. Wightianum</em>), which is grown as <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-216" data-page="216" data-folio="178" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n215_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 178">[p. 178]</button> an erect shrub in many parts of India, should in the Arāvallis assume a scandent habit quite foreign to the genus.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of climbing plants in this Region, <em>Ephedra Alte</em>, <em>Cuscuta reflexa</em>, <em>Cassytha filiformis</em>, with species of <em>Convolvulaceae</em> and <em>Asclepiadeae</em>, are the most frequent. The herbaceous vegetation includes plants of all the Orders in the decad, with others of <em>Fumariaceae</em>, <em>Papaveraceae</em>, <em>Cruciferae</em>, <em>Resedaceae</em>, <em>Caryophylleae</em>, <em>Zygophylleae</em>, <em>Ficoideae</em>, <em>Cucurbitaceae</em>, <em>Plantagineae</em>, <em>Amarantaceae</em>, <em>Chenopodiaceae</em>, and <em>Polygoneae</em>. The European <em>Asphodelus fistulosus</em> is a pest in cornfields. The curious eastern Rosaceous plant <em>Neurada procumbens</em> is confined to this Region, and the widely spread <em>Frankenia pulverulenta</em> and <em>Herniaria hirsuta</em> advance very little to the east of it. The Persian <em>Seetzenia orientalis</em> is in India all but confined to Sind; and the only Orchid is the diminutive terrestrial <em>Zeuxine sulcata</em>, which is ubiquitous in the plains of India. Grasses abound, especially <em>Eragrostis cynosuroides</em>, <em>Cynodon Dactylon</em>, <em>Eleusine flagellifera</em>, <em>Aeluropus villosus</em>, the odorous <em>Andropogon muricatus</em>, <em>Irawancusa</em> and <em>Nardus</em>, and the inodorous <em>I. squarrosus</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Ferns are exceedingly few in this Region; five are found rather commonly in the dry districts, namely, <em>Nephrodium molle</em>, <em>Adiantum caudatum</em>, <em>A. Capillus-Veneris</em>, and <em>Actinopteris radiata</em>, with, on Mount Abu only, <em>Nephrodium cicutarium</em>, <em>Cheilanthes farinosa</em>, <em>Botrychium virginianum</em>, and <em>Adiantum lunulatum</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is not possible within the limits of this Sketch to enumerate in detail the botanical features of the various districts of the Indus Plain Flora, or to discuss the distribution of its principal trees and shrubs, many of which are limited as to locality. Having, however, been kindly favoured by Mr. T. R. Drummond, F.L.S., with some observations on this subject made purposely for this Sketch, I shall summarize them here, before concluding with a brief notice of some peculiarities of the Salt Range, Sind, and Indus Delta Floras.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Mr. Drummond selects the following plants as characteristic of the Indus Plain throughout: <em>Peganum Harmala</em>, <em>Crotalaria Burhia</em>, <em>Dalbergia Sissoo</em>, <em>Aerua lanata</em>, <em>Asphodelus fistulosus</em>, and species of <em>Suaeda</em>, <em>Kochia</em>, <em>Cenchrus</em>, <em>Pennisetum</em>, <em>Apluda</em>, <em>Eleusine</em>, and <em>Saccharum</em>. In Sind and the South Punjab he cites species of <em>Maerua</em>, <em>Crataeva</em>, <em>Cleome</em>, <em>Wrightia</em>, <em>Acacia rupestris</em>, <em>Nerium</em>, <em>Pulicaria Wallichiana</em>, <em>Echinops</em>, <em>Anticharis</em>, <em>Cordia Rothii</em>, <em>Blepharis sindica</em>, species of <em>Ocimum</em> and <em>Arnebia hispidissima</em>, the last two covering a large area of sand and perfuming the air in spring. In the Indus valley proper <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-217" data-page="217" data-folio="179" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n216_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 179">[p. 179]</button> he gives the following as characteristic: <em>Capparis spinosa</em>, <em>Diplotaxis Griffithii</em>, <em>Malcolmia strigosa</em>, <em>Gymnosporia</em>, <em>Sageretia Brandrethiana</em>, <em>Pluchea arguta</em>, <em>Pulicaria glaucescens</em>, <em>Boucerosia Aucheriana</em>, <em>Arnebia Griffithii</em>, <em>Withania coagulans</em>, <em>Stachys parviflora</em>, <em>Otostegia limbata</em>, <em>Eremostachys</em>, <em>Olea cuspidata</em>, <em>Pennisetum dichotomum</em>, <em>Aeluropus villosus</em>, <em>Haloxylon</em>, <em>Salsola</em>, and <em>Nannorrhops Ritchieana</em>. To the eastern districts belong <em>Capparis spinosa</em>, <em>Anogeissus pendula</em>, <em>Euphorbia Royleana</em>, <em>Cordia Rothii</em>, <em>Dichrostachys cinerea</em>, <em>Sterculia urens</em>, <em>Moringa concanensis</em>, <em>Boswellia serrata</em>, and <em>Acacia leucophloea</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Rāwalpindi, of which the principal hills are the Salt Range, occupies the NW. corner of the Indus Plain area. Glancing at the map, it might be supposed that its Flora was that of the outer hills of the Himālayas; but this is not so, for though typically Himālayan plants occur on the more elevated parts of the range, its predominating vegetation is Oriental and European. Of this the most conspicuous examples are the <em>Resedaceae</em>, many species of <em>Cruciferae</em>, and the remarkable Palm <em>Nannorrhops Ritchieana</em>, the north-eastern limit of which is the Salt Range, the south-western limit Sind and Baluchistān.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sind occupies about one-third of the Indus Plain area. Its Flora is a very poor one, comprising perhaps not more than 500 recorded species; but it is a very noteworthy one, in that it comprises a much larger proportion of North African plants than does any other Indian area, and that a very considerable number of these have not been found anywhere to the eastward of Sind. Its Flora promises to offer a most interesting study when material for this shall be forthcoming, the absence or rarity of species of higher groups which are prevalent in the Punjab being as remarkable as the prevalence of some others. The indigenous Cotton (<em>Gossypium Stocksii</em>) and Palm (<em>Nannorrhops Ritchieana</em>), both mentioned already, are its two most notable plants.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Indus Delta repeats the vegetation of the Sundarbans of Bengal, with a greatly reduced number of species, and the absence of the two Palms (<em>Nipa fruticans</em> and <em>Phoenix paludosa</em>), and of the genus <em>Calamus</em>. It has, however, species of <em>Avicennia</em>, <em>Sonneratia</em>, <em>Rhizophora</em>, <em>Ceriops</em>, <em>Aegiceras</em>, and <em>Scaevola</em>, the latter genus unknown in the Sundarbans, together with the stately grass, <em>Oryza coarctata</em>, which has been found nowhere except in these two deltas.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Gangetic Plain Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Gangetic Plain Region presents three assemblages of plants which may be regarded as botanical Sub-regions : <strong>I.</strong> The dry upper valley from Eastern Rājputāna to the Kosi river; <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-218" data-page="218" data-folio="180" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n217_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 180">[p. 180]</button> that is, to a little above the bend of the Ganges at Rājmahāl; II. Bengal proper of the old maps, defined<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-020" href="#sec-07-fn-020" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-020" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> as 'the humid region of the Gangetic delta and the region immediately north of it.' This Sub-region includes the Assam plain and a coast strip of Orissa, as far as the Mahānadī river. III. The Sundarbans.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The number of indigenous species in this Region is small, possibly amounting to 1,500, under 112 Orders, of which the following ten are dominant; the attached numbers in brackets are those of the corresponding decad in the Indus Plain Region:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Gramineae</em> (1).</li><li>6. <em>Malvaceae</em> (8).</li><li>2. <em>Leguminosae</em> (2).</li><li>7. <em>Acanthaceae</em>.</li><li>3. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (4).</li><li>8. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> (9).</li><li>4. <em>Compositae</em> (3).</li><li>9. <em>Convolvulaceae</em> (10).</li><li>5. <em>Scrophularineae</em> (5).</li><li>10. <em>Labiatae</em> (6).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The only other Orders rich in genera and species are <em>Cucurbitaceae</em>, <em>Asclepiadeae</em>, <em>Verbenaceae</em>, and <em>Amarantaceae</em>. The largest genera are <em>Hibiscus</em>, <em>Indigofera</em>, <em>Crotalaria</em>, <em>Ipomoea</em>, <em>Polygonum</em>, <em>Cyperus</em>, <em>Fimbristylis</em>, <em>Panicum</em>, <em>Andropogon</em>, and <em>Eragrostis</em>; <em>Gymnospermae</em> and <em>Cupuliferae</em> are absent; and the European Orders <em>Ranunculaceae</em>, <em>Cruciferae</em>, <em>Caryophylleae</em>, <em>Geraniaceae</em>, <em>Rosaceae</em>, <em>Saxifragaceae</em>, <em>Campanulaceae</em>, and <em>Gentianeae</em> are very scantily represented, chiefly by annual weeds.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Upper Gangetic Plain.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The indigenous vegetation of the upper part of this Sub-region is that of a dry country, the trees in the dry season being leafless (for the most part) and the grasses and other herbs burnt up; but by far the greater part of the land to the eastward contrasts with that to the westward in being under cultivation. In the extreme west, the Flora is continuous with that of the Indus Plain, and might perhaps be better included in that Region, <em>Peganum Harmala</em>, <em>Pluchea lanceolata</em>, <em>Tecoma undulata</em>, and other plants characteristic of the Punjab being equally so of the western part of this Sub-region. These gradually disappear in following the course of the Ganges downwards into a more humid climate, and towards the entrance of the Kosi river they are replaced by <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-219" data-page="219" data-folio="181" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n218_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 181">[p. 181]</button> the plants typical of Bengal proper. The principal forest is that of Ajmer, flanking the Arāvalli and other hills which bound the Sub-region on the west. The most characteristic tree of this forest is <em>Anogeissus pendula</em>; others more or less restricted to Western India are <em>Boswellia serrata</em>, <em>Balsamodendron Mukul</em> (which is rare), <em>Moringa pterygosperma</em>, <em>Rhus mysorensis</em>, <em>Acacia Senegal</em>, and <em>Prosopis spicigera</em>, accompanied by the common trees of the drier parts of India, especially <em>Butea frondosa</em>. The Bengal rose (<em>Rosa involucrata</em>) occurs frequently, forming an erect bush in the open, and seeming to an English eye to be quite out of place in its climate and its surroundings. Several British herbaceous plants are common, flowering in the cool season, especially <em>Ranunculus sceleratus</em>, <em>Malva rotundifolia</em>, <em>Lathyrus Aphaca</em>, <em>Anagallis arvensis</em>, and <em>Veronica agrestis</em>. <em>Dendrocalamus strictus</em> and <em>Bambusa Balcooa</em> are the only indigenous Bamboos, being natives of the bordering hills rather than of the plain. The cultivated <em>Phoenix</em> and <em>Borassus</em> are the two Palms most commonly seen, but two species of Rattan (<em>Calamus</em>) are found in thickets. Considerable areas of this Sub-region are occupied by the Usar, or Reh-lands, which, being impregnated with alkalies, and converted into swamps in the rainy season and into deserts in the dry, are as unfavourable to a native as they are to an introduced vegetation. <em>Salvadora persica</em> is said to be the only tree that will succeed on the most saline of them, and of herbaceous plants a few perennial-rooted grasses are the only ones which thrive.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Bengal Proper.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Bengal proper, by its humidity and luxuriant evergreen vegetation, contrasts favourably with the upper valley of the Ganges. The villages are usually buried in groves of Mango, Figs, and Bamboos, with the Betel-nut Palm, Palmyra, Phoenix, and Coco-nut. The trees are of many kinds, and it is difficult to distinguish the indigenous from the introduced. Except perhaps a few <em>Fici</em>, the most common belong to the latter class. Such are <em>Michelia Champaca</em>, <em>Polyalthia longifolia</em>, <em>Bombax malabaricum</em>, <em>Eriodendron anfractuosum</em>, <em>Lagerstroemia Flos-reginae</em>, <em>Pterospermum acerifolium</em>, with species of <em>Terminalia</em> and <em>Artocarpus</em>. The shrubby and herbaceous vegetations are for the most part of species found all over India. In the <em>Jhīl</em> district in the east, where the waters of the great rivers Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Surmā inosculate and overflow during the rains, aquatic and marsh plants, especially <em>Cyperaceae</em> and tall <em>Gramineae</em> of many kinds, prevail, but not the <em>Nymphaeaceae</em> (<em>Nymphaea</em>, <em>Nelumbium</em>, and <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-220" data-page="220" data-folio="182" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n219_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 182">[p. 182]</button> <em>Euryale</em>), which affect stiller and shallower waters. The most interesting of the water-plants of India is <em>Aldrovanda vesiculosa</em>, the curious floating Fly-trap mentioned at p. 161. There are few Orchids, and those are mostly terrestrial. The epiphytic Orchids include species of <em>Vanda</em>, <em>Luisia</em>, and <em>Cymbidium</em>. <em>Aroideae</em> abound, and are conspicuous features both wild and in cultivation; among the latter are broad-leaved species of <em>Amorphophallus</em>, <em>Alocasia</em>, and <em>Colocasia</em>; of the wild the most conspicuous are <em>Scindapsus officinalis</em>, clothing the trunks of trees with its magnificent foliage, and <em>Pothos scandens</em>, which simulates the Ivy in covering walls and tree trunks. The prickly <em>Lasia heterophylla</em> abounds in the marshes, and species of the curious genus <em>Cryptocoryne</em>, with twisted spathes, are frequent on the river margins. <em>Pistia Stratiotes</em> is ubiquitous in fresh water, and several species of <em>Lemna</em> are common. An anomalous feature in the Flora of Bengal is the occurrence on rising grounds between the <em>Jhīls</em> of a few plants typical of the Khāsi Hills. Of this the District of Mymensingh affords an example, in which may be found, together with the common plants of Bengal, a few wanderers from the hilly regions to the northward.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Sundarbans.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The estuarial Floras<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-021" href="#sec-07-fn-021" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-021" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> of India are notable, inasmuch as that, considering the limited areas they occupy, they contain more local species than do any other botanical regions in India. This is due to the saline properties of their waters, and to tidal action on the land. The islets of the Sundarbans are in great part clothed with a dense evergreen forest of trees and shrubs, amongst which the various Mangroves hold the first place, with an undergrowth of climbers and herbaceous plants, together with <em>Typhaceae</em>, <em>Gramineae</em>, and <em>Cyperaceae</em>. Two gregarious Palms form conspicuous features, the stemless <em>Nipa fruticans</em> in the swamps and river banks, with leaves thirty feet long, and the elegant <em>Phoenix paludosa</em> in drier localities; as also do the cultivated Coco-nut and Betel-nut Palms. The principal exceptions to these forest-clad tracts are the sand hills occurring at intervals along the coasts facing the sea, the vegetation of which differs from that of the inland muddy islets and grassy savannahs which become more frequent in advancing eastward towards the mouth of the Meghnā.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-221"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="221" data-folio="183" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n220_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 183">[p. 183]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Three hundred indigenous species of Flowering plants, under seventy-two Orders, and seventeen Ferns and their allies are comprised in the Sundarbans Flora.<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-022" href="#sec-07-fn-022" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-022" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> Of these the dominant Orders are :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Leguminosae</em>.</li><li>2. <em>Gramineae</em>.</li><li>3. <em>Cyperaceae</em>.</li><li>4. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>.</li><li>5. <em>Orchideae</em>.</li><li>6. <em>Compositae</em>.</li><li>7. <em>Asclepiadeae</em>.</li><li>8. <em>Verbenaceae</em>.</li><li>9. <em>Convolvulaceae</em>.</li><li>10. { <em>Malvaceae</em>. <em>Rubiaceae</em>. <em>Acanthaceae</em>. <em>Urticaceae</em>.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A very large proportion of genera consist of a solitary species. Of the trees and shrubs one alone, <em>Ficus</em>, contains four. There are about fifty species of trees which may be classed according to whether they are purely estuarial, or common to other parts of India. Among the former are <em>Hibiscus tortuosus</em>, <em>Thespesia populnea</em>, <em>Brownlowia lanceolata</em>, <em>Amoora cucullata</em>, <em>Carapa moluccensis</em> and <em>obovata</em>, <em>Bouea burmanica</em>, <em>Erythrina indica</em>, <em>Afzelia bijuga</em>, <em>Rhizophora conjugata</em> and <em>mucronata</em>, <em>Ceriops Roxburghiana</em>, <em>Kandelia Rheedei</em>, <em>Bruguiera gymnorhiza</em> and <em>parviflora</em>, <em>Lumnitzera racemosa</em>, <em>Barringtonia racemosa</em>, <em>Sonneratia apetala</em> and <em>acida</em>, <em>Aegialitis rotundifolia</em>, <em>Aegiceras majus</em>, <em>Cerbera Odollam</em>, <em>Avicennia officinalis</em> and <em>alba</em>, <em>Excaecaria Agallocha</em>, <em>Sapium indicum</em>, <em>Casuarina equisetifolia</em> (doubtfully indigenous), <em>Nipa fruticans</em>, and <em>Phoenix paludosa</em> ; to which may be added, as estuarial woody climbers, <em>Hibiscus tiliaceus</em>, <em>Dalbergia spinosa</em> and <em>torta</em>, <em>Mucuna gigantea</em>, <em>Derris sinuata</em> and <em>uliginosa</em>, <em>Finlaysonia obovata</em>, and <em>Sarcolobus globosus</em> ; and among under-shrubs <em>Acanthus ilicifolius</em> and <em>volubilis</em>. Of trees common to inland Bengal there are about as many as there are purely estuarial; they include <em>Kleinhovia Hospita</em>, <em>Micromelum pubescens</em>, <em>Aegle Marmelos</em>, <em>Zizyphus Jujuba</em>, <em>Odina Wodier</em>, <em>Cassia Fistula</em>, <em>Pongamia glabra</em>, <em>Acacia tomentosa</em> and <em>arabica</em>, <em>Barringtonia acutangula</em>, <em>Ixora parviflora</em>, <em>Morinda bracteata</em>, <em>Diospyros montana</em> and <em>Embryopteris</em>, <em>Cordia Myxa</em>, <em>Dolichandrone Rheedii</em>, <em>Vitex trifolia</em> and <em>Negundo</em>, <em>Cyclostemon assamicus</em>, <em>Croton oblongifolius</em>, <em>Anti-desma Ghaesembilla</em>, <em>Trewia nudiflora</em>, <em>Streblus asper</em>, <em>Trema orientalis</em>, and four species of <em>Ficus</em>.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-222"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="222" data-folio="184" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n221_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 184">[p. 184]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of herbaceous plants that are purely estuarial there are few, the most remarkable of them being two tall grasses : one, <em>Oryza coarctata</em>, which grows profusely on the banks of the islets, but (except in the delta of the Indus) has been found nowhere else; the other, <em>Myriostachya Wightiana</em>, is also a native of estuaries on the eastern side of the Bay of Bengal and of Ceylon.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of aquatic plants with floating leaves <em>Nymphaeaceae</em> are entirely absent, as are <em>Lemnaceae</em>; but <em>Limnanthemum cristatum</em> is found, and in the salt-pans on the northern limits of this Sub-region <em>Aldrovanda vesiculosa</em>, mentioned above (pp. 161, 182). There are also a few species of <em>Utricularia</em>, <em>Ipomoea aquatica</em>, and the common <em>Naiadaceae</em> and <em>Hydrocharideae</em> of India. <em>Pistia</em> abounds in tanks and the fresher-water districts. The Sundarbans Orchids comprise eight genera and thirteen species, all epiphytic. A species of <em>Cirrhopetalum</em> alone is endemic. Of parasites there are four species of <em>Loranthaceae</em>, a <em>Cuscuta</em>, and <em>Cassytha</em>. <em>Bambuseae</em> are entirely absent. The indigenous Palms are the above-mentioned <em>Nipa</em> and <em>Phoenix</em>, and two Rattans (a <em>Calamus</em> and a <em>Daemonorops</em>), both the latter common to Bengal.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Major Prain, in his ‘Flora of the Sundribuns’ (see footnote, p. 183), enumerates thirty-five littoral flowering plants, under twenty-one Orders, which, though widely distributed on other shores of India, have not been detected in this Region; among the most common of these are <em>Calophyllum inophyllum</em>, <em>Heritiera littoralis</em>, <em>Suriana maritima</em>, <em>Sophora tomentosa</em>, <em>Lumnitzera coccinea</em>, <em>Pemphis acidula</em>, <em>Scaevola Koenigii</em>, <em>Pisonia</em> (three species), <em>Euphorbia Atoto</em>, <em>Lepturus repens</em>, and <em>Spinifex squarrosus</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A most remarkable character of the estuarian vegetation is the habit of several of the endemic species to send up from their subterranean roots a multitude of aerial root-suckers, in some cases several feet long, which act as respiratory organs. These suckers occur in species of <em>Avicennia</em>, <em>Carapa</em>, <em>Heritiera</em>, <em>Amoora</em>, <em>Sonneratia</em>, and in <em>Phoenix paludosa</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Western Peninsula (the Deccan and Malabar Regions).</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Flora of the large portion of India, roughly bounded on the north by the Vindhya, Kaimur, and Rājmahāl Hills, and extending south to Cape Comorin, comprises two very distinct botanical Regions, a comparatively narrow western and a broad eastern; but materials do not exist for drawing the phytographical boundary line between them with any approach to accuracy. This is due to the fact that the western Region (Malabar), which is mountainous throughout (and technically <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-223" data-page="223" data-folio="185" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n222_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 185">[p. 185]</button> called the Western Ghāts), sends, for a great part of its length, spurs across the more depressed eastern Region, carrying with them characteristic western plants. Furthermore, no complete local Flora has been published for any considerable area of the Peninsula, and over many parts no collections have been made; not even a list of the plants around the city of Madras has been published, and only a very imperfect one of the Bombay Presidency<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-023" href="#sec-07-fn-023" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-023" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. Before, therefore, proceeding to describe the two Regions of Malabar and the Deccan, I shall, regarding them as one, contrast it with that of the Botanical Regions on the eastern side of the Bay of Bengal (Burma).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Upwards of 4,000 species of Flowering plants have been recorded from the whole Western Peninsula as above delimited, under about 150 Natural Orders, of which the ten dominant are here given in approximately numerical sequence, the figures in brackets indicating their corresponding position in the Burmese decad :—</p></div><div Palms <em>Nipa</em> and <em>Phoenix paludosa</em> are unknown on the west side south of Orissa, as are <em>Cupuliferae</em>, of which upwards of forty species are found in the Eastern Region.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Malabar Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Malabar is almost throughout a hilly or mountainous country, and is (except in the north) of excessive humidity, the mountains often rising abruptly from the flat coast of the Arabian Sea. The average breadth of the Region may be about 50 miles. Its abrupt western face is clothed with a luxuriant forest vegetation of Malayan type, except towards the north, where, with the drier climate, the elements of the Deccan and Indus Plain Floras compete with that of Malabar. The eastern face slopes gradually into the elevated plateau of the Deccan, but it is varied by many spurs being thrown off which extend far to the eastward, often, as above stated, enclosing valleys with a Malabar Flora. One great break occurs in the chain in lat. 11° N., where a transverse valley separates Travancore from the mountains north of it, and carries species characteristic of the Malabar Flora almost across the Peninsula. Travancore, thus isolated, presents a remarkable similarity to Ceylon in position, outline, and in many features of its vegetation. The most distinctive characters of the Malabar Flora, in contrast with that of the Deccan, are : firstly, the presence of <em>Guttiferae</em> (thirteen), <em>Dipterocarpeae</em> (twelve), <em>Myristiceae</em>, a <em>Helicia</em>, many Palms (twenty-one), and <em>Bambuseae</em>; secondly, the great excess of species of Malayan type, especially <em>Sterculiaceae</em>, <em>Tiliaceae</em>, <em>Anacardiaceae</em>, <em>Meliaceae</em>, <em>Myrtaceae</em>,</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-225"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="225" data-folio="187" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n224_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 187">[p. 187]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><em>Melastomaceae</em>, <em>Ampelideae</em>, <em>Gesneraceae</em>, <em>Piperaceae</em>, <em>Scitamineae</em>, <em>Orchideae</em>, and <em>Aroideae</em>. One Coniferous plant alone has been found in the whole Deccan Peninsula, <em>Podocarpus latifolia</em>, confined to the Tinnevelly Ghāts at 3,000 to 5,000 feet elevation. Burma and the Malayan Peninsula are the only other Regions in which it occurs. Of the Palms, one genus is all but endemic, <em>Bentinckia Coddapanna</em>, a native of Travancore, the only congener of which is confined to the Nicobar Islands. The only species of <em>Pinanga</em> (<em>P. Dicksonii</em>) is endemic, as are nine species of <em>Calamus</em>. Other Palms are solitary species of <em>Arenga</em>, <em>Caryota</em>, <em>Corypha</em>, with the cultivated <em>Areca Catechu</em>, <em>Borassus</em>, and Coco-nut, which latter may be indigenous on the coast, as it is said to be in the Laccadive Islands. <em>Phoenix sylvestris</em>, doubtfully indigenous, is common in the north but rare in the south; and there are several indigenous species of the genus in the hills which have not been botanically distinguished, one especially that grows gregariously in the Ghāts 40 miles west of Poona, with a trunk 30 feet high and 15 inches in diameter. Among shrubs, the genus <em>Strobilanthes</em> with forty-six species holds the first place, distinguished for the beauty of their flowers and for the singular habit (alluded to at p. 160) of many of its species flowering simultaneously for the first and only time, at a fixed period of growth, and dying after fruiting. Of <em>Bambuseae</em> there are seventeen species, arboreous and shrubby, five of them also natives of Ceylon. Among herbaceous plants the genus <em>Impatiens</em> with about sixty endemic species is the most conspicuous, almost carpeting the ground in many places, in others occurring epiphytically, which is not known to be the case with this huge genus in any other part of India. Of Orchids there are only about 200, a singular contrast to the hosts of this Order inhabiting Burma and the Malayan Peninsula. Most of the genera are epiphytic, and of these the largest is <em>Dendrobium</em>, with sixteen species. <em>Habenaria</em>, with about forty-six species, is the largest by far of terrestrial genera. <em>Umbelliferae</em> occur throughout the range of the Western Ghāts, increasing in numbers northwards; eleven genera with thirty species have been recorded. Several genera of the curious aquatic Order <em>Podostemonaceae</em>, mentioned at p. 161, abound in the fresh-water torrents and in stiller waters of the Ghāts.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Nīlgiris.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Nīlgiri Hills form a nœud of the Western Ghāts, where these attain their greatest elevation, viz. 8,760 feet. They rise precipitously from the west to extensive grassy downs and table-lands seamed with densely wooded gorges (Sholas).</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-226"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="226" data-folio="188" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n225_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 188">[p. 188]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">These grassy downs possess in parts a rich shrubby and herbaceous Flora. Among the shrubs, some of the most characteristic are <em>Strobilanthes Kunthianus</em>, <em>Berberis aristata</em>, <em>Hypericum mysorense</em>, many <em>Leguminosae</em>, as the common Gorse (introduced), <em>Sophora glauca</em> and <em>Crotalaria formosa</em>, <em>Rhododendron arboreum</em>, species of <em>Rubus</em>, <em>Osbeckia</em>, <em>Myrtaceae</em>, <em>Hedyotis</em>, <em>Helichrysum</em>, <em>Gaultheria</em>. Among the herbaceous plants are species of <em>Senecio</em>, <em>Anaphalis</em>, <em>Ceropegia</em>, <em>Pedicularis</em>, and <em>Cyanotis</em>. Most conspicuous of all is the tall <em>Lobelia excelsa</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">But the richest assemblage is found in the Sholas, which, commencing at about 5,000 feet, ascend to the summits of the range. They are filled with an evergreen forest of tall, usually round-headed trees, with a rich undergrowth. Of the trees, some of the most conspicuous are <em>Michelia nilagirica</em>, <em>Ternstroemia japonica</em>, <em>Gordonia obtusa</em>, species of <em>Ilex</em>, <em>Meliosma</em>, <em>Microtropis</em>, <em>Euonymus</em>, <em>Photinia</em>, <em>Viburnum hebanthum</em>, <em>Eugenia</em> (three species), and several of <em>Symplocos</em>, <em>Glochidion</em>, <em>Araliaceae</em>, and <em>Laurineae</em>. Of shrubs, <em>Strobilanthes</em> takes the first place, then <em>Rubiaceae</em>, with species of <em>Eurya</em>, <em>Ligustrum</em>, and <em>Vernonia</em>. Of climbers, there are <em>Rosa Leschenaultiana</em>, <em>Jasminum brevilobum</em>, <em>Gardneria ovata</em>, <em>Gymnema hirsutum</em>, and <em>Elaeagnus latifolia</em>. The genus <em>Impatiens</em> takes the lead among conspicuous herbaceous plants, and the beautiful <em>Lilium neilgherrense</em> is a notable feature. At lower elevations in the Sholas, <em>Hydnocarpus alpina</em> is a very common tree, and in dry localities <em>Rhododendron arboreum</em> and <em>Vaccinium Leschenaultii</em>. Two species of Bamboo are found in the Sholas, namely, <em>Arundinaria Wightiana</em> in the higher parts, and <em>Oxytenanthera Thwaitesii</em> (also a native of Ceylon) in the lower, where also three Tree-ferns appear, with many Orchids and several species of <em>Calamus</em>, all evidences of a higher temperature.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sholas similar to those of the Nīlgiris occur on the Anaimalai, Palni, and other ranges of the Malabar Ghāts ; but these, being of a lower elevation, and in a lower latitude, harbour a more tropical vegetation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The most interesting feature of the Nīlgiri Flora is its affinity with that of the cool regions of the far-distant Khāsi, Manipur, and Nāgā Hills in Northern Burma. Among trees and shrubs common to these two localities (and most of them to the temperate Eastern Himālayas also), are <em>Ternstroemia japonica</em>, <em>Hypericum Hookerianum</em> and <em>napaulense</em>, <em>Eurya japonica</em>, <em>Rhamnus dahuricus</em>, <em>Photinia Notoniana</em>, <em>Rubus ellipticus</em> and <em>lasiocarpus</em>, <em>Carallia integerrima</em>, <em>Rhododendron arboreum</em>, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-227" data-page="227" data-folio="189" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n226_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 189">[p. 189]</button> <em>Gaultheria fragrantissima</em>, and <em>Gardneria ovata</em>, together with species of <em>Kadsura</em>, <em>Berberis</em>, <em>Pittosporum</em>, <em>Elaeocarpus</em>, <em>Euonymus</em>, <em>Meliosma</em>, <em>Pygeum</em>, <em>Rosa</em>, <em>Viburnum</em>, <em>Lonicera</em>, <em>Vaccinium</em>. The herbaceous plants common to the Nīlgiri and Khāsi Hills are too numerous to mention. Most of them are of European genera, and some are European species, as <em>Stellaria uliginosa</em>, <em>Circaea alpina</em>, <em>Sanicula europaea</em>, and <em>Prunella vulgaris</em>. Other herbaceous European genera are <em>Thalictrum</em>, <em>Ranunculus</em>, <em>Cardamine</em>, <em>Geranium</em>, <em>Alchemilla</em>, <em>Fragaria</em>, <em>Potentilla</em>, <em>Parnassia</em>, <em>Lysimachia</em>, <em>Swertia</em>, <em>Halenia</em>, <em>Gentiana</em>, <em>Calamintha</em>, <em>Scutellaria</em>, <em>Ajuga</em>, &amp;c., with many of <em>Cyperaceae</em> and grasses.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Peat bogs<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-026" href="#sec-07-fn-026" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-026" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, which are of the rarest occurrence in India, are found in depressions of the Nīlgiri and Anaimalai Hills at about 7,000 feet elevation. Their chief constituents are the débris of grasses, sedges, mosses, and rushes. The curious <em>Hedyotis verticillata</em>, found elsewhere only in Ceylon, is characteristic of these bogs, whose surface is covered with a herbaceous vegetation of species of <em>Utricularia</em>, <em>Scrophularineae</em>, <em>Eriocaulon</em>, <em>Xyris</em>, <em>Exacum</em>, <em>Commelinaceae</em>, <em>Lysimachia</em>, &amp;c.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Nīlgiri Hills have been largely and successfully planted with exotic trees of temperate climates, among which the Australian gum-trees and acacias are the most conspicuous.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Laccadive Archipelago.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Laccadive Archipelago<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-027" href="#sec-07-fn-027" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-027" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>, situated in the Arabian Sea 140 miles from the coast of Malabar, consists of coral islets fringed with Coco-nut Palms. Its vegetation is Malayan, with no endemic species. The trees have mostly been introduced by man, as <em>Areca Catechu</em>, <em>Artocarpus integrifolia</em>, <em>Morinda citrifolia</em>, <em>Terminalia Catappa</em>, <em>Eugenia Jambos</em>; others, all littoral, are probably the result of ocean-borne seeds, as <em>Pandanus fascicularis</em>, <em>Hernandia peltata</em>, <em>Pisonia alba</em>, <em>Ochrosia borbonica</em>, <em>Guettarda speciosa</em>, <em>Thespesia populnea</em>. The herbaceous plants are chiefly the weeds of the Western Peninsula. The littoral grass <em>Spinifex</em>, mentioned at p. 193, was probably bird- or wind-borne.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Deccan Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Regarding the whole Peninsula south of the Ganges Valley and east of the Malabar Ghāts as one botanical Region, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-228" data-page="228" data-folio="190" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n227_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 190">[p. 190]</button> it is primarily divisible into two Sub-regions: one, the elevated, usually hilly, sometimes mountainous, plateau terminating eastward more or less abruptly at no great distance from the sea in what are called the Eastern Ghāts, from which the descent is more or less sudden to the low coast land of Coromandel, which forms the other Sub-region. The great plateau may prove to be further transversely divisible into Sub-regions, but so little is accurately known of the distribution of plants over the Deccan that neither the limits nor the botanical characters of such can be satisfactorily described. Such may be—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. The region bounded on the south by the Ajanta hills and Godāvari river, exclusive of the plains of North-east Orissa which belong to the Bengal Sub-region. It thus includes the upper valleys of the Narbadā, Tāpti, Mahānadī, and Godāvari rivers, belonging for the greater part to the political Provinces of Bombay, Central India, the Central Provinces, Berār, Chotā Nāgpur, and Orissa. It is hilly and even mountainous almost throughout, rising in scattered isolated peaks or table-lands to above 4,000 feet, of which the two loftiest are Parasnāth in Hazāribāgh (4,490 feet), and Mahendragiri (4,923 feet) in Ganjām near the sea.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This hilly and mountainous country is for by far the greater part botanically unexplored. It is in the main covered with deciduous-leaved forests, the type of which may be inferred from the following list of forest trees of Singhbhūm in Chotā Nāgpur, drawn up for this Sketch by Mr. H. H. Haines, F.L.S., of the Forest Department.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Singhbhūm is in the zone of deciduous-leaved forest, and lies in the ‘Central Indian Sāl tract.’ With a high temperature in the shade and mountains rising to 3,000 feet with scorched southern slopes and deep damp valleys, its Flora contains representatives of dry hot countries with plants characteristic of the moist tracts of Assam, of some of which latter a list follows this. On rocks often too hot to be touched with the hand are found <em>Euphorbia Nivulia</em>, <em>Sarcostemma</em>, <em>Sterculia urens</em>, <em>Boswellia serrata</em>, and <em>Cochlospermum</em>. The ordinary mixed forest of dry slopes is composed of <em>Anogeissus latifolia</em>, <em>Ougeinia</em>, <em>Odina</em>, <em>Cleistanthus collinus</em>, <em>Zizyphus xylopyra</em>, <em>Buchanania latifolia</em>, and species of <em>Terminalia</em> and <em>Bauhinia</em>. The Sāl varies from a scrubby tree of 30 feet to one of 120 feet high, and is often associated with <em>Adina</em>, <em>Bassia latifolia</em>, <em>Diospyros</em>, <em>Symplocos racemosa</em>, <em>Pterocarpus Marsupium</em>, <em>Eugenia Jambolana</em>, and especially <em>Wendlandia tinctoria</em>. Its common associates elsewhere, <em>Careya arborea</em> and <em>Dillenia</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-229" data-page="229" data-folio="191" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n228_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 191">[p. 191]</button> <em>pentagyna</em>, are here confined to the valleys; but <em>Dillenia aurea</em>, a tree of the Eastern Peninsula and tropical Himālayas, is, curiously, common in places. The Flora of the valleys includes <em>Garcinia Cowa</em>, <em>Amoora Rohituka</em>, <em>Hardwickia binata</em>, <em>Saraca indica</em>, <em>Ficus Roxburghii</em>, <em>Gnetum scandens</em>, <em>Musa sapientum</em> and <em>ornata</em>, and others less interesting. The best-represented woody Orders are the <em>Leguminosae</em>, <em>Rubiaceae</em> (including six species of <em>Gardenia</em> and <em>Randia</em>), <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>, and the <em>Urticaceae</em> (mostly Figs). Of other Orders the grasses number between one and two hundred species, including the Sabai grass (<em>Ischaemum angustifolium</em>) and Spear-grass (<em>Andropogon contortus</em>), which are most abundant; the <em>Cyperaceae</em> about fifty, <em>Compositae</em> fifty, and the <em>Acanthaceae</em> about eleven under-shrubs and twenty-five herbs.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Flora, though mainly that of other parts of the Deccan, presents a few types of the Eastern and Western Himālayas, both tropical and temperate, as epiphytic Orchids, species of <em>Thalictrum</em> and <em>Berberis</em>; and what is more singular, in Chotā Nāgpur a few plants elsewhere confined to the humid districts of Assam and Burma<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-028" href="#sec-07-fn-028" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-028" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, as <em>Michelia Champaca</em>, <em>Dillenia aurea</em>, <em>Dysoxylum procerum</em>, <em>Pygeum acuminatum</em>, <em>Lasianthus laurifolius</em>, <em>Dysophylla Andersoni</em>, <em>Lysimachia peduncularis</em>, <em>Ardisia depressa</em>, <em>Beilschmiedia fagifolia</em>, <em>Litsea nitida</em>, <em>Cyclostemon assamicus</em>, and Tree-ferns.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">2. The Deccan, in a restricted sense the country between the Godāvari and Kistna rivers, a much less mountainous region, including Hyderābād.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">3. Mysore, roughly limited by the Kistna river on the north, and the lower course of the Cauvery on the south. It is more hilly than the Deccan proper, and even mountainous in the Salem District, where the botanically unexplored Shevaroy and Kalrāyan hills attain 5,000 feet elevation.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">4. The small Districts of Madura and Tinnevelly, which form a prolongation of the Coromandel coast terminating at Cape Comorin.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Deccan.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Over the Deccan Region deciduous forests are the most conspicuous feature on the plateau, and comparatively evergreen ones on the coasts and slopes with an eastern aspect. The Teak occurs at intervals nearly over the whole area, but the Sāl, which is common in the north, does not advance beyond the Godāvari on the south, or west of long. 78° E. Much of the open country presents a jungle of small trees <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-230" data-page="230" data-folio="192" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n229_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 192">[p. 192]</button> and shrubs, together with a herbaceous vegetation which is leafless or burnt up in the dry season. In the large river valleys and those of the higher hills, types of the Malabar Flora penetrate far to the east. Of forest trees there are several hundred species, among which <em>Sterculiaceae</em>, <em>Meliaceae</em>, <em>Anacardiaceae</em>, <em>Leguminosae</em>, <em>Combretaceae</em>, <em>Rubiaceae</em>, <em>Bignoniaceae</em>, <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>, and <em>Urticaceae</em> are well represented. The Satin-wood (<em>Chloroxylon Swietenia</em>) and Indian Red-wood (<em>Pterocarpus santalinus</em>) yield the most ornamental Indian timbers; the Tūn (<em>Cedrela Toona</em>) one of the most useful. The odoriferous Sandal-wood (<em>Santalum album</em>) is widely distributed over the southern part of the area, as are the small trees <em>Cochlospermum Gossypium</em>, <em>Butea frondosa</em>, and some species of <em>Bauhinia</em>, all conspicuous in the dry season for their beautiful flowers. Of shrubs, species of <em>Capparis</em>, <em>Grewia</em>, <em>Flacourtia</em>, <em>Zizyphus</em>, <em>Buchanania</em>, <em>Holarrhena</em>, <em>Lagerstroemia</em>, <em>Woodfordia</em>, <em>Azima</em>, <em>Diospyros</em>, <em>Flueggia</em>, and <em>Phyllanthus</em> are very prevalent, often overgrown with climbing <em>Menispermaceae</em>, <em>Malpighiaceae</em>, <em>Toddalia</em>, <em>Cuscuta</em>, <em>Cassytha</em>, <em>Smilax</em>, <em>Dioscorea</em>, <em>Asclepiadeae</em>, <em>Apocyneae</em>, <em>Ampelideae</em>, and <em>Convolvulaceae</em>. In rocky places the columnar <em>Euphorbia neriifolia</em> and <em>tortilis</em> are conspicuous features. The herbaceous vegetation of the Deccan includes most of the common annuals and perennials of Bengal, among which <em>Acanthaceae</em> are notable. <em>Commelinaceae</em> and species of the Labiate genus <em>Leucas</em> are more abundant than in any other Indian Region. Except in the northern districts, <em>Orchideae</em> and <em>Scitamineae</em> are very rare. Of Palms there are <em>Calamus viminalis</em>, <em>Phoenix sylvestris</em>, <em>P. robusta</em>, and <em>Borassus flabellifer</em>, the three latter growing gregariously; and there are besides <em>P. acaulis</em> and <em>P. humilis</em>, both in the northern tract, and an undetermined species grows gregariously in the Shevaroy Hills near Salem. The chief Bamboos are <em>Bambusa arundinacea</em> and <em>Dendrocalamus strictus</em>. Ferns and their allies are very rare, except in the north, where forty-seven species are recorded from Chotā Nāgpur.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The rich black cotton soil that prevails over large areas in the Deccan deserves a special notice, as being characterized by a peculiar assemblage of the indigenous plants of the Region. I am indebted to Mr. Gamble for the following list of its common trees: <em>Capparis divaricata</em>, <em>Acacia arabica</em>, <em>Prosopis spicigera</em>, <em>Parkinsonia aculeata</em>, and <em>Balanites Roxburghii</em>; of shrubs, <em>Cadaba indica</em>, <em>Zizyphus nummularia</em>, <em>Cassia auriculata</em>, <em>Calotropis procera</em>, and <em>Jatropha glandulifera</em>; and of <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-231" data-page="231" data-folio="193" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n230_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 193">[p. 193]</button> herbs, <em>Hibiscus Trionum</em>, <em>Momordica cymbalaria</em>, and <em>Cressa cretica</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Coromandel Sub-region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The narrow strip of low-lying land between the Eastern Ghāts of the Deccan and the sea is dry, hot, and, in many tracts, sandy. Except at the mouths of the many rivers, where Mangroves and other common estuarial trees and shrubs prevail, there is little to break the uniformity of the vegetation, which is of the Deccan type, with a greatly reduced number of species. Thickets of thorny evergreens and deciduous trees and shrubs abound, belonging to the genera <em>Flacourtia</em>, <em>Randia</em>, <em>Scutia</em>, <em>Diospyros</em>, <em>Mimusops</em>, <em>Garcinia</em>, <em>Sapindus</em>, <em>Pterospermum</em>, &amp;c. It is well known for being a favoured district for the production of Nux vomica, Satin-wood, and Ebony (<em>Diospyros Ebenum</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-029" href="#sec-07-fn-029" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-029" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>). Two very peculiar gregarious plants, a Palm and a grass, form impenetrable spinous thickets in sandy soils near the sea; these are <em>Phœnix farinifera</em> and <em>Spinifex squarrosus</em>, of which latter great globular masses become uprooted and are carried by the wind along the shore.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">In the extreme south the Districts of Madura and Tinnevelly, being sheltered from the monsoon by the Palni Hills on the north and Ceylon on the east, are exceptionally hot and arid. The umbrella-shaped <em>Acacia planifrons</em> is confined to these Districts and to North Ceylon, regions which resemble Egypt in the prevalence of <em>Cocculus Leaeba</em> and <em>Capparis aphylla</em>, and in the production of the finest Cotton and the best Indian Sennas (<em>Cassia obovata</em> and <em>angustifolia</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Ceylon Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Ceylon, though so near in position to the Western Peninsula, and presenting so close an affinity to its Flora, as also to those of both Malabar and the Deccan, nevertheless contains so large a proportion of endemic genera and species that it constitutes a separate Region of the Indian Flora. Its botanical features coincide with its physical, the moist mountainous southern and south-western districts having a Flora of the Malabar type, and the hot dry northern districts one of the Coromandel type. It differs from the Malabar Flora in having many more Malayan types.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The number of indigenous Flowering plants in Ceylon is about 2,800 species, under 149 Natural Orders, and that of Ferns and their allies 257 species. <em>Coniferae</em>. Of the above, <em>Cactaceae</em> is the most noteworthy, being represented by the only species of that vast New World Order which is known to be indigenous in the Old World, namely, <em>Rhipsalis Cassytha</em>, also a native of tropical America, Africa, the Mauritius, and Madagascar. The absence in Ceylon of <em>Coniferae</em> and especially of <em>Salicineae</em> is remarkable, <em>Salix tetrasperma</em> being a widely spread Indian shrub or small tree.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-233"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="233" data-folio="195" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n232_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 195">[p. 195]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Ceylon possesses no fewer than twenty-three endemic genera, of which ten, comprising forty-six species (all but two endemic), belong to the typical Malayan Order of <em>Dipterocarpeae</em>, which is represented by only twelve species in the Peninsula. The principal Orders containing very many endemic species are <em>Orchideae</em> seventy-four, <em>Rubiaceae</em> seventy-two, <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> fifty-three, <em>Melastomaceae</em> thirty-eight, and <em>Myrtaceae</em> twenty-six; and of genera <em>Strobilanthes</em>, <em>Eugenia</em>, <em>Memecylon</em>, <em>Phyllanthus</em>, and <em>Hedyotis</em>. The genus <em>Impatiens</em> abounds; upwards of twenty-one species are recorded, nearly all of them endemic. Of other conspicuous Orders, <em>Orchideae</em> contains 160 species, more than half of them endemic. Of Palms, eight indigenous genera, one alone endemic, and eighteen indigenous species, eleven of them endemic, exclusive of the introduced Betel-nut, <em>Borassus</em>, and Coco-nut. The Talipot (<em>Corypha umbraculifera</em>) is one of the most imposing of the Order. The <em>Nipa</em> occurs rarely, Ceylon being its western limit, Australia its eastern. <em>Cycas circinalis</em> is common in the forests. Of <em>Bambuseae</em> there are five genera and ten species (of which four are endemic).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">At elevations above 6,000 feet a few temperate northern genera appear, fewer than might have been expected in mountains that attain heights of upwards of 7,000 and 8,000 feet. Of these genera, <em>Agrimonia</em>, <em>Crawfurdia</em>, and <em>Poterium</em> are not Peninsular. The following are Peninsular: <em>Anemone</em>, <em>Thalictrum</em>, <em>Berberis</em>, <em>Cardamine</em>, <em>Viola</em>, <em>Cerastium</em>, <em>Geranium</em>, <em>Rubus</em>, <em>Potentilla</em>, <em>Alchemilla</em>, <em>Sanicula</em>, <em>Pimpinella</em>, <em>Peucedanum</em>, <em>Galium</em>, <em>Valeriana</em>, <em>Dipsacus</em>, <em>Artemisia</em>, <em>Vaccinium</em>, <em>Gualtheria</em>, <em>Rhododendron</em>, <em>Gentiana</em>, <em>Swertia</em>, <em>Calamintha</em>, <em>Teucrium</em>, <em>Allium</em>. Of Peninsular temperate genera that are absent in Ceylon, <em>Fragaria</em> and <em>Rosa</em> are two the occurrence of which might have been expected, both being Nīlgiri genera.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Remarkable features in the vegetation of Ceylon are the Patanas, grass- or shrub-covered stretches of country, most prevalent in the south-east of the island, from the sea to 5,000 feet altitude. They are partly natural, and partly due to the destruction of the forests and their replacement by subsequently abandoned field-crops. A peculiar, endemic, pale green Bamboo, <em>Ochlandra stridula</em>, so called from the crackling noise caused by treading on its broken stems, covers hundreds of square miles of these Patanas. In grassy places <em>Imperata arundinacea</em> prevails; and in scrub-forests such tropical trees occur as <em>Pterocarpus Marsupium</em>, <em>Careya arborea</em>, <em>Phyllanthus Emblica</em>, <em>Terminalia Bellerica</em> and <em>T. Chebula</em>. <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-234" data-page="234" data-folio="196" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n233_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 196">[p. 196]</button> At higher levels <em>Rhododendron arboreum</em> appears. In moist districts a Fern (<em>Gleichenia linearis</em>) occupies the ground<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-030" href="#sec-07-fn-030" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-030" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Maldive Archipelago.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Maldive Archipelago, a very large group of coral islets with few other flowering plants than Coco-nut Palms, littoral shrubs, and weeds of cultivation, lies about 400 miles SW. of Ceylon, to which the group is politically subordinate. An exhaustive study of their Flora has been published by Messrs. Lewis and Gardiner in the <em>Annals</em> of the Peradenya Botanical Garden, who record 284 species of Flowering plants and Ferns. Of these none are endemic, about 160 are indigenous, ninety-eight cultivated, and twenty-six probably introduced by man. <em>Ardisia humilis</em> and <em>Cladium Mariscus</em> are notable natives of the southern atolls.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Burmese Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Burma is botanically by far the richest Region of British India, and at the same time, as such, the least known. This is due to its great area; to its variety of climates, from a littoral and southern of great humidity to a drier interior, almost arid in places; to its complicated systems of mountain ranges; and to its many geological features and surface soils. The greater portion of Burma having been comparatively recently brought under British rule, very large areas of it are as yet in great part botanically unvisited. This is especially the case with the meridional ranges of Chittagong and Arakan, which extend for 500 miles along the Bay of Bengal, attaining an elevation in parts of 6,000 to 8,000 feet; and with the many continuous or broken, often multiple, ranges bounding Burma on the east from Assam to the Keda State of the Malay Peninsula, which extend for 1,500 miles, and reach even greater elevations. There are also subsidiary longitudinal ranges between the great rivers that have never been botanized, and arid interior areas with little or no rainfall.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Burmese Region, when better known, will probably prove to be botanically divisible into four Sub-regions, before discussing which some general observations on the cardinal features of its whole Flora are necessary. Its recorded species of Flowering plants amount to about 6,000<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-031" href="#sec-07-fn-031" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-031" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>, under 161 Natural <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-235" data-page="235" data-folio="197" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n234_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 197">[p. 197]</button> Orders, of which the following ten are the dominant, the numbers following in brackets showing their corresponding positions in the tropical zone of the Eastern Himālayan Region on the north (p. 168):—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Orchideae</em> (1).</li><li>6. <em>Acanthaceae</em> (10).</li><li>2. <em>Leguminosae</em> (2).</li><li>7. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (6).</li><li>3. <em>Gramineae</em> (3).</li><li>8. <em>Urticaceae</em> (4).</li><li>4. <em>Rubiaceae</em> (7).</li><li>9. <em>Compositae</em> (8).</li><li>5. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> (5).</li><li>10. <em>Scitamineae</em>.</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The proportion of Monocotyledons to Dicotyledons is about 1 to 2·3; of genera to species, 1 to 3·25. One Order alone (<em>Scitamineae</em>) is not in the Sikkim decad. <em>Acanthaceae</em> are relatively much more numerous in Burma, <em>Urticaceae</em> in Sikkim; but these proportions are founded on very insufficient data. Over and above the Orders included in the decad, the following are very largely represented in Burma:—<em>Magnoliaceae</em> (21 sp.), <em>Dipterocarpeae</em> (26 sp.), <em>Begoniaceae</em> (43 sp.), <em>Melastomaceae</em> (57 sp.), <em>Gesneraceae</em> (60 sp.), <em>Asclepiadeae</em> (100 sp.), <em>Cupuliferae</em> (44 sp.), <em>Laurineae</em> (100 sp.), <em>Myristiceae</em> (11 sp.), <em>Coniferae</em> (8 sp.), <em>Cycadeae</em> (3 sp.), <em>Palmeae</em> (68 sp.), <em>Pandaneae</em> (12 sp.), <em>Bambuseae</em> (68 sp.). Balsams and Ferns abound, but data are wanting for even a rude estimate of their numbers; about fifty Balsams have been collected. The Coniferous genera are <em>Cephalotaxus</em>, <em>Taxus</em>, <em>Dacrydium</em>, <em>Libocedrus</em>, <em>Podocarpus</em> (2 sp.), and <em>Pinus</em> (2 sp.). Of <em>Orchideae</em>, 700 species are recorded.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Burma being in the main a forest-clad country, it may be well as an initial step, in sketching what is known of its Flora, to regard it as a whole from this point of view. Fortunately, Kurz in his <em>Forest Flora of British Burma</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-032" href="#sec-07-fn-032" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-032" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> has classified the forests as known to him with remarkable lucidity. I shall therefore, before proceeding to indicate the botanical subdivisions of the Region, summarize his chief results, premising that the northern districts, which are politically in Assam, and which I regard as a Sub-region of Burma, are not included in his work.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The forests are classified by Kurz according as they are evergreen or deciduous, in relation to their elevation above and proximity to or distance from the sea, their climatic and geological <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-236" data-page="236" data-folio="198" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n235_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 198">[p. 198]</button> conditions, and the nature of the soil<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-033" href="#sec-07-fn-033" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-033" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> in which they grow; to which are added observations on their associated plants, shrubby and herbaceous.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1. Evergreen littoral forests. These are estuarial, and of the same character and with the same conditions as the Sundarbans of Bengal. They appear at intervals along the coast, and also on the Andaman Islands.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">2. Evergreen swamp forests occupy the low land and depressions in alluvial plains, and borders of lakes and rivers. They shelter a multitude of trees, shrubs, and herbaceous plants, the most characteristic trees being <em>Mangifera longipes</em> and <em>Xanthophyllum glaucum</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">3. Evergreen tropical forests are the most strongly Malayan; they affect the humid coast districts, and are the special home of Dipterocarps, Palms, and Bamboos. They prevail along the coast range from Chittagong to Tenasserim.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">4. Evergreen hill forests succeed the tropical above 3,000 feet elevation. Oaks and their allies are characteristic, and, if the climate is sufficiently damp, epiphytic Orchids appear. Above 6,000 feet this forest becomes stunted, and in it Rhododendrons (including <em>R. arboreum</em>), <em>Eurya</em>, <em>Rosa</em>, Honeysuckle, dwarf Bamboos, and other temperate shrubs appear, with <em>Anemone</em>, <em>Viola</em>, <em>Gentiana</em>, the common Bracken, Mosses, and Lichens. The crests of the ridges are often crowned with forests of <em>Pinus Khasya</em> towards the north, and of <em>P. Merkusii</em> towards the south.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">5. Open deciduous forests affect diluvial or older alluvial soils. The Eng (<em>Dipterocarpus tuberculatus</em>), whence the name of Eng forest, is the characteristic tree, and there are other species of the same Order, together with a few Oaks, Palms, and Bamboos.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">6. Dry deciduous forests recall the vegetation of the Deccan. <em>Acacia Catechu</em> is characteristic, and gives its name (Cutch) to a special class of forests in which it is the most valuable tree. The soil is usually calcareous.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">7. Mixed deciduous forests are of two classes, an upper and a lower. The upper are restricted to sandstone or metamorphic soils; Teak is the characteristic tree, Palms and Dipterocarps are few, Deccan types are numerous. The lower deciduous forests occupy alluvial soils, and have, consequently, a different undergrowth.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">8. Dune forests are deciduous and mixed; they are littoral, <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-237" data-page="237" data-folio="199" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n236_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 199">[p. 199]</button> occupying calcareous sands and gravels. Species of <em>Erythrina</em>, <em>Bombax</em>, <em>Afzelia</em>, and some estuarial plants occur in them, with <em>Cycas Rumphii</em> and <em>Pandanus fascicularis</em>. <em>Ipomoea biloba</em> and creeping grasses cover the sands.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">9. Bamboo jungles are formed of many species of <em>Bambuseae</em>, often presenting impenetrable thickets and strangling other plants. Seldom are more than two species associated together.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">10. Savannahs are limited tracts of what would be swamp forests had they more trees in them. They are covered with tall coarse grasses, as <em>Saccharum spontaneum</em>, <em>Phragmites Karka</em>, <em>Polytoca barbata</em>, many sedges, and in drier places <em>Imperata arundinacea</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Assuming Burma as a botanical Region to be divisible into four Sub-regions—Northern, Western, Eastern, and Central— these may be outlined as follows :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Northern Burma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Northern Burma<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-034" href="#sec-07-fn-034" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-034" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, a mountainous country extending for 500 miles in a NE. direction from the great bend of the Brahmaputra in Bengal to the Chinese Province of Yunnan. Its northern boundary is the range of mountains flanking the Assam valley on the south. Politically it belongs to Assam ; and its chief districts, beginning at the west, are known as the Gāro, Khāsi, Jaintiā, Nowgong, Nāgā, Pātkai, and Manipur Hills, the direction of all which is from SW. to NE., except in Manipur, where they trend from N. to S. The average height of these hills may be 4,000 to 5,000 feet, with a few peaks rising to above 10,000. The climate is of maximum humidity ; there are no arid areas, as in Central Burma. The vegetation throughout this Sub-region approximates to that of the Eastern Himālayas, differing conspicuously in the absence of an alpine zone, and of any species of <em>Picea</em>, <em>Abies</em>, <em>Tsuga</em>, <em>Larix</em> or <em>Juniperus</em>, and in the presence of <em>Pinus Khasya</em> and of a Pitcher-plant (<em>Nepenthes</em>). From that of Central and Southern Burma it differs in the absence of Teak, in the paucity of Dipterocarps, in the presence of the Nepāl and Sikkim Palm <em>Trachycarpus Martiana</em>, and of Sāl, which finds its eastern limit in the Khāsi Hills, and of <em>Pinus Khasya</em>, which is replaced farther south by <em>P. Merkusii</em>. In the valleys and lower elevations the vegetation of the tropical zone of the Himālayas prevails ; but at elevations above 4,000 feet temperate genera and species mainly replace it, many of them identical with the Himālayan, though maintaining a lower level by 3,000 <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-238" data-page="238" data-folio="200" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n237_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 200">[p. 200]</button> feet or more. Such are, of herbaceous plants, species of <em>Ranunculus</em>, <em>Anemone</em>, <em>Thalictrum</em>, <em>Delphinium</em>, <em>Corydalis</em>, <em>Geranium</em>, <em>Impatiens</em>, <em>Drosera</em>, <em>Astragalus</em>, <em>Rubus</em>, <em>Potentilla</em>, <em>Fragaria</em>, <em>Sanguisorba</em>, <em>Astilbe</em>, <em>Parnassia</em>, <em>Valeriana</em>, <em>Senecio</em>, <em>Pedicularis</em>, <em>Primula</em>, <em>Tofieldia</em>, <em>Iris</em>, <em>Allium</em>, and <em>Paris</em>. Of temperate shrubs, species of <em>Berberis</em>, <em>Cleyera</em>, <em>Camellia</em>, <em>Eurya</em>, <em>Saurauja</em>, <em>Ilex</em>, <em>Neillia</em>, <em>Luculia</em>, <em>Viburnum</em>, <em>Ligustrum</em>, <em>Rhododendron</em>, <em>Vaccinium</em>, <em>Gaultheria</em>, and many others of Himālayan type. Of trees at the high level, among the most conspicuous are <em>Rhododendron arboreum</em>, species of <em>Magnolia</em>, <em>Manglietia</em>, and <em>Michelia</em>, <em>Acer</em>, <em>Prunus</em>, <em>Pyrus</em>, <em>Pieris</em>, <em>Bucklandia</em>, <em>Alnus</em>, <em>Betula</em>, <em>Carpinus</em>, <em>Quercus</em> (twenty species), <em>Taxus</em>, and <em>Pinus Khasya</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">A conspicuous feature of the western district of this Sub-region is the open unforested character of elevations above 4,000 feet, reminiscent of the Nīlgiri Hills and presenting genera of trees, shrubs, and herbs common to that far-distant region (see p. 188). It is in one spot of a few yards wide alone in all Burma<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-035" href="#sec-07-fn-035" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-035" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> (in the Jaintiā Hills) that the Pitcher-plant (<em>Nepenthes khasiana</em>) is found growing prostrate among wet grass and stones, at an elevation of about 4,000 feet. It is the northernmost known member of this singular genus, its British Indian congeners being natives of Ceylon and the Malayan Peninsula, the latter 1,500 miles to the southward, and they are all climbers. It has not been found elsewhere.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">To the eastward the loftier districts of this Sub-region are forest-clad, Bamboos often replacing the arboreous vegetation and dominating the scenery. It is a singular fact that Manipur, the most distant of the districts from Sikkim, possesses species of the latter country not hitherto found elsewhere in Burma.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Western and Southern Burma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The botany of the humid strip of land between the sea and the crests of the Chittagong and Arakan hills differs from that of Central Burma, being interrupted by the estuarial Flora of the deltas of the Irrawaddy, Sittang, and other rivers, eastward of which it reappears along the Tenasserim coast to Mergui. Though many collections have been made in different parts of it<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-036" href="#sec-07-fn-036" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-036" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>, these do not suffice to supply a decad of its dominant <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-239" data-page="239" data-folio="201" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n238_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 201">[p. 201]</button> Natural Orders. The nature of its tree vegetation may be gathered from Kurz's classification given above, which is that of a dense evergreen forest where Dipterocarps (twenty-six species), Oaks, and Bamboos are conspicuous features, some of the first of these towering over all other trees. Ferns, scandent Palms, and Orchids abound, of which latter Orders novelties are still being sent to botanical establishments in India and Europe. The general character of the vegetation may be gathered from Nos. 3 and 4 of Kurz's classification of the Burmese forests (p. 198). In detail it may be supposed to contain many plants of the temperate tracts of the North Burmese Sub-region.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Eastern Burma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Flora of the complicated ranges of mountains intervening between Burma and China on the north and Siam on the south is all but unknown. They have been visited by few botanists or collectors, and their very limited collections throw little light on the interesting question of the community or diversity of the border Floras of these three countries. Only four such collections are known to me: Lieut. Pottinger's in the Kachin Hills, Sir H. Collett's in the Shan States, and those of the Rev. C. Parish and of T. Lobb in the Tenasserim Hills.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Lieutenant Pottinger's collections were made in 1897 at elevations of 450 to 7,000 feet in a mountainous country in the extreme north-east of Central Burma, between lat. 25° and 27° N., and long. 97° and 99° E.: that is, a little to the eastward of the Hukawng valley by which Griffith entered Central Burma in 1836. They were made under great difficulties, owing to the climate and to the hostility of the natives, so that a large proportion of them were lost. In Major Prain's account<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-037" href="#sec-07-fn-037" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-037" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> of this collection he records 601 Flowering plants and twenty-seven Ferns and their allies, of the former of which forty-one are endemic new species, including a new genus of <em>Leguminosae</em> (<em>Cruddasia insignis</em>), and another allied to <em>Escallonia</em>. Not a single Oak is on the list, which is very remarkable (the specimens were presumably lost). Teak here finds its northern limit in Burma (the trees of it were gnarled); as does <em>Shorea siamensis</em>, the only Dipterocarp on the list. The Tea plant was found throughout the route. Only four species of <em>Impatiens</em> were collected, but many were seen. Of the few plants hitherto supposed to be endemic in China, but now found also in Burma, the most conspicuous were <em>Wistaria chinensis</em>, <em>Rhododendron indicum</em>, and <em>Gelsemium elegans</em>, which latter <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-240" data-page="240" data-folio="202" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n239_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 202">[p. 202]</button> is not included in the Flora of British India, although it had been found in Assam. The following are the ten dominant Orders, with their approximate number of species in each :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1. <em>Orchideae</em>, 77. 2. <em>Leguminosae</em>, 60. 3. <em>Acanthaceae</em>, 26. 4. <em>Rubiaceae</em>, 25. 5. <em>Labiatae</em>, 20. — 6. <em>Urticaceae</em>, 20. 7. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>, 18. 8. <em>Compositae</em>, 17. 9. <em>Scitamineae</em>, 17. 10. <em>Aroideae</em>, 17.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The contrast between the Kachin collection and that made by Sir H. Collett only some 200 miles to the southward in about the same longitude, and for the most part at similar elevations, is startling, and can only be accounted for by assuming that in both cases the number of species collected was too small for an instructive comparison. Of Pottinger's 601 Flowering plants not 100 are recorded among Collett's 843.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Sir H. Collett's collections were made in 1887–8 in Upper Burma and the Southern Shan States : that is, in the valleys of the Salween and Sittang rivers, lat. 19½° to 21½° N., long. 96 to 97½° E. ; and on Popa (4,000 feet high) in the Irrawaddy valley. They contain 843 species of Flowering plants, which have been enumerated by himself and Mr. Hemsley in the <em>Journal of the Linnean Society</em><sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-038" href="#sec-07-fn-038" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-038" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, where no fewer than eighty-three are described as new and endemic. The most remarkable of the novelties were <em>Osteomeles anthyllidifolia</em>, a Rosaceous shrub resembling the Blackthorn, previously known as a native of China and of some Pacific Islands, all whose congeners are Andean ; a Rose and a Honeysuckle (<em>Rosa gigantea</em> and <em>Lonicera Hildebrandiana</em>), both with flowers of extraordinary size, and a new genus of <em>Leguminosae</em> (<em>Neocollettia</em>). The collection contained nine Oaks.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Though so near the Chinese frontier, few species of that country were added to the Burmese Flora ; the most interesting was a species of <em>Speranskia</em>, a monotypic Chinese Euphorbiaceous genus. About twenty-five of the species and one-fifth of the genera are British.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The following are the ten dominant Orders in Sir H. Collett's collection, with the approximate number of species in each :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">1. <em>Leguminosae</em>, 90. 2. <em>Gramineae</em>, 80. 3. <em>Compositae</em>, 60. 4. <em>Labiatae</em>, 41. 5. <em>Rubiaceae</em>, 30. — 6. <em>Acanthaceae</em>, 30. 7. <em>Convolvulaceae</em>, 29. 8. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em>, 25. 9. <em>Orchideae</em>, 22. 10. <em>Verbenaceae</em>, 21.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-241"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="241" data-folio="203" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n240_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 203">[p. 203]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The collection, having been made at various elevations between 3,000 and 12,000 feet, is one of mixed tropical and temperate types, the latter descending as low as 4,000 feet, according to Sir H. Collett's observation. The most salient features of the collection are the fewness of <em>Orchideae</em> and the large number of <em>Compositae</em>; but very many Orchids have since been found in the same collecting ground, chiefly around Fort Stedman and the Tale Lake. <em>Tectona Hamiltoniana</em> is included, but not the true Teak (<em>T. grandis</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Rev. C. Parish's collection, and that of Thomas Lobb, a collector for Messrs. Veitch, are chiefly Orchids, of which many interesting species were discovered in the Mulyet and Mokai mountains (altitude 5,000 and 6,300 feet) in Tenasserim. The existence of a <em>Nepenthes</em> in Mergui is alluded to on p. 200 (footnote). A more remarkable discovery, if confirmed, is that of a <em>Rafflesia</em> in the pass of Ta-ok, east of Moulmein, at an elevation of 3,000 feet, recorded by Mr. Theobald in Mason's <em>Burma</em>, vol. ii, p. 828.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Central Burma.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Central Burma, between the Arakan ranges and those east of the Sittang river, is divisible into two Sub-regions, a northern dry, and a southern humid, but in what latitude the change of climate becomes marked by the vegetation is not determined ; it is, no doubt, irregular in its course, and influenced by the great rivers, by the hills between these, and by proximity to the Arakan range which exhausts the moisture of the south-west monsoon. One small district, that of Minbu, between the Irrawaddy and Arakan, in lat. 20° N., is spoken of as being a desert, no doubt figuratively<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-039" href="#sec-07-fn-039" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-039" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. The character of the upper Central vegetation is largely that of the drier parts of Western India ; it agrees with that of No. 6 in Kurz's classification (p. 198) in being mainly dry deciduous. Two species of Teak (<em>Tectona grandis</em> and <em>T. Hamiltoniana</em>) occur, with many Leguminous trees, <em>Acacia Catechu</em> often forming forests, and more rarely <em>Dipterocarpus tuberculatus</em>.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Andaman Islands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Andaman Islands have been visited by botanists at few and distant points only, of which the chief is Port Blair. Kurz collected in them 560 species of Flowering plants<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-040" href="#sec-07-fn-040" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-040" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup>; and Major <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-242" data-page="242" data-folio="204" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n241_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 204">[p. 204]</button> Prain, who visited several of the smaller islets, Barren Island, Narcondam, and the Coco, as well as one of the main islands, added perhaps 150 to Kurz's record. The total would probably not exceed one-third of the whole Flora. The forests with which most of the Archipelago is clad are typically Burmese, and are regarded by Kurz as a less developed stage of his seventh class, mixed evergreen and deciduous. Their most remarkable feature is the apparently total absence of the Cupuliferous genera <em>Quercus</em> and <em>Castanopsis</em>, of the first of which there are forty species on the neighbouring continent, and of the second eleven. Of <em>Dipterocarpeae</em> there are very few, but owing to the number of their individuals and gigantic stature they form the dominant feature of the forests; of <em>Myristica</em> there are four. Considering, however, that nothing is known of the vegetation over nearly the whole of the Archipelago, and that its interior hills, which reach 2,400 feet in altitude, have not been botanized over, it is evident that it would be premature to regard the apparent absence of <em>Cupuliferae</em> as ascertained fact, or indeed that of any of such other desiderata as Mr. Kurz indicates, namely <em>Magnoliaceae</em>, <em>Onagraceae</em>, <em>Umbelliferae</em>, <em>Vacciniaceae</em>, <em>Scrophularineae</em>, <em>Labiatae</em>, <em>Polygoneae</em>, <em>Amarantaceae</em>, <em>Chenopodiaceae</em>, <em>Coniferae</em>, <em>Pontederiaceae</em>, <em>Amaryllideae</em>, and other small families, and all fresh-water plants. One Coniferous plant (<em>Podocarpus</em>) is included in his <em>Forest Flora</em> as a native of the Andamans, and others of the desiderata have, since the publication of that work, been found by Major Prain.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Mr. E. M. Buchanan, of the Forest Department, informs me that the absence of grasses like <em>Saccharum</em>, <em>Panicum</em>, and <em>Imperata</em>, and of all indigenous erect Bamboos except <em>B. schizostachyoides</em>, is a peculiar feature of the Andaman vegetation, and that their belts of pine forest on silty deposits facing the sea seem to have no parallel in Burma or the Nicobars.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Nicobar Islands.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Nicobar Islands are even less known botanically than the Andamans, and it is questionable whether they belong to the Burmese or to the Malay Peninsular Flora. They were visited both by Mr. Kurz<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-041" href="#sec-07-fn-041" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-041" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>, who collected on Katchall and Camorta 573 Flowering plants and fifty Ferns and their allies, and by Major Prain, who obtained 110<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-042" href="#sec-07-fn-042" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-042" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> on the islets of Car Nicobar, Batti Malv, and a few other localities, adding considerably to his predecessor's list. The general character of their vegetation is that of the Andamans, with the apparent <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-243" data-page="243" data-folio="205" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n242_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 205">[p. 205]</button> absence of <em>Dipterocarpeae</em>, <em>Datiscaceae</em>, and the <em>Podocarpus</em>. The presence in the Nicobars of a genus of <em>Monimiaceae</em>, an Order elsewhere in British India confined to the Malayan Peninsula and Ceylon, would indicate a closer affinity with those Regions. On the other hand, the occurrence of <em>Capparis ambigua</em>, a plant confined to the two Archipelagoes, indicates a community in their Floras. It would be interesting to know whether any species of <em>Nepenthes</em>, of which eight are found in the Malay Peninsula, exist in the Nicobars.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Malayan Peninsula Region.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">Except the Island of Penang, and the Protected States of Perak, Selangor, and the British territories of Wellesley, Malacca, and Singapore, little is known of the Flora of the Malay Peninsula, the greater part of which is under Siamese dominion. Of the range of mountains which forms its backbone and which rises to peaks 4,000 to 7,000 feet in height, a few have been visited botanically, including one (7,000 feet) in Perak, and Mt. Ophir (4,183 feet) in Malacca. It is hence obvious that materials do not exist for estimating with any approach to finality either the Flora as a whole or the relative number of its dominant Natural Orders.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Except where cultivation interferes, the whole Peninsula is clothed with an evergreen vegetation, that of the shore being estuarial. Mr. Ridley informs me that the number of recorded species of Flowering plants from this Region in the rich herbarium of the Royal Gardens at Singapore is 4,547, and of Ferns and their allies 368; but this does not include many species discovered by collectors sent from the Royal Gardens, Calcutta, which are in course of publication<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-043" href="#sec-07-fn-043" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-043" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> by Sir George King. The ten dominant Orders are, as given me by Mr. Ridley, the following, to which I have added in brackets their corresponding position in the Burmese decad:—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Orchideae</em> (1).</li><li>2. <em>Leguminosae</em> (2).</li><li>3. <em>Euphorbiaceae</em> (5).</li><li>4. <em>Rubiaceae</em> (4).</li><li>5. <em>Anonaceae</em>.</li><li>6. <em>Gramineae</em> (3).</li><li>7. <em>Scitamineae</em> (10).</li><li>8. <em>Melastomaceae</em>.</li><li>9. <em>Cyperaceae</em>.</li><li>10. <em>Urticaceae</em> (8).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The proportion of Monocotyledons to Dicotyledons is 1 to 2·2, and of genera to species 1 to 2·3. One species is the most singular of all known Balsams (<em>I. mirabilis</em>), with a simple or branched trunk often four feet high and as thick as a man's leg. It is a native of the small islet of Terutan, one of the Lankauwi group, and of the adjacent coast of West Siam.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The Malayan Peninsula is much richer in Palms than is any other region of British India. Upwards of thirty genera and 150 species are recorded, and these numbers will no doubt be exceeded in the forthcoming history of Indian Palms which is about to appear from the pen and pencil of Dr. Beccari of Florence, in the <em>Annals</em> of the Royal Botanic Garden, Calcutta. Both generically and specifically the Malayan Palms differ from those of Burma, and find their affinities with those of Sumatra and other islands of the Eastern Archipelago. To this, however, there appears to be one remarkable exception, for Mr. Ridley informs me by letter that an undescribed species of <em>Borassus</em>, an essentially African genus, of which one species is commonly cultivated all over the plains of India, has been recently found in the forests of Perak.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of Bamboos fewer Malayan species have been recorded than of Burmese, but more than in the Malabar region.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-245"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="245" data-folio="207" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n244_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 207">[p. 207]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">About twenty-two species, under five genera, are known, most of them endemic or common to the adjacent islands. Of Conifers eight species are recorded for the Malayan Peninsula, belonging to the genera <em>Agathis</em>, <em>Dacrydium</em>, and <em>Podocarpus</em> (five species); of <em>Cycadeae</em>, only <em>Cycas Rumphii</em>. The small Order <em>Monimiaceae</em>, of which two species are natives of the Malay Peninsula, is unknown in Burma (though inhabiting the Nicobars), but is represented by two species in Ceylon. The most notable plants of the Peninsula are perhaps two species of the stemless and leafless root-parasites, <em>Cytinaceae</em>, namely, <em>Brugmansia Lowi</em>, and a <em>Rafflesia</em> probably identical with the famous <em>R. Arnoldi</em> of Sumatra; and seven species of the Pitcher-plant (<em>Nepenthes</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Penang Islet.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The islet of Penang, lying opposite the coast of Wellesley, distant ten miles, demands a separate notice, if only for the fact of the astonishing number of species, and so many of them arboreous, that it contains. Though its area is only 106 square miles, and its greatest elevation 2,750 feet, yet, according to a catalogue of its Flowering plants drawn up and printed (1894?) by Mr. C. Curtis, F.L.S., Assistant Superintendent of its Forests, it contains 1,813 species, together with 173 Ferns and their allies. With the exception of one, the tenth (<em>Apocyneae</em> replacing <em>Scitamineae</em>), the ten dominant Orders are the same as those I have given as the decad of the whole Peninsula, but they do not follow the same sequence, <em>Rubiaceae</em> ranking first, <em>Leguminosae</em> fourth, and <em>Orchideae</em> third; and the proportion of Monocotyledons to Dicotyledons is very different, 1 to 4·1. Other Orders largely represented are <em>Myrtaceae</em> thirty-five, <em>Acanthaceae</em> thirty-one, <em>Dipterocarpeae</em> twenty-eight, <em>Myristicaceae</em> and <em>Guttiferae</em> twenty-five each, <em>Cupuliferae</em> seventeen (of which thirteen are Oaks), Palms thirty-four. Of <em>Compositae</em> there are only twenty-three, of Bamboos one only, of <em>Nepenthes</em> three, of Gymnosperms eight (<em>Gnetum</em> four, a <em>Dacrydium</em>, <em>Agathis</em>, and two <em>Podocarpi</em>).</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">Cocos and Keeling islets.</aside></div><p class="prose-p">The Cocos and Keeling islets, in the South Indian Ocean, 500 miles S.W. of Java, and still more distant from Singapore, are British possessions, which have in recent years been transferred from the Government of Ceylon to that of the Federated Malay States. Their scanty Flora is purely tropical Indian, and in great part littoral. Mr. H. B. Guppy spent ten weeks there, and made a very interesting collection of seeds and fruits cast up on the shores of Keeling's Island, of which a list has been published by Mr. W. B. Hemsley, F.R.S. (See <em>Nature</em>, xli. 491, 492; and <em>Science Progress</em>, i. 40.)</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-246"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="246" data-folio="208" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n245_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 208">[p. 208]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX A</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>The Kurram Valley</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">The Kurram Valley.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> Kurram is the only valley in the long range of mountains bordering British India on the west of the Indus of which the Flora has been described. It was exhaustively explored by Surgeon-Major J. E. T. Aitchison, F.R.S., in 1879-80, who collected in it about 900 species of Flowering plants, and twenty-six Ferns and their allies<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-044" href="#sec-07-fn-044" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-044" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup>. The valley is nearly 100 miles long, and the elevations at which the collections were made varied from 2,000 feet above the sea at the mouth of the valley to 15,000 feet on its flanks: that is, from the tropical Flora of the Indus Plain to the alpine of the Himālayas. Of the ninety-three Orders to which the Flowering plants belong, the following ten are the dominant. The added numbers in brackets indicate the positions of the same in the Western Himālayan Region :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Compositae</em> (2).</li><li>6. <em>Cruciferae</em> (8).</li><li>2. <em>Gramineae</em> (1).</li><li>7. <em>Umbelliferae.</em></li><li>3. <em>Leguminosae</em> (3).</li><li>8. <em>Ranunculaceae</em> (6).</li><li>4. <em>Labiatae</em> (5).</li><li>9. <em>Boragineae.</em></li><li>5. <em>Rosaceae</em> (9).</li><li>10. <em>Cyperaceae</em> (4).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The low position of <em>Cyperaceae</em>, and the subjection of <em>Gramineae</em> to <em>Compositae</em>, may be attributed to the dryness of the climate, as also may the prevalence of <em>Boragineae</em>. The genera and most of the species are Himālayan, the chief exceptions being such plants as <em>Herniaria</em> and <em>Peganum</em>, which are typical of the Indus Plain. The nine <em>Coniferae</em>, which include the Deodār, are all Himālayan. The only Palm is <em>Nannorrhops Ritchieana</em>, which extends from the Salt Range to Sind and Baluchistān. The distinctively Oriental genera, such as <em>Pistacia</em> and <em>Eremurus</em>, are few. Of <em>Bambuseae</em> there are none. On the whole, the Flora may be regarded as a dyingout Himālayan, and not as typically Oriental. It differs considerably from that of British Baluchistān.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-247"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="247" data-folio="209" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n246_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 209">[p. 209]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">APPENDIX B</h2><h3 class="sec-h2"><em>British Baluchistān</em></h3><div class="prose-unit"><div class="sidenote-stack"><aside class="shoulder-sidenote">British Baluchistān.</aside></div><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">The</span> small tract of country (about 180 miles long) bearing this name is enclosed between Afghānistān and Baluchistān proper. It is mountainous, 5,000 to 8,000 feet elevation above the sea, with peaks rising above 10,000 feet, and its climate is one of great extremes of cold and heat. Not having been brought under British administration until the early volumes of the <em>Flora of British India</em> were considerably advanced, its plants were not taken up in that work.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Only three botanists have collected in it. Griffith<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-045" href="#sec-07-fn-045" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-045" role="doc-noteref">1</a></sup> in the spring of 1839 passed through it when accompanying the army of the Indus from Shikārpur to Kandahār, Dr. Stocks<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-046" href="#sec-07-fn-046" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-046" role="doc-noteref">2</a></sup> visited it in the spring of 1850, and Mr. J. H. Lace, F.L.S. (of the Indian Forest Department), resided in it from 1885 to 1888. Some of the plants collected by the first two travellers are included in Boissier's <em>Flora Orientalis</em>, and excellent observations on the vegetation of the country are published in the works cited below. Mr. Lace alone was enabled to make a detailed botanical exploration of the district. He collected upwards of 700 species, which, aided by Mr. Hemsley, he has published<sup class="fn-ref"><a id="ref-sec-07-fn-047" href="#sec-07-fn-047" data-fn-target="sec-07-fn-047" role="doc-noteref">3</a></sup>, prefacing his account with an exhaustive description of the botanical features of the country. These materials prove the Flora to be Oriental, with an admixture of Himālayan and Indian plants; it is Afghān, in short, and very different from that of the Indus Plain, and of Baluchistān in its lower levels, which is more Arabic-Persian. It may be gathered from the observations of these three botanists that the vegetation, though poor, is very varied and presents many local assemblages of species, dependent upon climate, soil (including saline), humidity, and elevation within a very limited area. The ten dominant Orders of the eighty-two in Mr. Lace's <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-248" data-page="248" data-folio="210" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/imperialgazette01hunt/page/n247_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 210">[p. 210]</button> collections are the following, with, in brackets, their relative positions in the Kurram Valley Flora :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><ul class="editorial-list"><li>1. <em>Compositae</em> (1).</li><li>6. <em>Chenopodiaceae</em>.</li><li>2. <em>Gramineae</em> (2).</li><li>7. <em>Boragineae</em> (9).</li><li>3. <em>Leguminosae</em> (3).</li><li>8. <em>Liliaceae</em>.</li><li>4. <em>Cruciferae</em> (6).</li><li>9. <em>Caryophylleae</em>.</li><li>5. <em>Labiatae</em> (4).</li><li>10. <em>Rosaceae</em> (5).</li></ul></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Of the eighty-two Orders, no fewer than twenty are monotypic, including <em>Acanthaceae</em>, which is well represented in every other botanical Region of India. The forest trees are <em>Juniperus macropoda</em> (the only Conifer), which is found at 7,000 to 10,000 feet elevation; <em>Populus euphratica</em> and species of <em>Pistacia</em>, <em>Dalbergia</em>, <em>Celtis</em>, <em>Acacia</em>, <em>Prosopis</em>, <em>Salix</em>, <em>Fraxinus</em>, <em>Ulmus</em>, <em>Crataegus</em>, and <em>Tamarix</em>. (The Oriental Plane included in Mr. Lace’s list I assume to have been introduced.) The Pomegranate and Fig are indigenous. <em>Nannorrhops</em> is the only Palm. There are no <em>Bambuseae</em> in the collections, and only six Ferns.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Before concluding, it must be recorded that the very considerable European and Oriental Order of <em>Cistineae</em> has its extreme eastern limit in Native Baluchistān. Stocks collected <em>Helianthemum Lippii</em>, a widely distributed species extending from the Levant to Persia, near Gandāva, sixty miles south of the British frontier. Should it be found within the latter it would add a Natural Order of plants to British India.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">J. D.
