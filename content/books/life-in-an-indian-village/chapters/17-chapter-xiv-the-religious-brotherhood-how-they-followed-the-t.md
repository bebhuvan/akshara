In a neatly built room, in the middle of the village, surrounded on all sides by a flower garden, is the meeting place of this religious association, which in Tamil is called <em>Bajanakutam</em>. At the entrance over the gateway is represented the trident mark of the Vishnava sect with the figures of Garuda and Hanuman on either side. Inside, on the walls all round, are hung pictures representing the different incarnations of Vishnu, and on the floor are seen scattered in confusion various musical instruments. Here in this room, consecrated to Vishnu, the members of the <em>Bajanakutam</em> <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-201" data-page="201" data-folio="193" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n200_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 193">[p. 193]</button> meet for worship. The day on which the festival described in my last paper took place, was a gala day with the people of the association. All the members were present on the occasion, and followed the idol to the top of the hill, and in the evening when it was taken down in procession they displayed unusual interest in their work and did their utmost to please the people. They were all dressed alike, with sandal abundantly smeared over their bodies. Their hind-locks were profusely decked with flowers. The chief man of the association, who is also the most intelligent, had what is called a <em>tambour</em> resting on his right shoulder, and he dexterously passed his forefinger over the steel wires of the instrument, while in his left hand he had two small pieces of wood with iron rings and small bells attached. The sound produced by striking the two pieces of wood against each other, blending with the tinkling of the small bells, produced a curious effect. This musician had, besides, strings of small bells tied round his legs. Thus accoutred, he sang in a loud voice and also danced, with both his hands engaged. Another member of the <em>Bajanakutam</em> played the drum, while a <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-202" data-page="202" data-folio="194" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n201_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 194">[p. 194]</button> third played the fiddle. Two or three had small circular shaped metallic instruments which when struck against each other sent forth sharp shrill sounds. To the accompaniment of these musical instruments the members of the association sang sacred songs when following the idol. There were hundreds of people near the singing party who seemed to thoroughly enter into the spirit of what was sung. Their faces beamed with joy, and some actually danced and clapped their hands. It may interest my readers to have translations of one or two of the songs sung on the occasion to which I refer. It may be well that I should explain briefly the various allusions in the latter. The third stanza refers to the story of Gajanthra having once gone to drink water in a channel and been caught by a crocodile. For several days the elephant had neither food nor water, and his life was in imminent danger. At last when he had no strength to extricate himself from his perilous position and was about to be dragged into the <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-205" data-page="205" data-folio="197" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n204_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 197">[p. 197]</button> water, he thought of Vishnu. It is said that Vishnu immediately appeared and saved him.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The allusion in the next stanza is to the story which appears in the <em>Mahabaratha</em>, one of the two great national epics of the Hindus. Duriyodhana of the Kurus wanted to insult the chaste Draupatha, of the Pandus, before a large assembly, and with this object he directed that she should be stripped of her cloth. Draupatha thought of Vishnu, and thereupon the cloth which she was wearing went on increasing in length. The result was that the person charged with the duty of stripping grew tired, and Draupatha was saved.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The next stanza refers to one of the most popular stories in India. Prakalath was the son of Hirania, who tried to dissuade him from owning Vishnu as his god by punishing him in various ways. But Prakalath would not renounce Vishnu, and in the end was saved, while Hirania was killed by Vishnu himself.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The story of Krishna lifting the hill of Govarthanagiri to save the shepherds will be found fully related in my last chapter.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is said that Valmiki, the renowned author of the <em>Ramayana</em>, was at first an illiterate <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-206" data-page="206" data-folio="198" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n205_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 198">[p. 198]</button> hunter, but that by having been taught constantly to utter the word <em>Rama</em> he came to understand the divine truths, and afterwards by inspiration wrote the grand epic.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">It is said that Siva once taught his wife about the greatness of Vishnu. It is believed that Kabir, a Muhammadan, and Rama Doss, a Hindu, were in very recent times (in the present <em>kaliyuga</em>) saved by Vishnu for believing in him even at the risk of their lives.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The above stories are known to the most ignorant villager, so that the references to them in the pieces sung by the members of the <em>Bajanakutam</em> were highly appreciated. Also the references made in the first piece to the ass that bears the <em>kunguma</em> flower, the lame man that wishes to reach the honeycomb on the tree, and the frog that is unable to extract the honey from the lotus, are so well known that every one understands them. In fact, there are proverbial sayings founded on them which every Hindu is able to quote. Further, the language in which the above lyrics and all similar lyrics are expressed is very plain and simple. Although they are written with full regard for the rules of Tamil <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-207" data-page="207" data-folio="199" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n206_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 199">[p. 199]</button> prosody, they are nevertheless easily understood, as they are intended for the masses. There is still another peculiar characteristic to be found in these pieces, and that is the reference made to Siva, who, it is said, has acknowledged the superiority of Vishnu. The Saivites do not admit this, and the Saivites and Vishnuvites hate each other most intensely.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The members of the <em>Bajanakutam</em> honour and respect one another greatly. When two members meet, they fall at each other's feet and exchange kind words. At these times they use a language peculiar to themselves, and which is called in Tamil <em>Paribasha</em>. In this article and the previous one I have given a brief account of the great feast of <em>Pongal</em>, which to Hindus is as important as Christmas is to Christians, and of the religious association existing in Kélambakam, the members of which follow the village idol and sing sacred lyrics.</p></div><section class="apparatus-zone" aria-label="Footnotes"><h2 class="apparatus-heading">Critical Apparatus &amp; Notes</h2><ol class="apparatus-list"><li id="sec-17-fn-001" class="apparatus-item" role="doc-endnote"><a class="fn-backlink" href="#ref-sec-17-fn-001" aria-label="Return to text">1.</a> <span class="fn-body">A costly flower used in Indian medicines.</span></li></ol></section>
