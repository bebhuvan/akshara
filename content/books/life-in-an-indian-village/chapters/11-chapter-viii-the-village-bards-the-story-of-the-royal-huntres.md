<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-119"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="119" data-folio="111" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n118_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 111">[p. 111]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">VIII.</h2><h3 class="sec-h2">The village bards—The story of the royal huntress.</h3><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Macaulay</span> says : “ The Greek Rhapsodists, according to Plato, could scarce recite Homer without falling into convulsions. The Mohawk hardly feels the scalping knife while he shouts his death song. The power which the ancient bards of Wales and Germany exercised over their auditors seems to modern readers almost miraculous.” The above remark applies with equal force to the Indian bards, who go about in villages reciting tales. The power exercised by them over the villagers is simply marvellous. I once witnessed two bards reciting a tale to the people of Kélambakam, wherein the adventures of a royal prince, his adversity, his banishment from the land of his fathers, his love for a huntress, and his ultimate marriage with her, were all graphically described. The following <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-120" data-page="120" data-folio="112" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n119_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 112">[p. 112]</button> is the story. It first describes the land over which a good king ruled.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">It was a land of plenty and of wealth ;<br>
There God's indulgent hand made for a race<br>
Supremely blest a paradise on earth.<br>
A land of virtue, truth, and charity,<br>
Where nature's choicest treasures man enjoyed<br>
With little toil, where youth respected age,<br>
Where each his neighbour's wife his sister deemed,<br>
Where side by side the tiger and the lamb<br>
The water drank, and sported oft in mirth.<br>
A land where each man deemed him highly blest<br>
When he relieved the mis'ries of the poor,<br>
When to his roof the wearied traveller came<br>
To share his proffered bounty with good cheer.<br>
Such was the far famed land of Panchala.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The good king is then described in the following lines.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">Here reigned a king who walked in virtue's path,<br>
Who ruled his country only for his God.<br>
His people's good he deemed his only care,<br>
Their sorrows were his sorrows, and their joys<br>
He counted as his own ; such was the king<br>
Whose daily prayers went up to Him on high<br>
For wisdom and for strength to rule his men<br>
Aright, and guard the land from foreign foes.<br>
Such was the far famed king of Panchala.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This good king had a son who is next spoken of in the tale.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-121"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="121" data-folio="113" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n120_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 113">[p. 113]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">An only son he had—a noble prince,<br>
The terror of his foes, the poor man's friend.<br>
He mastered all the arts of peace and war,<br>
And was a worthy father's worthy son.<br>
What gifts and graces men as beauties deem,<br>
These nature freely lavished on the youth,<br>
And people loved in wonder to behold<br>
The face that kindled pleasure in their minds.<br>
The courage of a warrior in the field,<br>
A woman's tender pity to the weak,<br>
All these were centred in the royal youth.<br>
His arrows killed full many a beast that wrought<br>
Dread havoc on the cattle of the poor.<br>
Such was the famous prince of Panchala.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">Then follows an account of the good people of the country. They go to their king and complain to him of a ferocious tiger.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">The people, they were all true men and good,<br>
Their ruler they adored, for by their God<br>
He was ordained to rule their native land.<br>
They freely to their king made known their wants,<br>
And he as freely satisfied their needs,<br>
And e'en the meanest of the land deemed it<br>
The basest act to sin against his king.<br>
Such were the people of the ancient land<br>
Of Panchala, who stood one day with tears<br>
Before their king to pour their plaintive tales<br>
Of ruin wrought upon their cattle by<br>
The tiger of the forest, that all day<br>
Was safe in his impenetrable lair,<br>
But every night his dreaded figure showed<br>
And feasted on the flesh of toiling beasts.</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-122"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="122" data-folio="114" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n121_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 114">[p. 114]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The king at once commands his son to go to the forest and kill the beast.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">The king gave ear to their sad tales of woe,<br>
And straightway called his only son, and said—<br>
"Dear son! my people's good I value more<br>
Than thine own life. Go therefore to the woods<br>
With all thine arrows and thy trusty bow,<br>
And drag the dreaded tiger from his den,<br>
And to their homes their wonted peace restore.<br>
His spotted skin and murderous claws must soon<br>
Be added to the trophies of the past,<br>
Now hanging on our ancient palace walls."</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The prince obeys his father, but for a while his search for the tiger proves fruitless.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">The prince obeyed, and to the forest went,<br>
Three days and nights he wandered in the woods,<br>
But still found not the object of his search.<br>
He missed his faithful men and lost his way,<br>
Till worn and weary underneath a tree,<br>
Whose shady boughs extended far and wide,<br>
The lonely straggler stretched his limbs and slept,<br>
And for a time forgot his dire distress.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The prince's feelings are then very graphically described in the following lines.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">He woke, and thus addressed himself with tears,<br>
"Here I am left deserted and alone;<br>
Perchance my faithful people at this hour,<br>
Are vainly searching for their hapless prince,<br>
While I die here of hunger and of thirst.</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-123"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="123" data-folio="115" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n122_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 115">[p. 115]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">And gladly would I welcome now the brute<br>
That has attracted me to this strange spot,<br>
To plunge his claws into my body, tear<br>
My flesh, and break my bones, and feast on me<br>
By gnawing them between his horrid jaws,<br>
And so spare me from this slow lingering death."</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The prince then meets a huntress, and the meeting is thus related.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">So thought the royal youth of his sad doom,<br>
When lo! a spotless figure, with a bow,<br>
A pouch with arrows dangling on her back,<br>
A hatchet in her hand for cutting wood, .<br>
And with a pitcher on her head, appeared.<br>
Here every day she came to gather wood,<br>
And, dressed in male attire, her heavy load<br>
Took to the nearest town, sold it, then reached,<br>
At close of day to cook the ev'ning meal,<br>
Her cottage on the outskirts of the wood,<br>
Where, with her sire, bent down with years, she lived,<br>
And dragged her daily miserable life.<br>
Such was the maid that was upon that day,<br>
As if by instinct, drawn to the fair youth,<br>
And such the huntress Radha he beheld.<br>
A fairer woman never breathed the air,<br>
No, not in all the land of Panchala.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">They meet, and the prince subsequently kills the tiger with the help of the huntress, who gives him food.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">The maid in pity saw his wretched plight,<br>
Then from the pitcher took her midday meal,</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-124"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="124" data-folio="116" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n123_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 116">[p. 116]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">And soon relieved his hunger and his thirst.<br>
The grateful prince, delighted, told his tale,<br>
And she, well pleased, thus spake—"Fair youth! grieve<br>
not,<br>
Behold the brook that yonder steals along,<br>
To this the tiger comes at noon to quench<br>
His thirst. Then, safely perched upon a tree,<br>
We can for ever check his deadly course."<br>
Both went, and saw at the expected hour<br>
The monarch of the forest near the brook.<br>
In quick succession, lightning-like from them<br>
The arrows flew, and in a moment fell<br>
His massive body lifeless on the ground.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The king's son then takes leave of the huntress, and returns home.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">Then vowing oft to meet his valiant friend,<br>
The prince returned, and with the happy news<br>
Appeared before the king, who blest his son<br>
And said: "My son! well hast thou done the deed;<br>
Thy life thou hast endangered for my men;<br>
Ask anything and I will give it thee."<br>
"I want not wealth nor power," the prince replied,<br>
"But, noble father! one request I make.<br>
I chanced to meet a huntress in the wood,<br>
And Radha is her name; she saved my life.<br>
I but for her had died a lingering death,<br>
Her valour and her beauty I admire,<br>
And therefore grant me leave to marry her."</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The father resents this request, and banishes the prince from the country.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-125"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="125" data-folio="117" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n124_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 117">[p. 117]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">The king spake not, but forthwith gave command<br>
To banish from his home the reckless youth,<br>
Who brought disgrace upon his royal house,<br>
And who, he wished, should wed one worthy of<br>
The noble race of ancient Panchala.<br>
Poor youth ! he left his country and his home,<br>
He that was dreaded by his foes was gone.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The neighbouring king, taking advantage of the prince's absence from the country, invades Panchala.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">Vain lust of power impelled the neighb'ring king,<br>
The traitor who usurped his sovereign's throne,<br>
To march on Panchala with all his men.<br>
He went, and to the helpless king proclaimed—<br>
“Thou knowest well my armies are the best<br>
On earth, and folly it will be in thee<br>
To stand 'gainst them and shed thy people's blood.<br>
Send forth thy greatest archer, and with him<br>
My prowess I will try ; this will decide,<br>
If you or I should sit upon the throne,<br>
And whether Panchala is thine or mine.”<br>
The king, bewildered, knew not what to do,<br>
But soon two maidens, strangers to the land,<br>
Met him, and, of the two, the younger said—<br>
“O righteous king ! we left our distant homes<br>
To visit shrines and bathe in holy streams.<br>
We have been wandering in many climes,<br>
And yesternight this place we reached, and heard<br>
Your loyal people speak of your sad plight.<br>
In early youth I learned to use the bow,<br>
I pray thee, therefore, send me forth against<br>
The wretch that dares to wrest this land from thee.”</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-126"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="126" data-folio="118" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n125_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 118">[p. 118]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The king was pleased with this offer, which he gladly accepted. He sent word to the invader, and the hour and place of the contest were named. The contending parties duly met.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">And ere the treacherous wretch could string his bow,<br>
A pointed arrow, carrying death with it,<br>
Like lightning flew from forth the maiden’s hands,<br>
Pierced deep into his head, that plans devised<br>
To kill his royal master and once more<br>
Thought ill of Panchala and her good king.<br>
His body lifeless lay upon the field.</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The king was highly pleased with the victor, and asked her to state whatever request she had to make. Thereupon, the brave woman replied as follows :—</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">“ Thou, noble ruler of this ancient land !<br>
Before thy sacred presence and before<br>
All these assembled in thy royal court,<br>
I will reveal my story, sad but true.<br>
I am the only child of him that ruled<br>
The neighbouring state, whose kings for centuries<br>
In peace and friendship lived with Panchala.<br>
Alas ! the villain, whom my arrow gave<br>
To crows and to the eagles of the air,<br>
Usurped my father’s throne, and, sad to tell,<br>
He instant orders gave to murder us.<br>
The menials sent to do the cruel deed<br>
Felt pity for the fallen king and me,</div></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-127"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="127" data-folio="119" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n126_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 119">[p. 119]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza">His only daughter, in the woods left us<br>
And went away, reporting they had done<br>
The deed ; and there, in that deserted place,<br>
Unknown we lived a wretched life for years.<br>
And glad I am that death ignoble, which<br>
The wretch deserved, has now befallen him.<br>
This person standing here—I now remove<br>
The veil, and, by the mole upon his breast,<br>
Behold in him thine own begotten son—<br>
Was by thy orders banished from the land.<br>
Grant that I now may plead for him, because<br>
A woman's words can sooner soothe the heart.<br>
I crave your Majesty to pardon him<br>
For loving me, and take him back unto<br>
His father's home ; grant also, gracious king,<br>
That I, a princess, may be worthy deemed<br>
Of being wedded to thine only son."</div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">The king, rejoiced at this, immediately issued orders for the marriage of the prince and the princess. The story goes on to tell how they in their turn ruled a double kingdom for many long years.</p></div>
