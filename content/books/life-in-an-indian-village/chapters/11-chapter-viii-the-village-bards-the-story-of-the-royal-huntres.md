<div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-119"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="119" data-folio="111" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n118_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 111">[p. 111]</button></div><h2 class="sec-h1">VIII.</h2><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p"><span class="sc">Macaulay</span> says: “The Greek Rhapsodists, according to Plato, could scarce recite Homer without falling into convulsions. The Mohawk hardly feels the scalping knife while he shouts his death song. The power which the ancient bards of Wales and Germany exercised over their auditors seems to modern readers almost miraculous.” The above remark applies with equal force to the Indian bards, who go about in villages reciting tales. The power exercised by them over the villagers is simply marvellous. I once witnessed two bards reciting a tale to the people of Kélambakam, wherein the adventures of a royal prince, his adversity, his banishment from the land of his fathers, his love for a huntress, and his ultimate marriage with her, were all graphically described. The following <button type="button" class="folio-trigger folio-turn" id="page-120" data-page="120" data-folio="112" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n119_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect original scan for page 112">[p. 112]</button> is the story. It first describes the land over which a good king ruled.</p></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">It was a land of plenty and of wealth;</span><br>
<span class="line">There God’s indulgent hand made for a race</span><br>

<span class="line">A land of virtue, truth, and charity,</span><br>

<span class="line">A land where each man deemed him highly blest</span><br>
<span class="line">When he relieved the mis’ries of the poor,</span><br>

<span class="line">Whose daily prayers went up to Him on high</span><br>
<span class="line">For wisdom and for strength to rule his men</span><br>

<span class="line">Such was the far famed king of Panchala.</span></div></div><div class="prose-unit"><p class="prose-p">This good king had a son who is next spoken of in the tale.</p></div><div class="page-rail-marker" id="page-121"><button type="button" class="folio-trigger" data-page="121" data-folio="113" data-scan="https://archive.org/download/lifeinindianvill00ramarich/page/n120_w1400.jpg" aria-label="Inspect scan for page 113">[p. 113]</button></div><div class="prose-unit"><div class="verse-stanza"><span class="line">An only son he had—a noble prince,</span><br>
<span class="line">The terror of his foes, the poor man’s friend.</span><br>

<span class="line">And was a worthy father’s worthy son.</span><br>

<span class="line">A woman’s tender pity to the weak,</span><br>

<span class="line">His arrows killed full many a beast that wrought</span><br>

<span class="line">not,</span><br>
