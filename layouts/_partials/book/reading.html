The quirks of a machine reading

Each is uncommon; these are the kinds a careful reader may meet.

  • It tidies what it should copy. An old spelling modernised; the author’s own slip quietly corrected; a romanisation as the author printed it, Bhagavad Gita, given modern IAST, Bhagavad Gītā.
  • Where the print is faint, it guesses. A word, a figure, now and then a phrase, supplied because it fits rather than because it is printed. A line may be dropped, or read twice. This has grown rare, and it is the hardest kind to catch.
  • Accents and other scripts. Diacritics dropped, or added where the author had none; one name spelt two ways in two chapters. Words in Indian scripts, in Urdu, Persian or Arabic, and quotations in Greek or Latin are all read less surely than English, and a right-to-left line can come out in the wrong order.
  • It reads one page at a time. So a paragraph that runs over a page turn may be broken in two, or two paragraphs joined; the running head and page number may slip into the text; a heading may be set at the wrong level, or a title broken; a page in two columns read across instead of down.
  • Notes, tables and pictures. A footnote parted from its mark, or joined to the wrong one; a table’s columns shifted, or a figure misread, and a wrong number in a table looks exactly like a right one; an illustration, map or plate left with no marker where it stood.
  • Small things. Punctuation missing or changed; italics lost; a line-end hyphen kept, or a hyphenated word run together; library stamps, bookplates and handwriting in the front matter read as if they were printed.
